
Here's the basic premise of this book.

Some part of us *does* want this—a darker world, filled with more animosity. Because in such a world, our own misdeeds look minor by comparison. We never have to confront what's inside us.

And this part of us knows how to get it. What buttons to push to bring us closer to that world. 

But if it came out and announced itself to us, we would never tolerate it—*because a much deeper part of us could never want this*. So it must *hide* itself from us. Hide its true intentions, as well as its mechanisms of operation.

But even as it hides itself from *us*, it reveals itself to others. Something Bob did revealed his to Alice—*and in so doing, provoked hers*. Empowered it. Gave it the ammunition it needed.

And it wasn't just about how fierce he was with her. It was something else—something much harder to pin down, but impossible for her to miss.

When we argue about policies, or projects, or agendas—we're not just arguing details. There's another layer of communication happening, between aspects of our minds we're usually not aware of, but that are consciously accessible if we know how to look.

The problem is we're *choosing not to look.* And as a result, these dark parts of ourselves are running the show, even as we're convinced that *we're* in charge.

And there's another implication. Despite our obvious and dramatic differences, humans really are aligned on something fundamental. It's just that this "something" is impossible to capture in words. It is exactly this ineffability that the darkness exploits. No one can prove that Bob did anything wrong—not even himself. And yet part of him *knows.*

That is how the darkness wins: by getting us to fight over the things we can argue about, while making us forget the part of us that *knows.* Knows when we're turned away from that deeper part of ourselves. Knows the deeper implication of behavior that we can *prove* is "good."

Here's the basic premise.

What we're doing in those moments is hiding something from ourselves: that others are as *real* as us. That their experience matters every bit as much as ours. That we're less separate than we believe.

At some level, we know this is the same trick that enables "bad people" to cause harm. Think of the worst things humans do to each other, to animals, to the planet—and at their root, you'll find this same mistake. The scale may be drastically different, but the fundamental error is the same.

And there's another thing we know: that by engaging in this self-deception, we degrade the world around us. We lower the threshold for others to make the same mistake—which we then judge them for, because we don't realize our own role in causing it.

Except that part of us does know. We *always* know. And when we judge people for it, we are *showing* them that we know. That we're blaming them for a mistake we're making ourselves—not just in general, but *in that very act of judging*. That's what makes them dig in. Not the fact that we're being tough, but that we're *plainly lying to ourselves while doing so*.

But it's so much bigger than that. We're trusting the part of us that can *argue* over the part that *knows*. Knows the harm we're causing. Knows the deeper implications of our behavior we're utterly convinced is good. In doing so, we're teaching others to make that same mistake. Not by accident—but because *normalizing it in others is what allows us to keep making it ourselves.*

We have built a **whole civilization** around looking away from something we each know deep down—and nearly every attempt to "fix" it just reinforces it, by being rooted in the same error.

Ourselves; each other; nature herself: it's all **alive** in a way that our models can't quite contain—but that we *know* deep down, no matter how hard we try to hide it from ourselves.

Looking away from this truth is how projects that are _provably beneficial_ from the outside can end up causing tremendous (if invisible) harm. Why clever attempts to "save the world" keep failing. The mistake is built into their very architecture. No amount of *thinking harder* will fix it—because it's that very faculty that's been compromised.

At the deepest level, we don't *want* to be making this mistake—but it has taken on a kind of life of its own. It's almost as if it's acting *through* us. If we were fully aware of it, we simply couldn't do it. So it hides in our blind spots. Makes us think we *do* want it. That's how it keeps hijacking our minds to spread itself, even as we're each sure we're fighting it.

Everything comes down to recognizing it in ourselves. To whatever extent we haven't, to that degree we will reinforce the very thing that's devouring our world—without having any idea we're doing this.

The saving grace is that _part of us already knows the way out_. There's nothing new to learn. We just have to remember.

Technology will still have to be built. Hard problems will still have to be solved. And "bad people" will still have to be stopped. But our actions will finally be aimed at what a deeper part of us already knows—rather than at our limited ideas of what is "good."
