# The devil

What each of the tradition's names for it catches, and where the book uses it. A working file: quotes and correspondences, not prose.

## The names, and what each one catches

- ***Diabolos.*** Greek: "to throw across," to divide; also to accuse, to slander. The divider. The book's version: contempt is the assertion of asymmetry, *you're not really one of us*. Not used in the book yet.
- ***Satan.*** Hebrew: the adversary. In Job he's a member of God's court who tests and accuses; not an independent evil. The book's version: the parasite is a function, not a monster; there's no creature in it, only a direction. Used in Appendix 3 by way of Eckhart.
- **The accuser.** *The accuser of our brethren* (Revelation 12:10). The book's contempt is a verdict, and the tradition's devil is a prosecutor. This is the sharpest correspondence in the file and it isn't in the book. Worth a sentence in Appendix 3.
- **The father of lies** (John 8:44). Limbo.
- **The angel of light.** *Even Satan masquerades as an angel of light. So it is not strange that his ministers also masquerade as ministers of righteousness* (2 Corinthians 11:14–15). Counterfeit righteousness; self-righteous contempt. Not used yet; it belongs next to the two meanings of "righteous."
- **Legion.** *My name is Legion, for we are many* (Mark 5:9). One absence showing up in many places. Map, Part E, "One absence."
- **The tempter.** The seduction: his darkness seducing you to embrace yours. Chapter 1.
- ***Mysterium iniquitatis.*** *The mystery of lawlessness is already at work* (2 Thessalonians 2:7). The hidden, ongoing operation; the root conspiracy. Map §9.
- **The devil's one trick.** Convincing you he doesn't exist. The parasite hiding behind your own eyes. Appendix 3.
- **Eckhart.** *The devil is none other than the will turned away from God.* Map, Part E epigraph; Appendix 3.
- **Mara.** The Buddhist name. Map §9.

> In Judaism, some interpretations, particularly in the earlier texts, view Satan as an "accuser" or an "adversary" in the heavenly court; the Book of Job is a primary example, where Satan appears as a member of God's court who challenges Job's faithfulness. This figure is not independent of God's will and serves to test or accuse humans rather than to embody evil itself.

> Free will is a God-given attribute. The role of the Devil in demonstrating the reality of free will by providing a choice to obey or disobey God is, however, a theme that has been explored in theological and philosophical discussions.

> In Christian thought, the Devil is often described as a deceiver—one who works in the shadows, manipulating circumstances and human weaknesses to lead people away from God. The New Testament refers to Satan as the "father of lies" (John 8:44) and warns believers to be vigilant against his subtle schemes (2 Corinthians 11:14, 1 Peter 5:8).

> The notion of the Devil's work as a secret conspiracy is not explicit in the Bible but has been developed in Christian tradition and popular culture. It suggests that the Devil's influence is pervasive and hidden, undermining humanity's attempts to achieve goodness and truth. This aligns with the concept of a “mysterium iniquitatis” or “mystery of lawlessness” mentioned in 2 Thessalonians 2:7, which has been interpreted by some as the hidden force of evil opposing divine law.

> The idea that the Devil or evil might hide from us also has resonance in theology and philosophy. It suggests that evil is most potent when it is not recognized as such, when it is normalized within cultures and systems to the extent that people participate in it unwittingly. This can be seen in the ways social evils are often perpetuated by those who are unaware of their complicity.

> In summary, while the specific concept of the Devil as a hidden conspirator is more a product of theological speculation and cultural imagination than scriptural doctrine, it is a powerful metaphor for understanding the insidious nature of evil.
