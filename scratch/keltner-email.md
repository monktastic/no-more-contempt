Subject: Contempt as a signal (and a question for you)

Hi Dacher,

You may remember me: many years ago, I invited you to give [a tech talk at Google](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tRRXRlddibg). I hope you'll forgive a note out of the blue.

I've spent the last few years writing a book about contempt. I once worked as an RA for James Gross at Stanford, so I've had a foot in your field, and I think the book's central claim is in your territory.

The claim is that contempt is a social signal whose function is to provoke, and it only works because the target reads the sender's self-deception. On some level, the sender is counting on that. If that's right, it may help explain why contempt does so much damage, in marriages (Gottman's finding) and in politics.

The book starts from something Michael Pollan said in your 2019 conversation at Berkeley: that on LSD he felt "love is the most important thing in the universe." I've tried to work out what that would mean if it were literally true (because I've experienced it myself), including toward people we call "evil."

Here's the short version.

---

When I've been hurt, I feel entitled to lash out—at the world, and especially at whoever hurt me. It's my right, and I resent anyone who suggests otherwise. Usually I do it with contempt, which feels so damn righteous. What I don't see is that it's **evil**. I don't mean that as a moral lecture. I mean it's the same thing we recognize as evil in others: it makes the world worse for everyone, and part of me knows that and is fine with it anyway, as long as I "get mine."

When I treat someone with contempt, part of me knows I'm being cruel, and I show him that I know. I can do it with a look, even while telling him, and myself, that I'm just holding him to account. He can see I'm counting on him to get it. I know he sees that, and I hide that from myself too. If he calls me on it, I can take his protest as proof that I was right about him all along. What he takes from all this is a license: *if even the "good people" lie to themselves like this, why shouldn't I?*

In this way, contempt is a recursive trap. It relies on a state of mind where I both know something and don't. My enemies are in the same state, and that, too, I both know and don't. This is what the book spells out.

Why can't I see this while I'm doing it? Because something is warping my view, and it's a trick: I would never do this if I could see clearly. That warping is our real enemy. It's what evil *is*. It bends what I perceive and believe so that I can't see what I'm doing.

The license I give him can make it easier for him to justify hurting someone who had nothing to do with us. It spreads among allies, too. When I mock a shared enemy with a friend, we both leave a little more practiced, sure we were only bonding.

Until I recognize the warping in myself, I can't understand what's going on in the minds of my enemies. Once I do, I can more easily see what would free them of that confusion, instead of shoving them deeper into it out of a misguided sense of justice. That's called compassion, and it has nothing to do with being nice. Mistaking it for niceness is what keeps the cycle going, because true compassion is the only thing that works against it.
