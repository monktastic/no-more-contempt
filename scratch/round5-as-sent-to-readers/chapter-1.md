# Chapter 1: Turning Away

Imagine looking into a stranger's eyes long enough that their face stops being something you're looking at and becomes a place somebody is looking out of.

Stay there until you feel what comes with it—not a conclusion, but a recognition:

> _This one is like me. I want them to be all right._

Call this being _turned toward_ them. With it comes a sense of their innocence. Not that they're incapable of causing harm—but the sense of something innocent *underneath* it.

Now erase that meeting. Run it again—but this time, they've just done something atrocious. Something deliberate, to someone innocent—maybe even to someone you love—and they are unrepentant.

Keep looking into the same eyes. Can you still find that same recognition?

It's harder, for me by a lot. If it isn't for you, make the case real: someone from your own life, or from the news, whose harm you can't wave off.

For the rest of us: notice that instead of seeing someone whose wellbeing matters, now there is only something rotten. It's not merely something they did. It appears to be what they **are**.

Try and feel what you're showing them through your gaze. Is it just anger? Just *I will stop you?* Or is there something darker in it? Something that says:

> *This is all you are.*

Something in you wants this verdict to *land*; to register deep inside. Can you feel that? When we think this way about someone, it's very hard not to deliver it.

This overlooking of their humanity is what I'll call being _turned away_: you cannot see yourself in them. The everyday word for it is *othering*. And the need to make the verdict land is _contempt_: turning-away made into a message, with the sense that sending it is righteous.

Now, how do they react? Does it make remorse easier—or do they double down, feeling even more justified in their behavior?

Usually the latter.

Couldn't we have predicted this would happen? So why do we do it anyway? Is it just because we believe it will still be a net positive—or might something else be going on in there?

---

I'll describe what it's like when I'm in your shoes, and you can check if you recognize yourself in any of it. If it sounds like I'm contradicting myself in places, it's because two parts of my mind are in tension.

Part of me senses something *dark* about what I'm conveying. The message *this is all you are* carries a subtle but primal threat: *... and therefore your wellbeing has ceased to matter. What I do to you now no longer qualifies as harm.* Check if you sense how contempt carries that.

Who *wouldn't* violently defy a message like that? I certainly would.

And that's deeply uncomfortable, because I'm telling myself the opposite story: that their reaction proves we're nothing alike; that they're totally beyond redemption.

So I refuse to believe that's what I'm doing. I'm not *threatening* them. I'm just hinting at what will happen if they don't stop. When you look at it that way, it's really more like an *invitation*—for them to give up their darkness and come back to the realm of those who matter.

But there is **nothing** inviting about my tone or gaze. Nothing that signals they'd be welcome back. If there were, why would I be trying to convince them that *this is all they are*?

Their reaction seems to indicate they recognize this. Something in their eyes is saying:

> *Don't pretend you're doing something noble here. We both know what you're actually doing.*

And truth be told, something deep inside knows they're right. But I absolutely cannot afford to admit it, least of all here. I don't even want to believe I have that kind of darkness in me—the kind that would forsake a fellow being just to prove I'm right about them.

The good news is, maybe I don't, because here's the thing: fighting evil genuinely requires being fierce. Isn't that all I'm doing? Surely that's all I'm doing.

So I proceed, with newfound resolve that it's coming from a good place. At least, good enough. Look, I know I'm not a saint, but being a little sharp with a *bad person* is a pretty silly thing to quibble about. *I'm* the good guy here, and that's what matters!

And yet they just won't seem to let it go. Of *course* they wouldn't. It's exactly what you'd expect from a rotten person. And I'm not entirely sure I *did* communicate anything dark. So how are *they* so sure? There's only one answer: they are projecting. Classic.

Any doubt I may have had is now erased. *This really **is** what you are—and I'm going to enjoy making sure everyone sees it.*

Because you can't let evil just win like that. It's gone on for far too long in this world, and it ends now.

---

This is the "hot" form of contempt. It can also run cold. I just write the person off: a quiet filing-away, a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them. It's the same verdict, *this is all you are*, delivered by withdrawal, and they feel it just the same. If the hot version didn't sound like you, check this one. And if neither does—maybe because your enemies are rarely in the room with you—check if the people who *are* might sense something "dark" about your behavior.

If not, I have no argument to offer here. Read on, and see whether anything else fits.

---

I'll just go ahead and name what's happening.

I'm seeing genuine darkness in them. That part is accurate. And I know the harm it can cause in the world. Not for some abstract reason, but because *it lives in me, too.*

And as long as I have it, I might as well use it for a good purpose, like I am now. I don't experience it as "dark" at all—because when you use it against bad people, it's the *good* kind.

**And that's exactly the lie**. There is no "good kind" of darkness. That's not something I can convince you of. All you have to do is watch the results.

My target recognizes the profound hypocrisy of what I'm doing. How? Because it's the same trick he used to enable whatever bad thing *he* did. The darkness never feels "wrong" when *we* do it. Even the worst person experiences theirs as *right*. It's only in *other people* that it's bad.

Check if some part of you recognizes this move—if not in yourself, then at least as one that plagues humanity.

So when he implicitly accuses me of knowing what I'm doing, I don't really have a good defense. All I can do is double down. The problem is, he recognizes that move, too. Humans are *very* good at recognizing subtle "tells" in each other, and he can read my self-deception like an open book.

Now for the strange part. Normally, having my enemy read my self-deception, my hypocrisy, would be a horrible thing. But notice what happens here: the more he can see that I'm lying to myself, the more maddening it becomes for him, which makes him more defiant, which I can increasingly use as evidence against him.

In other words, this is the (possibly unique) case where revealing my self-deception actually benefits me. And it's more than just a benefit. I am *relying* on his reaction, because it is what exonerates me in my own mind. That's no small need. Realizing that I've been keeping my enemies trapped in the dark—and that part of me knew it, even as I told myself I didn't—would be **devastating**.

So there must be no way to objectively prove I'm doing any of this. Not just in the public record (though that matters too), but in my own mind. I *cannot* know I had any role in causing it. *Must* not. If I knew I'd planted the evidence, not only would it not exonerate me; it would implicate me.

And that is why we so rarely recognize that this is what our contempt has been doing.

---

So: for this to do what I need, I must be revealing to him that I am deceiving myself.

About what? A whole stack of things. Near the top is the fact that I'm keeping him trapped while pretending I'm inviting him out, to prevent myself from realizing that I'm keeping him trapped.

I wouldn't be working this hard at keeping him trapped unless some part of me suspected he could find his way out, with the proper help. Maybe not in practice, but at least in principle. It's just that if he found his way out, I would have to face myself—and I'm not ready to do that.

And at the bottom of the stack is the simple fact that I'm expressing something dark. Of course, part of what makes it "dark" is the entire trap—but it all grew out of a single kernel: my claim that *this is all you are.*

You watched the stack get built. At the bottom: *this is all you are*, with part of me knowing it's dark. So I call it an invitation. He sees through that, so I call it being fierce. He still won't let it go, so I call it projection. Each layer is something I both know and don't. Each is darker than the one beneath it, so I bury it deeper, which he feels more sharply, which is what forces the next one.

There's something curious embedded in that bottom layer, which we'll come back to. But for now, a burning question. Remember, I *need him* to see this. How do I show him something I don't (and *cannot*) know about myself, and moreover, *that* I don't know it?

---
## Limbo

Have you ever done something you knew was wrong at the time, but couldn't fully admit it to yourself until later? Maybe you took credit for someone else's work, said something cruel to someone who'd done nothing to you, or cheated on a test (or even a partner).

Recall what it was like to finally own up to it. The knowledge that it was wrong: did it feel like new information? Or like something you had always known, but somehow hadn't *let* yourself know?

We like to call such knowledge "unconscious"—as if we had no way of knowing—but that's not entirely accurate. It was *right there* to see if you had looked; fully available to consciousness. You were just *turned away* from it. The knowledge existed in a strange kind of **limbo**, in which you simultaneously knew and yet somehow didn't.

And it wasn't just sitting there idle, either: it affected your behavior. Any time someone came close to exposing it, or you started getting close to it yourself, you found a way to deflect. Did that happen passively?

Looking back, can you see how *some part of you knew exactly what you were doing, even as you did it?* The work of hiding it took place in limbo, too.

---

Now try to recall how you knew it was wrong in the first place.

Whatever it was, did it arrive before or after you'd reasoned through it? The part of you that knew: it couldn't say exactly how it knew. Meanwhile, another part of you was busy providing reasons. *Everyone does it. It's not a big deal. Just this once.*

Why did one side have to argue, and the other not? And which side did you listen to—the part that "just knew" or the part that could prove?

Also notice that limbo exists in degrees. It's not just black or white. The worse the behavior, the harder we must hide it from ourselves.

Can you harm or use someone while staring your conscience straight in the face, with no justification at hand? Try it in imagination, with something small: picture the person in front of you, fully in view, and see whether it will go.

Finally: when you were in limbo, did some part of you know you were, even as you hid it? If you look carefully, you'll find that limbo contains knowledge of itself, in limbo.

---

Before we move on, let's give ourselves a little grace.

When you finally admitted it to yourself, how did you hold yourself? The most common contempt of all is the one we aim at ourselves: *you are what you did.* It feels like accountability. It isn't. It's the same verdict, *this is all you are*, pointed inward, and it does what the verdict always does: it makes the part of you that did wrong dig in and hide. Nobody ever looked honestly at something in themselves while being told they were worthless for having it. 

So if, in what follows, you catch yourself thinking *I'm a terrible person*, treat that as one more instance of the thing we're studying. It's no truer of you than of your enemy, and no more useful. What you need in order to look is what your enemy needed from you: to be seen as more than what you did.

---

Okay, back to the scenario.

The answer to the question *how did I both know and not-know what I was doing* is, of course, limbo. But why was I in limbo in the first place? Because some part of me sensed that delivering the verdict *this is all you are* isn't actually helpful.

But I also asked a deeper question: *how did I show him?* I was showing him two things. That it was dark, and that I was hiding that from myself.

Take the first. How was I showing him that *this is all you are*? I didn't have to say it in words (and in fact, probably didn't). I was delivering that verdict through my tone, my gaze, my manner. And here's why it's not so hard to do: turning away isn't one form of darkness among many. It is the very *root*.

All harm begins by *turning away* from others. I can't prove this to you, but I invite you to check it yourself. Even the worst person: can they do it while being *turned toward* their victim in the way you were turned toward the stranger in the opening? Can they do it with kindness in their eyes? 

Here's another check. Think of what it's like to be on the receiving end of a dehumanizing gaze. Doesn't the person feel somehow absent or "turned away" from themselves? Why do they have to turn away, and what are they turning away *from*? Maybe there's no part of them that cares, but maybe they just no longer have access to it. Either way, the state looks essentially the same.

What you're seeing in them is limbo, aimed at *you*.

Here's the connection. Turning away from ourselves; from our conscience, our better nature; from our profound commonality with others—it's **all a single move**. And it includes turning away from the full implications of what our behavior creates in the world. It is what allows us to harm one another without compunction, and to benefit ourselves at the world's expense.

When it's directed at us, it is something we each recognize instinctively. It's common knowledge, in the everyday sense. Everybody knows what that look is: a person turned away from their own better nature. That's what darkness means, in anyone, and everybody knows it. (I think it's common knowledge in a stricter sense too, and that this is a hint about the nature of awareness itself. I say more in Chapter 3 and Appendix 1.) So when I turned away from my target—simply by perceiving them as something *other*—that look was already going out through my eyes, whether I wanted to admit it or not. That's why the defense "how are they so sure when even I don't know?" was disingenuous. I *did* know. I was just holding it in limbo.

That's how I showed him it was dark, and that already implies a kind of hiding. If the hiding stops there, we call it *malice.* But I was hiding one more thing: the fact that it *was* malicious. I was telling myself a story of how righteous it was—and if I had been completely honest with myself in the moment, it would have been obvious that this deception leaks, too.

We call that level *contempt.* Nobody, underneath, believes their contempt is righteous. In one sense, we might. But at some level there's always the knowledge of what it is. We try to bury it, but that gets read too. You can trace it for yourself: it is effective precisely because we know it's not righteous, and we rely on our target to know that we know this (so that it comes across as a message and not a slip), which means *we know they know we know*.

You don't have to trace the logic. You might have felt it before, as a kind of hall of mirrors. A message *beneath* the message, as if delivered via a subliminal channel.

---

Earlier I said we'd come back to something curious about that bottom layer of stuff I knew and didn't know. And remember, the bottom layer was about the darkness itself; the *this is all you are*.

We just checked that darkness is essentially a kind of turning away (from myself; my conscience; your humanity). And turning away entails *knowing and not-knowing*: that was limbo.

There is nothing *to* turning away except knowing and not-knowing. We have been using several metaphors to talk about the same basic thing: darkness, limbo, turning away, knowing and not-knowing.

If that's not intuitive, take a case. Suppose the bad deed you hid from yourself was cheating. How is that "dark"? It required you to make yourself an exception. That's something you couldn't have done while looking your "victims" in the eye. Even if you could somehow hold eye contact with them, you would have had to do it while seeing them as something *less than* you. Does that match your own case?

So: the bottom-most thing I was hiding from myself was nothing but *the fact that I was hiding*. The mistake I made was to not immediately catch it and stop. The whole stack got built by my refusal to acknowledge something in myself: that the thing I'm viciously accusing him of is the very thing I'm doing to him now.

And that's what he recognized, which is what makes him feel more justified refusing that knowledge in himself.

---

This is what I mean by there is no "good kind" of darkness. Not that it can't stop people. My behavior might make him think twice about doing it again—at least, if there's any chance of him getting caught. But does he feel remorseful? Is his private behavior likely to get better? I don't think so. If anything, he's likely to take his newfound rage out in hidden ways.

The darkness leaks out of my skull, empowering his, because its meaning is universal. My justifications, on the other hand, are believable only to me (and my allies). He obviously doesn't share them (nor do *his* allies, if he has any). And I *know* he doesn't. Any belief that there's a "good kind" is short-sighted: it ignores the long-term implications of our behavior.

If the above section was hard to admit, this one will be even harder. So again, I'll limit it to a personal recognition, and you can decide for yourself if it clicks.

I do not actually *believe* there's a "good kind" of darkness. I do not weigh the short-term benefit against the long-term harm and decide the tradeoff is worth it. Everything I can accomplish *with* the darkness can be accomplished better without it. I can be fierce and uncompromising with my enemies *without* provoking that "digging in" response that makes them embrace their darkness.

What actually happens is this: I use the short-term (and / or visible) effects to "prove" to myself that what I did was good, while part of me secretly knows that the darkness will still escape into the world, one way or another. This person *will* pass it along, likely to those who do not deserve it (not that anyone could "deserve" to have their mind darkened).

If I know that, why do I still do it? That's the wrong question.

My darkness cannot survive my looking directly at it, by definition. Which means that to survive, it must keep itself hidden from me. And what better way to prevent me from looking at my own faults than to have stuff to point at "out there" and angrily shake my fist at?

I want to be crystal clear here. I said "secretly knows," and I mean it. I am not a cartoon villain, and I don't think you are, either. What I'm telling you is that, in my absolutely deepest moments of introspection, that's what I've found: that as long as darkness remains in me, I cannot help but behave in ways that reproduce the problem out there, so that I do not have to look. And while I'm doing this, I always feel that it's righteous.

Which makes me wonder: might this be what my enemies are doing, too?

If so, there's probably a better way to deal with it than to keep pushing them deeper into that mistake. I might even have an inkling of what that way is. But as long as this problem continues to exist in me, I'm liable to keep doing this.
