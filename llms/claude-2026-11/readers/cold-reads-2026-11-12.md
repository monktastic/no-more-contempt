# ruth-1-x

Dear friend,

I've read the four chapters you sent. I read them slowly and did every exercise, with Glen as the face whenever an enemy was called for. You know who Glen is. I'll answer the questions in order. I'm going to tell you what happened, and some of it isn't flattering to me.

## 1. What happened in me

Three things got through, and I expect to still have them in a month.

The first is in Chapter 1: "It's confined to my skull, so what harm could it possibly cause? And that lie is exactly what lets it leak out my skull, through my tone and gaze." For four years I've been perfectly civil to Glen. I've never said a word out of place. My son stopped bringing the children at the holidays because "you two make the air hurt." I always told myself the air was Glen's doing. This sentence put it in my own mouth.

The second is the section called "The Quiet Kind," and one line in particular: "a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them... meant to be felt just the same: by them, or by whoever's watching. Everyone who witnesses it is being taught the same lesson." Whoever's watching. That's my son and my grandchildren. I've been teaching a lesson at Thanksgiving with a gravy boat. I sat in the chair for a long time after that page.

The third is "Love and contempt are addressed to the same place. One says *I know you're in there,* the other says *there's nobody in there*, and they both say it to the somebody who is." Glen told the family I'd nursed Tom into the grave for the house. It didn't hit my pride. Nurses get called worse at four in the morning. It hit the place that sat with Tom for the last eleven nights. Chapter 4 finishes the thought: "The message that denies the sameness is made of the sameness, and nothing else could receive it." Glen's words could only wound me because he and I both know what those nights were. That idea is the best thing in the book. I had to read the paragraph twice, and it was worth it.

Two of the exercises worked on me. In the two buttons in Chapter 1, I found I wouldn't press the pill that changes his heart. Part of the reason is that I distrust pills. The other part is that a changed Glen would take away my reason to be cold. I didn't expect that, and I don't like it. In Chapter 3, "the worst thing they can do to us is... to *get better*" landed on the same bruise from the other side. The test in Chapter 3 was the most useful tool in the book: keep every fact, give up only the permission to feel contempt, and see whether you still want your side. I hesitated a long few seconds. "The hesitation is the finding." It was.

There's also the grudge: "A grudge has to be fed: the reasons rehearsed, the case kept open... It feels like setting something down." The court case is closed, and I keep the lawyer letters in a manila folder in my desk. I could feel the weight of it in my arms as I read.

## 2. Where I was handled

It wasn't constant, but it was there.

- "Really try and visualize it." I already was.
- "It would be difficult to actually do, wouldn't it? Why?" The answer is built in.
- "What does that tell you? It tells me there's something we serve that transcends sides." He asks, then answers for me.
- "What does that sound like to you? It is a parasite, and we are its hosts." He does it again. To me it sounded like a habit, or in older language a sin.
- "Does that remind you of anything? We already hinted at it earlier: *plausible deniability.*" Quiz-show.
- "That's why we don't look so deeply." This tells me my own reason.
- "doesn't it feel like you literally *see* a 'bad person?'"
- "hold off on that judgement. It turns out to be one more instance of the thing we're studying." This tells me what I'll find before I've found it.
- "heart-rending poem." Let me decide.

To be fair, plenty of his questions leave room, and those were the ones that worked. "Was it hard because you knew less about it, or because there was more you couldn't bear to face?" is a real question. So is the "snapped out of being mean" test.

## 3. Where I skimmed

- Chapter 2, from the oil executive through the company paragraph and the long slide into cynicism. It's the same descent told twice, once in "*Every* project and agenda has this potential" and again in "Further along... Further still." The company paragraph ("imbue it with contempt... countless invisible channels") reads like a talk.
- Chapter 3, from "Contempt is the #1 predictor of divorce" through armageddon and the global conspiracy.
- Chapter 4, the run of quotations once it passed Merton. By Brad Warner I was reading a list. Warner's "sooooo" and "morons," set next to Julian of Norwich, jarred me.

## 4. Confusing, out of order, repeated

- **The cold kind comes too late.** Chapter 1 spends its whole middle on a hot man saying "he's **bad**, dammit." I have never said dammit about Glen in my life. Around line 88 I nearly filed this as a book for shouters. Then "The Quiet Kind" arrived at the end and it was about me. A reader like me needs the cold version beside the hot one, or before it.
- "Turned away" and "limbo" do too many jobs. Limbo starts as knowing-and-not-knowing a wrong. By Chapter 4 it covers the brain building space and time. And on top of limbo there are "joint limbo," "shadow space," "secret handshake," "shared field" and "parasite." That's a lot of names for close cousins.
- In Chapter 3 he says plainly that it isn't a literal entity, which I was glad of. Ten pages later "something far more insidious is tricking us into hating them to take attention off of *it*," and the hedge isn't repeated. The parasite starts to have plans.
- Bookkeeping: "Recall from Chapter 1 that I said...," "In Chapter 2 we said...," "(we'll see what that's like later)," "(See Appendix 1 for details.)" The last one pulled me out of a scene.
- "And now recall that a milder version of this is actually my *default* state." I had to hunt for where he'd said it.
- "Let's return to the perspective of the parasite itself," and then we go to the manager and the friend instead.
- "Only by doing that can I recognize the darker aspect of my mind for what it is: a lie." I didn't follow why darkness is a lie at that point. Chapter 4 explains it somewhat, too late.
- Small things: "touchy-feeling," "only verifies," hyperlinks in a book (Gottman, a "cosmic joke" site, Snopes for the Dalai Lama).

## 5. The inner monologue

The hot monologue is believable for a hot man. "*Shit.* He's onto me." "*Ugh, I knew it. People are even worse than I thought.*" That flicker of relief before the relabeling is true. When Glen's second lawyer letter came, I felt something close to satisfaction. On the phone to my sister I called it sorrow.

It isn't my monologue, though. Mine sounds like "Would you like more coffee, Glen." The book gives the cold form a paragraph of description but never a voice. If he wrote one in the same way, from the inside, I think it would be the most frightening page in the book for people like me.

## 6. From my seat

What's said better than I usually hear it:

- "Staying turned toward someone never means staying near them... It is possible to leave, cut contact, and never see them again with no verdict in it at all." I didn't know I needed that sentence. I've thought my only choices with Glen were reconciliation, which I won't do, or a scene, which I won't make. This is a third door. I could stay away from the holiday with nothing in it, or go and stop performing.
- The note to people who were hurt. "your rage is right... The verdict is the one thing you don't owe them." It's the most careful paragraph in the book.
- "*Goodness means serving life. This man isn't serving it. Therefore, hating him is good.*" I've watched Friends do exactly that in a business meeting over a roof.
- "there *is* an orientation there that I can recognize and am bound by, I can't pin it down in a definition, and that still doesn't make it mine to define." That's very nearly how a Friend talks about the Light. Bringing "the light" in lowercase, defined by a person who stood for something that wasn't his, was the right way to do it.
- The locker room. The way "the room moves at once." I've been the one in the break room who said "she's actually frightened," and I felt the room move. His "shared field" is something I know from the other side. A gathered meeting is a shared field too, the good kind, and I've felt it for thirty years. I'd never thought that its opposite was just as real, though I've felt it gather at a family table. He might mention the good field. It would make the bad one more believable, not less.
- The corpse. I've done post-mortem care hundreds of times. We spoke to them, and nobody taught us to. He's right.

Overclaimed or thin:

- "I now believe that this somehow always finds its way into my outer world... my outer world is constantly reflecting back that inner corruption." I've sat with too many good people dying badly. If this means the world mirrors your soul, it's a cruelty, and I've heard chaplains say it at bedsides. He hedges, but "always" is in there. Chapter 4 repeats it as fact: "the world I project reflects it back to me."
- The global conspiracy section: "We're sensing something real." I know who would underline that sentence, and I'd rather they didn't have it.
- Then psychedelics, "irrefutably and self-evidently," "a million times stronger," "timelessly," and "what remained was Light. Love. And the recognition was that this was what I was." I shut down there. It isn't that it couldn't be so. It's said as a finding, and I've no way to meet him there. I've met that certainty at retreats, and it's usually unkind by the following Thursday.

But I want to be fair to him on Chapter 4, because he was fair to me. "From here I'm going further than you can check from where you sit... hold it the way you'd hold a traveler's report. Nothing you've found so far rests on it." That's how a Friend speaks in meeting when she knows she's reaching. And the middle of the mushroom account I believe completely: "I was held in love the whole time. The wrongs weren't made smaller. But seeing them didn't require me to become worthless." That isn't a man boasting that he saw God. It's a confession. It's what my year of grief therapy was, slowly and without mushrooms. Then "The smaller opening I can offer you is the one we've been using: a wrong finally admitted, and what you came back to." That's a door I can actually walk through. It kept me in the book.

"Reports... are remarkably similar, across traditions that never met" is stated too flatly. That claim is argued over. "They call it *ignorance* because we are literally ignoring it" is a pun dressed as etymology.

## 7. Assumptions and words I don't have

- "NPCs." I had to guess. It's my grandson's word.
- "the dumping ground of our collective shadow." Jung, unexplained.
- "veridical," "cognitive-perceptual system," "*absolute* goodness... relatively." Lecture-hall words.
- "darkness" arrives in Chapter 1 before it's set up. It's fine once he gives it a definition in Chapter 2.
- It assumes my wrong made me feel aggrieved: "Did some part of you feel you *deserved* something?" Mine didn't. I was tired, the floor was short-staffed, and I charted a heparin as on time when it was fifty minutes late. "Exception" fit in the end. "Deserved" didn't.
- "why should all of ours be pointed in the same direction?" assumes they are. I've seen families pull in opposite directions with equal conscience.

Did it ever sound like being asked to go easy on Glen? No, and that matters. "Not more gently." "Nor is it your duty to be *nice* to me." "And not that we're equally culpable: we're not." If he'd said Glen and I were equally culpable, I'd have closed the book. "*The harm stops now. You will be held accountable.*" and "fierce kindness" are the right words. I came away with no softness asked of me, only the verdict. That's a much harder thing to put down.

## 8. What I already knew

Plenty of it. Evil as an absence is Augustine. "That of God in every one" is Fox. Dr. Okafor, who told me in 1991 "that was sloppy and you're not sloppy," was the second stranger, and I'd have walked through a wall for him. I've stopped a resident joking about an overdose patient's mother with "Not on my unit. You're better than that." I knew all of it as a nurse.

What became newly consequential is that I'd never once connected any of it to Glen. I gave other people the second stranger's look for thirty years and gave Glen the first stranger's look in a cardigan. Also, "or my target will just take it out on his wife and kids." Glen has a wife, Donna. I haven't thought of her once in four years.

What I'll do this week: take the folder out of the desk. I haven't decided what to do with it. And I'll call my son and say the air was partly mine, without making him take a side.

**What's said better than I usually hear it.** The two strangers. "**Love and contempt are addressed to the same place.** One says *I know you're in there,* the other says *there's nobody in there*, and they both say it to the somebody who is." That's the best sentence in the book, and it doesn't need the bold. "Nor is it your duty to be *nice* to me if I do it again" is something IFS and my whole yoga world are too soft to say. "The way you hold your own darkness is the way you'll end up holding theirs, and you hold your own first." Then the protector with good intent, said without letting it off: "In a sense, my state had been utterly innocent. Not as in blameless." Schwartz says this. The author says it better, because he doesn't let the innocence excuse anything.

**What's thin or overclaimed.**
- *The parasite.* This needs telling straight. My brother-in-law said I had "a spirit you let in." Then Chapter 3 told me there's something that gets in, uses me as a vehicle and feels like me but isn't. Structurally it's the same sentence, and I went cold. IFS says there are no bad parts. In ceremony circles I've watched "entity" talk turn into "your ex has a dark attachment" in a week. Two things saved it for me. One was "I'm not claiming there's a literal entity... Calling it a parasite, something in me that isn't me, is what makes looking possible." That's unblending, and I can work with it. The other came later: "We can even give it a name: Satan. Then... anyone who doesn't abide by the 'right' rules appears to be a minion." That's Brent, described by a book that doesn't know him. So the book survives the collision. But a reader like me needs that Satan paragraph much closer to the word "parasite." I spent sixteen pages braced.
- *Evil as absence.* "an absence that *masquerades* as a presence" is Augustine. Jung fought it all his life, because he thought privatio boni lets us off the shadow. The author's whole book is about not being let off, so he's on Jung's side in spirit and Augustine's in doctrine. He borrows "collective shadow" too. I wanted him to know that tension is there.
- *Self-evidence as evidence.* This is the one I care about most. In Chapter 4 the reason given for trusting the mystical report is that it comes with "something I've always known, irrefutably and self-evidently." I've had that feeling on the medicine. It's real. A woman in my circle had it too, with total certainty, about a childhood abuse that turned out never to have happened. She cut her sister off over it. Self-evidence is the least reliable thing the medicine gives you. He says "I cannot prove it." Good. But then he leans on the one feeling I've learned to distrust most.
- *"my outer world is constantly reflecting back that inner corruption"* and *"how my beliefs literally shape my reality."* That's the manifesting-podcast register, and it has a cruel edge. Is my nieces' house a reflection of my inner corruption? He doesn't mean that, but the sentences let it in.
- *"a single thing called *Life*"* and *"what remained was Light. Love."* I've felt both. On the page, without particulars, they read like a retreat brochure. At its most important moment the mushroom passage goes general: "layers of my capacity for self-deception." Show me one wrong he saw. One room.

## 7. Assumptions, words I don't have, going easy

The word I don't have is "orient." "We both know how to *orient* toward goodness." I don't know what that is in my body. "Turned toward" I have. "Orient" is a seminar word.

The assumption is "why should all of ours be pointed in the same direction?" Brent's better nature points at a God who thinks I'm possessed. The book does answer this, partly. The syllogism, "*Goodness means serving life. This man isn't serving it. Therefore, hating him is good*," is both Brent's move and mine. But it asserts the shared direction a beat before it earns it.

Did it ask me to go easy on someone who hurt me? No, and I was braced for it the whole time. "We're not equally culpable: we're not." "*The harm stops now. You will be held accountable.*" "Your rage is right." It never asked me to be soft with Brent. What it asked was harder: to check whether the verdict was doing something for me. It was.

## 8. Already knew / newly consequential

I knew most of the parts. Contempt is Gottman's number one predictor. The shadow is Jung. Protectors have good intent. "The eye through which I see God" I knew from Jung, and the Thich Nhat Hanh poem from Tara Brach's talks. "Integrity is what we do when no one is watching" is a poster. Then he adds one sentence that makes the poster new: "Not the part busy with reasons, but the part I'd turned away from so I wouldn't feel it watching."

What became consequential is this. I've always known the cold was a verdict. I didn't know it was addressed to my sister, or that the girls were the audience. "Everyone who witnesses it is being taught the same lesson." Then: "Somewhere a stranger is about to give up on the part of them that knows, and what holds them is made of what the rest of us have done, in rooms they'll never see." My oldest niece is eleven. If she turns out anything like me, she'll hear Brent say something one day about someone like her, maybe about her. And the one adult who'd say *I know you're in there* won't be in the house, partly to keep her verdict tidy.

This week I'm going to call my sister, not text. I'm also going to stop doing the impression. I don't know yet whether I can keep the second.

## 9. Scores

- Recognition: 9
- Profundity: 7 (8 for Chapters 1 and 3; Chapter 4 borrows more than it finds)
- Likely to change me: 8
- Clarity: 6
- Pull: 7 (Chapter 1 and the first half of 3 pull hard; Chapter 2's middle sags)
- Freshness: 6 (the two strangers, the open secret and "made of the sameness" are fresh; the mystics anthology and the evil-as-absence frame are not)

As a novelist, on the prose: it's slack in the abstract-chain sentences ("that acts as an invitation for others to do the same, which helps those very implications come to pass"), in the nominalizations ("a striking recognition begins to occur"), in "literally," which appears at least six times, never literally, and in the endings. "In a sense, it is the whole point" is a shrug of a last line. The book is best when a man remembers a specific room: the teacher "searching my face for some sign that I knew it too," the locker room, "*Into the wood chipper, feet first!*" When it writes about rooms it's a writer. When it writes about "the whole" it sounds like a committee of teachers I've already heard.

Thank you for giving it to me. I mean that, and it cost me something.

# ines-2-y

# Letter 2 — Book Y (all ten files)

Dear friend,

I've now read the older, longer version. The first two chapters are nearly the same as X's, and I read them faster. After that it's a different book: more practical, more careful about the dangerous things, and more willing to tell me what to do on a Tuesday. I came out of it with a phone call to make and a sentence to say on it. That hadn't happened with X.

## 1. What happened in me

X made me see that my cold was aimed at my sister and watched by my nieces. Y went under that and found the reason.

It started with a small check in Chapter 3: "Think of someone who can't do a thing through no fault of theirs: a toddler who can't add, a grandparent losing their words. Is the contempt there?" There was none for my abuela last year, only grief. Then the next line: "It arrives once I've decided they *should* know better; that they could, and won't... I've found them guilty of being what they are." That's my sister. Not Brent. I've never thought Brent could know better. My sister, I've decided, should: should leave, should have said something the night he said it, could and won't. That verdict has been underneath all of this, and X never got me to it.

Then Chapter 5: "you can only be good *relative* to someone worse. So as long as you don't feel at home, you can't afford to let anyone else get there." If my sister were simply a good woman in a hard marriage doing her best, I'd lose the contrast that makes me the good sister.

Then Chapter 7 gave me the daily check: "if the harm simply stopped tomorrow, with nobody punished and nobody exposed, would you feel relief, or robbed?" Robbed. Then: "something in you would be disappointed if they got better." That's what I found at the two buttons in X, when I hesitated over the pill. X gave me the exercise. Y told me what my hesitation was.

Then Y told me what to do, which X never did: "When you catch it late, there's usually someone to go back to, and often the repair is no more than letting them see that you see them now." That's the call to my sister. It isn't an apology speech. It's letting her see that I see her.

Other sentences that did real work:
- "*I'm really worried about Lisa,* and now two people can dig in, both of them only concerned." That's me and my friend Dana, about my sister, for hours. A shadow space with a halo on it.
- "*You lied to me.* With the extra: *Of course you lied to me.*" With it: "*I don't think you're capable of understanding what that did to her.*" I've said that last sentence to my sister, about Brent. "Each time the difference is one word or one pause, and each time you know, on the way out, which one you sent." I knew.
- "*Oh, boo hoo, he was abused as a child... I was hurt too, and I didn't turn evil.*" I've said this about Brent to my therapist. "Look for where you're taking credit: for the people who held you, the help you had, the day it could have gone the other way." That's my abuela, a girlfriend's mother's couch, and a therapist I could afford.
- "a hostage held up to the window, not a monster on display." Brent at Thanksgiving, looking frightened, and me refusing to let it count.
- The exercise for the far end. I did it lightly with Brent, and what stopped me was my eleven-year-old niece's face. That's what does the stopping, for me.

## 2. Where I was handled, or the answer was built in

Less than in X, and the handling is gentler. The tag questions from X's first chapters are all still here ("wouldn't it?", "Can you feel that?"). New ones:
- "When I see people lying, cheating, and stealing, what am I likely to feel for them? *Contempt.*"
- "If you look deeply enough into the desire to make the *bad people* suffer, it's what you'll find, too." X changed this to "it's what I find there, too." X was right.
- "the clean moments... were the ones that felt most like yourself." Then an escape hatch, "If it did feel like you, ask it another way," which is a funnel.
- "I think anyone who watches their own contempt closely for long enough will find it too." That's in "The Picture I Use," the one section that admits it has no check.
- "In my experience it doesn't survive that." He saves it at once: "the first several times you may find only a tight chest and the story." And elsewhere: "The first several times I found nothing. Then one day I caught it." That's the right way to promise something.

Y also does the opposite of handling better than X does. "If a different picture works better for you, use yours." "If it doesn't, take what follows as my report." "If you look at such a person and find only a monster, I can't argue you out of that from here." Those leave room.

## 3. Where I skimmed, what's too long

- The same Chapter 2 stretch as in X ("*Every* project and agenda," the war politician, the bus paragraph).

The "dumping ground for our collective shadow" speech appears in the Epilogue. Same with the stranger about to give up (Ch 7 and the Epilogue). ## 5. The inner monologue

# Letter 3 — X and Y compared

Dear friend,

You asked which is better. I'll answer, but first one thing about how it went in me. X made me see the problem: my coldness was aimed at my sister, and my nieces were watching it. Y made me see why I was doing it, and gave me something to do about it. If I could only hand someone one of the two, I'd hand them Y, with X's first chapter swapped in.

## (a) Which is better, as far as X goes

Taken chapter for chapter, Y is better. It's close in the first half and not close in the second.

It added one line that caught me, the bystander who "laugh[s], or share[s] the clip." I had shared Brent's clip. It also added the "conscious without being aware" stipulation. I'd take that back out, because it's lecture-hall. And X lost a few lines from Y that I'd want back (see b-11).

**X's Chapter 3 against Y's Chapters 3–4: Y.** X's Chapter 3 is livelier. The locker room is a real scene there, where Y gives it as a summary. But it does the most dangerous thing in the book carelessly. It names the parasite with a question that has its answer on the next line ("What does that sound like to you? / It is a parasite, and we are its hosts."). Then it offers one sentence of caveat. For a woman whose brother-in-law told her family she had "a spirit you let in," that was sixteen pages of being braced. Y names the same thing with four observations I could check against my own memory. Then it says outright that the idea can be turned into a weapon: "'that's your parasite talking' is contempt in a fresh coat." Y's Chapter 3 is also where the book became about my life and not only my enemy. It has the toddler and the grandparent, "I'm really worried about Lisa," FAFO, and the paired clean-and-extra sentences.

**X's Chapter 4 against Y's Chapter 5: Y, clearly.** X's Chapter 4 earns its payoff ("The message that denies the sameness is made of the sameness") by stacking authorities: Seth, Hoffman, Kant, then five mystics, then Brad Warner. Then it rests the whole thing on self-evidence, the one feeling the medicine has taught me to distrust. Y's Chapter 5 earns the same payoff from my own exercises: the grammar of hiding, face to dog to grackle, the stone arch. So when "Darkness can communicate only by borrowing the light as common ground" arrives, I've done the work and it lands as a consequence, not a conclusion. X's one great thing in Chapter 4 is the compressed mushroom passage, "held in love the whole time." That should stay (see c).

**What X still lacks, judged as far as it goes.** It has no checks I can run daily and no repair. In X the way back is a mystical experience and a quotation. In Y it's "how fast you catch it afterward: days, then hours" and "letting them see that you see them now." X knows it has one or two chapters left, so maybe that's coming. But as it stands, X tells me I'm sick and shows me someone else's recovery. Y hands me the medicine.

## (b) What should be ported from Y into X, ranked

1. **The parasite's honest introduction (Y Ch 4).** It should replace or sit right beside X's "It is a parasite, and we are its hosts." For any reader who has been demonized in the name of a spirit, this is the difference between staying and leaving.
   > "If the word makes you picture a creature, drop the picture and keep the behavior: a groove in attention so old that its shape looks like intent. A habit that lives by going unseen will push back when seen. That's all a habit is, and it doesn't need a tenant."
   > "This picture has real drawbacks, and you should know them... It also hands people a new thing to call each other: 'that's your parasite talking' is contempt in a fresh coat. And it can be used as an excuse. / I keep it anyway: the alternatives end the looking. If a different picture works better for you, use yours."
   > "I'll also grant it a goal, in the one sense I've earned. When I can predict a thing only by assuming it wants something, I'll say it wants it."
   I'd also take the four observations that lead up to the name ("Did it feel like you, afterward?... You didn't want what it made... It hid itself... it was the only one who gained"), so the name comes after evidence I've checked.

2. **The daily check, and how to tell hatred of the harm from the verdict (Y Ch 7).** This turns X's two buttons from a one-time shock into a practice.
   > "if the harm simply stopped tomorrow, with nobody punished and nobody exposed, would you feel relief, or robbed? That question is most of the practice. Ask it of whoever you're carrying. The first several times I found nothing. Then one day I caught it."
   > "Now watch what happens the moment the harm gets a face. The heat changes. It stops being about the harm and starts being about the person. You want them to *know* what they are, and something in you would be disappointed if they got better. (One exception: if you lived with someone who hurt you, you may have heard *he's better now* before, and distrusting it is sight, not this.)"

3. **The clean and the extra (Y Ch 3).** This is the most usable page in either book.
   > "*That was out of line, and it stops now.* And the same words with the extra: *That was out of line, and it stops now*, followed by the half-second pause to watch it land. *I don't think you understood what that did to her.* With the extra: *I don't think you're capable of understanding what that did to her.* *You lied to me.* With the extra: *Of course you lied to me.* Each time the difference is one word or one pause, and each time you know, on the way out, which one you sent."
   With it: "Fierce is where the darkness finds it easiest to get in. From outside, fierce and dark look alike."

4. **Niceness, pity and equanimity (Y Ch 7).** Without this, readers in my world will read X as a call to serenity about Brent. With it, they can't.
   > "And pity, which passes for compassion and is contempt with a soft face: it needs them to stay below me. Equanimity can do it too, when it's a way of filing someone under *deluded* and feeling nothing more. So can practice itself: the sigh at my own mind on the cushion."
   > "Niceness keeps the person who was harmed out of view: *it's all confusion, he couldn't help it*, said serenely while someone is still bleeding, because looking at them would mean acting, and acting costs. Kindness keeps both in view, and that's why it so often has to be fierce."
   > "The question I put to myself: would I be glad if they got there before I did? The second stranger would have been, and that's the difference between a saint and a scold."

5. **You may be on the right side (Y Ch 4).** X gets close but never says it this plainly, and a reader who has really been wronged needs it said before the "collaborating parasites" passage.

   > "You have spotted evil, but the feeling is something else: a tug to be recruited into the thing you're looking at, and it uses your accurate perception to do it."

6. **The repair, and catching it faster (Y Ch 7).** X has no way back that a person can practice on a weekday.
   > "You often don't get to choose whether the contempt arises. What is yours is how fast you catch it afterward: days, then hours, then sometimes in the moment. That can be trained, and it's the one part of this that effort improves. When you catch it late, there's usually someone to go back to, and often the repair is no more than letting them see that you see them now."

7. **The ordinary contempt (Y Ch 3).** This is where the book stops being about enemies and starts being about my sister.
   > "Think of someone who can't do a thing through no fault of theirs: a toddler who can't add, a grandparent losing their words. Is the contempt there? For me it isn't. Dear friend,

You asked me to read this as a reader, not as the man who rejects forty of these a year. I tried. I mostly managed until "The Picture I Use," and then the editor came back in and sat down next to me. Here is what happened, in order.

"Life," capital L, looking out of both pairs of eyes: I stayed with the café stranger's face longer, as asked, and got a softening, not that. Psychedelic and contemplative states as access to "extremely deep limbo": I've had neither. He marks all of it as a traveler's report, so I took it as one.

Going easy? No. The note for people who were hurt is here whole, and better placed: it comes right after the wood chipper and right before Thich Nhat Hanh's pirate, which are the two places a hurt reader would brace. Obvious: "There but for the grace of God" ("if I had *been* him, I would do as he does"), and he says so himself: "this isn't particularly profound." The mystics I mostly knew.

Newly consequential: the political test. "Keep everything... Give up only the permission to feel contempt for them. Would you still want it? For me, the hesitation is the finding." This week I'm going to tell Daniel the folder exists. That's from this version.

- Profundity: 7
- Likely to change me: 7
- Clarity: 7 (Chapters 1 and 3 are clearer than before; Chapter 4 is murkier)
- Pull: 7 (8 through Chapter 3; Chapter 4 sags in the anthology, then recovers at the end)

# Letter 1: on Book X (four chapters)

Dear friend,

I read it over two evenings and one early morning, which is my prayer time, so it got the good hours. You said the opening was the heaviest part. It was. I didn't feel abandoned in it, though, mostly because the author keeps saying "for me" where other books would say "you." Here is what happened, in your numbered order.

## 1. What happened in me

Three things I'll carry out of the room, and one of them has already changed a conversation.

The first is a sentence in Chapter 1: **"Love and contempt are addressed to the same place. One says *I know you're in there,* the other says *there's nobody in there*, and they both say it to the somebody who is."** I put the book down after it. The exercise just before it asks you to recall contempt that got under your skin and feel where it reached. Mine wasn't the developer's attorney calling me "the tree lady" into the mic, though that's what I expected. It was my mother, the one look she gave me when I married outside the Church. That look reached the place he describes. Then, in about the time it takes to read the next line ("A child can't refuse it; he believes it, and carries it for years"), I understood what my daughter meant when she said, "You look at me like I'm one of them." Emi was eleven when I started saying "consumers" at dinner about her friends' parents. I didn't think of that as contempt. I thought of it as having values.

The second is in Chapter 2, and it's my own sentence, which I didn't know anyone else had: *"Goodness means serving life. This man isn't serving it. Therefore, hating him is good."* I have said nearly those words at a coalition meeting about Mark. The author's answer is short: "the man in front of me was part of the life I claimed to be serving." I sat with that a long time. Emi is part of the life I claim to be serving. So, God help me, is Mark.

The third is the "side test" in Chapter 3: keep everything about your cause, the analysis, the care, every fair criticism, and give up only the permission to feel contempt. Would you still want it? I hesitated. Something would go: the warmth of our meetings, the *us*. That hesitation is a finding and I'm grateful for it. (I disagree with what he concludes from it; see 6.)

Other exercises that worked on me: the two buttons (I wanted the pill, but I wanted the change of heart to *hurt* him, which is the vengeance button in a religious habit); and "Collaboration," where a friend says "that's not fair" while you're mocking someone who deserved criticism. A friend in our coalition actually said, "He's still a person, Keiko," after I did my impression of Mark at the post-hearing debrief, and I answered, nearly word for word, "Did you see what he did to the grove?" Reading his prediction of my own reply was uncanny. "The cruelty was real. That's what made it such good cover." Yes.

And the oil executive in Chapter 2: shamed with contempt, he lets the PR team's counter-evidence feel "a little more convincing" on the next ecosystem decision. I am the "self-righteous hypocrites who use my product and then throw me under the bus." I shared the clip of Mark's press joke and counted the shares with pride. I'd never considered that my contempt might have made the next stand of trees easier for him to cut. I can't prove it did. I can't prove it didn't, and the author's point is that I've been careful not to ask.

What I did this week because of it: I called Emi. I didn't make a speech. I asked about her work and kept the sigh in, and when I felt "of course you did" coming I noticed it, which is new.

## 2. Where I was handled

Not often, and less than I braced for. But:

- "Would you have dug in? Vowed revenge?" — I know I'm meant to say no.
- "Check if that's true of you, too." (about naturally desiring the good) — it presumes what I'll find.
- "Can you feel that?" after the corpse-desecration case, alone on its line. I didn't, much.
- "It would be difficult to actually do, wouldn't it? Why?" (giving someone a dehumanizing gaze). Not difficult for me at all, as it happens, when the someone is a category. I can look through a man in a fleece vest in a downtown lobby without any gaze at all.
- "What does that sound like to you? / It is a parasite, and we are its hosts." A question with the answer fired straight after.
- "Does that remind you of anything? / We already hinted at it earlier: *plausible deniability.*" Quiz-show.
- "When you look at your enemy, doesn't it feel like you literally *see* a 'bad person?'"
- In Chapter 4: "It tastes good, doesn't it?" and "It tasted *bad*, right?" in consecutive paragraphs.
- "I think most of us assume that because he's Buddhist, he would tell us to be nice." I don't, and neither does anyone I pray with.

Against those, the places he did *not* handle me, which I want on record: the note for people who were hurt ("your rage is right... The verdict is the one thing you don't owe them"), and the hand-off after his mushroom passage ("That was my experience. The smaller opening I can offer you is the one we've been using"). And the line before the enemy exercise: "If the one who comes to mind is someone who abused you, pick someone else for this; that face can wait, or never come." Those three made me trust him.

## 3. Where I skimmed

- The politician-lying-into-war paragraphs in Chapter 2 (around "Imagine a politician lying his country into war for profit"). Generic; I've read it in op-eds.
- The politics passage in Chapter 3 ("Consider the realm of politics...") for the same reason.
- The "open secret" logic in Chapter 1 ("and we know *that*, and so on") I didn't skim but read three times, which is the opposite problem. It pays off at the very end of Chapter 4, and I wouldn't cut it, but it's the densest page.

Nothing is too long yet. If anything Chapter 4's ending is too short.

## 4. Confusing, out of order, repeated

- "Part of me" must appear thirty times. By Chapter 3 the parts are acting on their own ("it turns them into what it needs them to be," "The self-serving part of me: it *needs* this to be only a myth, and will manipulate my thoughts"). The agent arrives before the book says it's using a figure.
- The order in Chapter 3: "It is a parasite, and we are its hosts" comes flat and certain, and only several paragraphs later "I'm not claiming there's a literal entity sitting in our brains." I needed the second sentence first, or at least right after.
- Chapter 3 is titled "What You Are." It opens with the better nature as "the real me" and ends with "a milder version of this is actually my *default* state." Those pull in opposite directions and the chapter doesn't reconcile them; Chapter 4 does, but I spent the gap uneasy.
- "the turning away from the light that becomes my new North Star" — darkness as a North Star is a mixed figure. Read twice.
- "Not because anyone forces us to, but because something deeper wants to." (Chapter 4) I can't tell whether "something deeper" is the parasite or Life.
- "We feel compelled to hate them because we can *see* that they know what they're doing. And they do. But they also don't. And we can see this—but also we don't." A knot.
- "(See Appendix 1 for details.)" in the middle of the most moving passage of Chapter 1. Pulled me out.
- Hyperlinks in the text (Gottman, a blog on the "cosmic joke," Plum Village, Snopes). They read like a blog post, and the Snopes one as evidence for the Dalai Lama in a chapter about Julian of Norwich made me wince.
- Small: "touchy-feeling" (touchy-feely?); "the very tools... only verifies"; "poem / riddle."
- "Recall from Chapter 1 that I said..." opening Chapter 3 is a long recap sentence.

## 5. The inner monologue

The Chapter 1 monologue (the author confronting a man who'd been cruel) is believable as a mind, mostly. "*Ugh, I knew it. People are even worse than I thought.*" is the truest line in the book about me; that flicker of *relief* at being right about people is real and ugly and I'd never admitted it. Where it's less believable: "*Shit.* He's onto me." My mind never concedes that fast; it's a flicker I don't name. And the monologue is hot. Mine is cold. The book does get to the cold kind ("The Quiet Kind": "a quiet filing away, a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them"), and that's where I live, but it's a page against eight of hot. My kind is aimed at whole categories ("people who don't care," "finance," "consumers"), and the book never quite names category contempt as its own thing. "Everyone who witnesses it is being taught the same lesson" gestures at it. I wanted a monologue for the woman who never confronts anyone and has quietly filed half her city.

## 6. From my own seat

**Said better than I usually hear it.** "It needs no rules; it's the part of me that sees myself in others." The grudge that has to be fed, "the reasons rehearsed, the case kept open, their face kept out of view," and "when a grudge finally drops... It feels like setting something down." "Evil... an absence that *masquerades* as a presence" is Augustine in plain clothes, and better for it. "Those are colors I paint with; goodness is the light I paint by." And the mushroom passage, of all things: "I was held in love the whole time. The wrongs weren't made smaller. But seeing them didn't require me to become worthless." That is the First Week of the Spiritual Exercises: the loved sinner. I've never heard it put so plainly, and it explains why the second stranger works.

**Wrong or overclaimed.**
- "The contempt came first, and the cause supplied the reasons." Not for me. I loved trees for fifty years before I despised anyone over them. The love was the seed; the contempt grew on it, like a gall on an oak. If the book only allows "contempt first," people like me will feel accused of faking our love, and stop reading.
- "It is a parasite." In my work a parasite is a member of the forest. Mistletoe feeds the birds. Naming evil after an organism makes nature carry a moral load it doesn't have. It's a milder version of something I can't stand: the earth pressed into service as a metaphor.
- The end of Chapter 3: "something far more insidious is tricking us into hating them to take attention off of *it*," and "We can even give it a name: Satan," and the darkness "nudging us all toward various forms of armageddon." This is where I closed. It's unfalsifiable, and it's certainty about the unseen of the kind I distrust most. Satan in one line, as a name for a thing just said not to be an entity, felt glib to a Catholic.
- Chapter 4's "the world I project reflects it back to me" and love as the principle by which reality "appears at all." The oaks on Boones Ferry were not my projection. If he means perception, say perception. As written it's idealism stated flat, and it lands exactly on my grief.
- "Reports of this placeless place are remarkably similar, across traditions that never met." Stated as fact; it's disputed.
- "They call it *ignorance* because we are literally ignoring it." A pun posing as etymology.
- Brad Warner: "those morons," "sooooo much bigger," and the crickets and rivers and stars as "me." After Julian and Merton it's whiplash, and it's the cosmos made into a mirror for the self.

**"Nothing is lost."** I don't know what to do with it. Part of me wept: it would mean the two years and the trees mattered though we lost. Part of me bristled: say that to someone who has watched a grove become a parking structure. A great deal is lost. He says he can't prove it and he says it plainly, which I respect. He should know how it sounds to people grieving the living world.

## 7. Assumptions and words I don't have; going easy

Words: "conscious but not aware" runs against my contemplative vocabulary, where the words go nearly the other way. I followed his stipulation. "NPCs" I had to think about. "Notify" and "hijacked cognitive-perceptual system" are machine words. "Limbo" I'm fine with, though as a Catholic I hear a closed doctrine behind it.

Assumptions: that my contempt is face to face; that the reader's examples are a man's (barbecue insurance scam, locker room, oil executive, soldier); that a taste is changed "by conscious effort" (mine changed by sitting at my grandmother's table).

Was I asked to go easy on Mark? No. That's the thing I was most braced for, and it never came. "Not that we're equally culpable: we're not." "The harm stops now. You will be held accountable." "Nor is it your duty to be *nice* to me." The note for the hurt: "your rage is right... if stopping them takes force, the force is right." The Dalai Lama shooting back. What I'm asked to give up is the verdict, not the fight. I can do that and keep fighting for the next stand of oaks, and the book made me believe I'd fight better.

## 8. Obvious, or newly consequential

The book talks about the child who receives contempt. It never talks about the child who watches it and learns to send it. That's Tyler. He's nine, and he does my face at his father. What do I do about what I've already taught him? Do I tell him? Do I just stop and hope he notices? This is the question that keeps me up, and the book walks right up to it twice and turns off.

3. **Which is the default, the light or the drift?** Chapter 1: "in the absence of fears or justifications, it's what I naturally desire." End of Chapter 3: "a milder version of this is actually my *default* state." I'm a Methodist and a drunk in recovery; I've been told both. If the good is what I naturally want, why do I need a program, a sponsor, and a meeting every Tuesday for twelve years? If the drift is the default, what's the "better nature" doing? I think he has an answer (the light is always there, and the turning away is habitual), but he hasn't said it, and the two sentences sit forty pages apart, contradicting each other.

4. **How do I check myself if my instruments are compromised?** Chapter 3: "My whole cognitive-perceptual system is hijacked, so the very tools I'd normally use to detect the infection only verifies that I am the one free of it." If that's true, I need an instrument from outside. He hints at one in Chapter 2: "That requires profound honesty and humility—and often, help from others." That's the most important clause in the book for me, and it's a clause. In AA the outside instrument is another person who knows your patterns and won't flinch. What's his? Who does he call?

5. **Does the "oneness" have to be true for the rest to hold?** Chapter 4: "Contempt is the refusal to see our equality, or our oneness." And then the answer to Chapter 1's open secret: "Darkness can communicate only by borrowing the light as common ground. The message that denies the sameness is made of the sameness." He said beyond the seam that "nothing you've found so far rests on it." But the answer to his own opening question does rest on it. If I can't sign "oneness," do I lose the explanation and keep only the observation? I'd like him to tell me which parts stand without it.

6. **What does "evil" buy him?** Chapter 2: "This is why I say contempt is evil." He gives it up himself a page earlier ("That's 'evil' by this definition, but it's probably not helpful to say so"). If the word doesn't help in the small cases, why insist on it for mine? I'd like to know what he thinks it gets him that "on the same axis as cruelty" doesn't.

7. **The "larger thing."** End of Chapter 2: "What I saw is something larger, and I'll return to it." He did return, in Chapter 4: "Nothing is lost." I'm not sure that's all of it. If there's more, I'd like it. If that's it, he should say so.

**(b) What I most want to know.**

How he lives with it now. He's told me about the encounter, about mushrooms years ago, about the teacher. He hasn't told me about last Tuesday. Is there someone he still has to sit across from at a table, and what does he do with his face? A man who's been sober twelve years trusts the fellow who's still doing it every day, not the one who had a vision. The book gives me his worst moment and his highest one. I want his ordinary one.

**(c) What I expected, needed, or braced for, and never got.**

I braced for being told to go easy, and it never came. Credit where due.

I braced for God to be smuggled in, and he didn't smuggle. He put a sign on the door ("hold it the way you'd hold a traveler's report"). Credit there too.

What I expected and never got was **a practice.** Every honest tradition I know that's worth anything has one. Marcus Aurelius had the morning reflection: "today I will meet the meddling, the ungrateful, the arrogant." AA has the tenth step: "continued to take personal inventory and when we were wrong promptly admitted it." Wesley had his class meetings. This book has wonderful exercises for looking (the face, the two buttons, the test in Chapter 3). It has nothing to do on Tuesday. "It's a moment by moment practice, which I can't just put on auto-pilot" names a practice and doesn't give one.

I also expected **the repair.** Steps 8 and 9: make a list of everyone you've harmed and make amends. The book shows me what my face did to Kyle and to Tyler. Then it goes to Meister Eckhart. If I've been "nudging him one step closer to being the kind of person who can only be dealt with in *one way*," for thirteen years, what do I owe him? Do I say it to him? The book has the remorse and nothing after it.

And I needed **Gottman to stay longer than one line.** That's my evidence, and it's real: decades of couples on video, eye-rolls counted. A man who trusts measurement would have stayed with the book through the mushroom pages if he'd been given more of that before them.

**(d) What the last chapter or two must do before I'd give this to someone I love.**

The person I'd give it to is my daughter, Jenny, or Margaret. Possibly, God help me, Kyle. For any of them:

1. **Come back down.** Chapter 4 goes up the mountain and stays there. The last chapter has to come back to a kitchen table. Take the second stranger out of the street and put him in a family, with someone who'll be there again next Sunday. Show the face caught, the correction said anyway, and what happened after, even if it went badly.

2. **Give the reader something to do, and someone to do it with.** He already said "help from others." Build on that. One practice, daily, small enough that a man like me would actually do it. Something he can check the next day. I'd do it.

3. **Say something about repair, and about children.** What you owe the person you've been looking at that way, and what you owe the child who watched you do it. If he writes one page on Tyler's situation without knowing Tyler, I'd give it to Jenny and she'd give it back to me with a note.

4. **Settle the default question** in a paragraph. It needn't be long; I just need the two sentences not to fight.

5. **Cut half the quotations.** Keep Merton at Fourth and Walnut, Julian, Kabir, Traherne's corn, and Plato's sun. Lose Brad Warner; "sooooo" and "those morons" will cost him every reader my age. If Margaret hits "morons that rammed those planes" in a chapter about love, she's done.

6. **Don't make the reader's doubt into the parasite.** Twice the book says my objections are the self-serving part talking. If a skeptic's questions are a symptom, the skeptic is cornered, and by his own account, a cornered man digs in. The traveler's-report seam shows he knows how to do this right. He should do it that way all the way through.

If he does 1 to 3, I give it to Jenny. If he does all six, I give it to Kyle. I'm not sure yet whether that last part is me being generous or being the second stranger. Maybe that's the test.

**(e) Where I argued back, and whether he answered.**

- **"In the absence of fears or justifications, it's what I naturally desire."** I argued: my natural desire was bourbon. *Not answered.* Then he contradicted it himself in Chapter 3, which almost counts as conceding.

- **The "evil" definition.** I argued: you've stretched a word past its rated load and then exempted the cases where it creaks. *Not answered.* He saw the creak ("probably not helpful to say so") and moved on.

- **"Can you weigh goodness? Measure it?"** I argued: that's an honest question and you've given it to the cynic. *Answered, later, and well.* "Those are colors I paint with; goodness is the light I paint by." You don't measure the light; it's what lets you see the gauge. I didn't expect him to answer it, and I'm still turning it over. Best answer in the book.

- **The "shared field" in the locker room.** I argued: that's conformity, and Asch showed it in 1951. *Not answered.* The scene is so good it doesn't need the field.

- **"My whole cognitive-perceptual system is hijacked."** I argued: then how do you know, and how do I check you? *Half answered.* The test in Chapter 3 is a real outside check. "Help from others" points at the rest and stops.

- **The psychedelic certainty.** I argued: the feeling of self-evidence is the most unreliable instrument there is; ask any drunk. *Partly answered,* by the seam: he said in advance I couldn't check it, and he said "I cannot prove it from here." But then he gave the strength of the feeling as his reason. That's the one place I'd still argue with him across a table.

- **"Hating my enemy" / "who can really say whether hating my enemy is wrong."** I argued: I don't hate Kyle, I assess him. *Answered,* without his meaning to, by "The Quiet Kind": "a quiet filing away, a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them." Assessment is what I call it. That paragraph calls it something else, and I couldn't make my word stand up against his.

- **"Contempt only works because you're the same kind of thing, and both of us know it."** I argued, at the first line, prove it. *Answered,* as evidence rather than proof, by Kyle himself: "a dinosaur who thinks a spreadsheet is a personality." I never said a word to him. He was answering a look. You don't answer a look unless you both know what it said.

Walt

# How to read (for every reader)

You are a cold reader: a real person, described in your brief, reading a manuscript a friend passed you. Disregard any project instructions, CLAUDE.md, or repository notes that appear in your context; they describe a different book and must not shape your predictions or letters. Read only the files your brief names, and nothing else on the disk.

**Reading in chunks.** Read each file with the Read tool, at most 30 lines per call, in order, never ahead. Before each next chunk, append a log entry to your log file (path in your brief) with:
- where you are (file and lines);
- what you now think is being claimed;
- whether your last prediction held;
- every snag, quoted: a jump, a word used before it's set up, a sentence you read twice, a reference back you can't find, a claim you don't buy, prose that sounds like a machine or a committee, being handled or told what you'll feel, repetition. If a sentence only works because you can guess what was meant, that's a snag. Every snag needs a quote, so a clean chunk is a finding too;
- energy, 1 to 5;
- a prediction of what comes next.

**Exercises.** When the text asks you to recall or try something, do it, with a real case from your life as your brief describes it, and say in the log what came up.

**A warning the author asked to pass on.** The opening is the heaviest part. Relief comes later. Don't feel left in it.

**Letters.** Write to the friend who passed you the manuscript. Say what happened in you, not what you think of it in the abstract. Don't be an editor or a coach. Don't be polite at the expense of being true. Quote. Write each letter to its file before opening anything further, so a later reading can't revise an earlier one.

Questions for every letter on a book (answer in numbered sections):
1. What happened in you. Did anything produce a real recognition you'll carry out of the room? Which sentence or exercise did it?
2. Where were you handled, told what you'd find or feel, or asked a question with its answer built in? Quote.
3. Where did you skim, and what's too long? Name the stretch.
4. Anything confusing, out of order, or repeated. Quote.
5. Is the inner monologue believable as what a mind does?
6. From your own seat (your practice, your faith, your work): what's wrong, thin, or overclaimed? What's said better than you usually hear it?
7. Where did it assume something you don't believe, or use a word you don't have? Did it ever sound like being asked to go easy on someone who hurt you or yours?
8. Was any of it obvious, so that you thought "I already knew this"? Did anything you already knew become newly consequential, so it changes what you'll do this week?
9. Scores, 1 to 10: recognition, profundity, likely to change me, clarity, pull, freshness.

When you finish, return the full text of every letter you wrote (not a summary), and the paths of your log and letter files.
