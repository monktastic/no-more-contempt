## The Deepest Form

The contempt I've found hardest to see is aimed at everything at once. It's the world held as stuff: a lifeless collection of things, there for us to use. It's so common it passes for realism.

Peoples have subjugated one another for as long as there have been peoples. That isn't new. What the modern world added was a program. In early modern Europe, Francis Bacon wrote of "enlarging the bounds of Human Empire" over nature, and Descartes hoped science would make us its "masters and possessors."[^mastery]

Power over nature isn't what troubles me. We need food and shelter, and there's no shame in a bridge. What troubles me is the way of seeing that grew up around that power. Nature became something we stand outside of, measure, and put to use. In time, *what I can measure* quietly became *what exists*. The part of me that recognized something sacred in a forest was told it was having a feeling. A feeling can be respected, but it gets no say over what is built. That's the turning away from Chapter 1, at the scale of a civilization. My enemy knew what I was doing and couldn't prove it. Here everyone knows, and the knowing doesn't count. A culture can keep its churches, its prayers, even its sincere believers, and still run its institutions as though nothing were sacred.

This is the pattern I meant when I said the one that gave rise to colonization still exists. A people held as a resource and a forest held as lumber are the same seeing, aimed at different things. It went furthest in Europe, which exported it by force. It runs everywhere now, India included. It runs in me.

What I say next I can't give you a check for. In the fullest seeing I've known, the line between living and not-living didn't hold. A stone was there in its own right as much as the tree beside it. As far as I can tell, mystics in nearly every tradition report the same. The sacred they find isn't a feeling laid over the world. It's what the world was all along, before we learned to see it as stuff.
