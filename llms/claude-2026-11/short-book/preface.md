# The Book

> *For so long as the root of wickedness is hidden, it is strong. But when it is recognized, it is dissolved. When it is revealed, it perishes. ... It is powerful because we have not recognized it.* —The Gospel of Philip

People often come back from psychedelics saying that love is the most important thing in the world, that it's the right response to literally every situation. What are we to do with that? Sure, love your enemies, up to a point. But past that, continuing to love them seems soft, even stupid. So it seems reasonable to conclude that "literally" is just an overexcited mind getting carried away. Maybe we can rescue the insight by removing that word: *love most people, most of the time*. Lovely, but I doubt anyone needed magic mushrooms to tell them that.

But what if "literally" is actually correct? What if it's the "love" part we're misunderstanding, and it means nothing like how we normally use that word—being nice, or soft, or even tolerant?

Well, then they probably should have picked a better word, or done a better job defining it, right? But what if—as many will insist—it is precisely *that which cannot be defined?*

Then we really have a problem. It becomes one of those things where you either get it or you don't—and if you don't, there's no clear reason to try, and no clear way to start if you did.

I don't think that's okay. If it's actually the *most important thing in the world*, then someone ought to be able to get it across.

This is my attempt.

---

Why should I succeed?

I don't know that I will. But here's what I do know: the key insight is something every one of us already knows, deep down. The real challenge isn't in conveying it, but *evoking* it.

What evoked it in me, many years ago, was being pointed back to an experience I'd had many times: places where I'd refused to love, out of a confused sense of duty. I saw how that single confusion was behind essentially all the horrors of the world, and how I'd been contributing to them, even as I was flawlessly convinced I was helping. But the strangest part is that I wasn't being shown something new—I was being shown how I had always known this, but pretended that I didn't.

When that pretense was stripped away, something was revealed: something that had always been there, patiently waiting to be remembered.

I can't claim to be living this realization fully: far from it. But it's clear why remembering it is the only way I'll ever be of real help to others.

The hope is to evoke that recognition in you, using that same experience that had worked on me.

---

Most of the book is about the opposite of love, which is evil. The main engine is one familiar thing: *moral contempt*, the way we treat our enemies as though they were *inherently defective.* Traced carefully, it turns out to be a uniquely insidious expression of evil. It's how the darkness spreads between us while masquerading as righteousness.

I know *evil* is a heavy word, and I mean it: not as a value judgment or an attack, but as a description, and the book earns it. And it isn't an abstract idea; my hope is to help you notice whether you've been acting on a knowledge of it all along. Where something is only what I see, I say so.

The book is meant to be read from a settled seat; Chapter 1 especially, since it asks before it tells. If it provokes the recognition, the rest is detail.

This work is *far* from finished. Part of my hope for this version is to find people with deeper insight and better communication skills than mine, who wish to collaborate. If that's you, let me know.

And a warning. Parts of this are hard to read, because they ask you to look at things in yourself that nobody enjoys looking at. Go slowly, and stop when you need to. Nothing here is a verdict on you.

May all beings be free of suffering and its causes.
