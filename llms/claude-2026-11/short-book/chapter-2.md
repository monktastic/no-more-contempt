Imagine looking into a stranger's face until it stops being something you're looking at and becomes a place somebody is looking out of. Don't try to add anything on. Just stay with the face, and see what comes.

If I stay there long enough, here's what comes for me—not a conclusion, but a recognition:

> *There's someone in there. They're real from the inside, the way I am.*

Now notice how you feel about their wellbeing while that stays in view. For me, it starts to register. Not necessarily as affection, but as something I can't treat as nothing. Using them for my own ends feels *wrong*, in a way I didn't have to learn or reason through.

This is what I mean by being *turned toward* them. Notice whether it requires any stories to maintain, or if there's something natural about it—something that stories get in the way of. We're not *fully* turned toward them yet (we'll see what that's like later), but it's in the right direction.

From this place, try to wish them harm. For me it can't be done. Something has to go out of view first, or be covered over: I must *turn away* from that perception—usually by adding a story about them and what they are. Accounts of atrocity return again and again to the same preparation: the hood, the number instead of the name, the word for vermin.

Out of view doesn't mean out of sight. A torturer has his victim in plain sight, and the cruelest take pleasure from watching their face closely. What they've turned from isn't the person's presence. It's the claim that might otherwise arise from it: that the one looking out of that face counts the way they do. That's the only sense of "in view" I'll use, and it's the sense contempt was using all along, when it spoke to the one who's in there to tell him nobody is.

Not everyone you pass is in view in this sense, but that doesn't make it contempt. The hundred people around you in an airport are in the background; if one of them dropped a bag and started crying, they'd come into view, and you'd feel the pull. Turning away is keeping someone out of view when they'd come forward, because their reality would otherwise place a claim on you.

Now imagine your enemy's face instead (if it's someone who abused you, pick another), and try for the recognition of sameness. If it won't come, notice what arrives in its place, and how fast. For me the story comes immediately, and obscures that recognition. That's the turning away, live. Notice how you feel about their wellbeing from here.

*You can skip this next step, especially if the exercise is already unsettling.*

If you want to see how far that turn can go, try, just for a moment, to stop registering the life in a person altogether. For me, it's nearly impossible. Something in me refuses to go there, because I can feel where that perception leads. Notice what happens in you; don't try to push past a refusal. Or imagine going out in public and giving someone a dehumanizing gaze. It would be difficult to actually do, wouldn't it? Why?

Now return to the stranger's face, and the recognition that there's someone there. 

---

What does *evil* mean? Start with where you'd already use the word: a politician who lies his country into a war, executives who keep selling a drug they know is killing people, someone who tortures an animal for fun. What is the word tracking?

It can't be outcome alone. The driver who hits a child who ran out between parked cars will carry it for the rest of his life, and nobody calls him evil. The driver who tries to hit a child and misses surely deserves the word. The friend who spilled wine on your fancy couch left a visible mark; the one who made a small cutting remark didn't. Yet the remark is what you're still thinking about a week later.

Nor is it just about size. The man who tortures a mouse hurts one tiny creature—a few grams of matter—and nobody hesitates over the word. Smaller still, watch what cutting in line does to you. The cost is thirty seconds, but the heat is out of all proportion. What you're reacting to isn't the cost. “Evil” may be too strong a word for that case, but check whether it lies on the same axis.

Now think back to your own wrong in Chapter 1. If it was cheating on a test, what did you have to do to feel okay with it? For me, I have to feel that the rules don't apply to me. I make myself an exception, the way the politician does: other people's children can die for his ambition. The executives make one for their company, the man with the animal for his fun, the line-cutter for his time.

But *making myself an exception* and *feeling like I'm worth more* don't quite capture it. If my back is hurting, I can take the last seat on the bus without it being evil in the slightest. A human is worth more than a mouse in a meaningful sense, and sometimes I must rely on that to do good.

What do the evil cases have in common? It's that someone had to be put out of view. It is the turning away.

Here's what I find in myself. Turning away from myself, from my better nature, from the life in others, and from the full implications of my behavior—they're all facets of a single move. It is that move I call *evil*, and I think it's what we've been tracking all along.

The padded expense, the lie to the insurer, the joke that makes someone smaller, the slur, the order to clear a village: each needs someone held out of view so that I, or mine, can be the exception.

If I get on the bus with a pregnant or elderly person and pretend not to see them, I'll usually find myself muttering a story about why it's not my duty to help. Or maybe I bury myself in my music before getting on, to drown out any potential of seeing others. That's "evil" by this definition, but it's probably not helpful to say so. I may legitimately feel like I need more comfort in this moment, and calling myself names isn't going to help me clarify that in a way that helps me express the need while remaining *turned toward*. It's a subtle issue, and we won't get into all the nuances here.

The harm I caused in the last chapter was tiny beside an atrocity. But it still felt malevolent, because it's the same fundamental gesture across all scales. The tiniest whiff of it carries the scent of the whole.

In the very worst people, it appears as something like a *thirst for devouring innocence*. My desire to smother my enemy's "innocence": it was exactly that. When I look deeply enough into my desire to make the *bad people* suffer, it's what I find there, too. It is the thing we all hate; hatred itself. That's why we don't look so deeply.

This is why I say contempt is evil. I don't just mean it's *bad.* I mean it is the thing in disguise. That's why it works.

---

Of course, outcomes matter too. If the harm I caused my enemy ended with him, we might still balk at the name "evil." After all, maybe *the ends justify the means*? In that case, my inner knowing would just be issuing a false alarm, and I'd be right to silence it.

But I don't think that's true. I think I'm using that argument to avoid seeing something. Cruelty is wrong by itself, and the ends usually bear that out.

Start with an act that has no living victim left to hurt.

Ask a soldier whether it's wrong to desecrate an enemy's corpse, even if nobody is watching and no one will ever find out. Most will say yes, without hesitation. But why? A corpse can't be hurt, and his loved ones will never know.

Look what it does to the soldier. It encourages him to degrade life. Not just the life of his enemy, but *life in general.*

Can you feel that?

If his allies were watching, it would teach them the same thing. We can't pinpoint where or how—or even if—that lesson will manifest as physical harm to anyone, and yet we sense the wrongness anyway. We don't need to trace the consequences to know that. Why not?

---

Say I'm an oil executive who's been harshly criticized for deceiving the public—in one case, with my dignity intact; in another, with contempt. Later, a proposal crosses my desk that will benefit me while potentially degrading a sensitive ecosystem. There's no conclusive proof that it will, and my PR team has provided ample counter-evidence.

How am I likely to choose in each case?

If I'm honest, I'm more likely to license myself to act out of self-interest after the contempt—because *fuck those self-righteous hypocrites who use my product and then throw me under the bus for it*. I don't tell myself I'm using that as an excuse to cause harm. The counter-evidence just feels a little more convincing. I give one side of a genuinely uncertain question slightly more weight, and feel I've made a reasonable decision. No one can prove why I tipped the scales—not even me, if I keep looking at the evidence instead of what I'm using it for.

My choice remains mine. Their contempt didn't force it. But it did make my own turning away feel more natural, and others will suffer as a consequence. Will they factor in that longer-term outcome? No. They'll pat themselves on the back for a job well done: a liar exposed.

And yet, I think, part of them can sense that's not the whole story.

When I'm in their shoes, my conscience is warning me about *something.* I don't know what—whether an ecosystem will suffer, or my target will just take it out on his wife and kids. So I tell myself the warning is naive. I mean, how could it possibly predict the future?

By turning away from myself, I turn away from the full implications of my behavior—and that acts as an invitation for others to do the same, which helps those very implications come to pass.

And it doesn't stop with the two of us. Say I'm only watching: I see someone made small by a person who still looks like the one in the right. Maybe I laugh, or share the clip. The next time I want to make an exception for myself, I have his example to draw on.

The audience has catchphrases, too. *Darwin award. FAFO. Play stupid games, win stupid prizes.* Each one enjoys somebody's harm and files it as deserved: they were stupid, so it doesn't count. Have you said one lately? What did saying it feel like?

Contempt for any one being is contempt for *life in general.* I don't mean this as a philosophical claim. I mean that when I really stop to taste what it's "made of," this becomes impossible to ignore. The only reason it feels "righteous" is because I'm paving over that flavor with convincing stories.

---

What does the alternative look like?

*The harm stops now. You will be held accountable.* Firm action, if necessary. Any number of fierce behaviors that show him what I stand for, without assuming he's incapable of standing for the same. It leaves the door open for him to return. I can't know he'll take it, but I can at least know I didn't give him *license*.

When I'm the first stranger, I tell myself the outcome is all that matters. But notice what I actually do: I make sure the facts are on my side and *sneak in* the thing I know will harm him. If I really believed the visible facts told the whole story, why would I need to sneak in anything? I must already know they don't, or else I couldn't use that lie to my advantage.

When I measure outcomes, I get to choose where to draw the boundary. The effects on other people's minds, and thus on what they do later, are among the hardest to count—and yet they can be of far greater consequence than what I *do* measure. This, I've found, is where my darkness has the greatest opportunity to inject itself into the world. 

Imagine a politician lying his country into war for profit while encouraging dehumanization of the enemy. Beyond the physical damage, the people on the other end are being taught the same lesson I taught my enemy: *they're evil*. They feel morally compelled to retaliate, with less concern for what lines they cross. He then points to that as proof that he was right, justifying more war—and more profit.

Does he know he's doing this? Yes and no: he "honestly" believes his reasons (some of which may be entirely valid) while another part of him shapes them to get what it wants. Another politician might well have fought the same war without spreading that contempt, likely producing less corruption in all minds involved, and thus less long-term harm.

I have the same choice when I'm his supporter: if his lies benefit me, I stop asking what it costs others. I can point to its real benefits, downplaying the invisible harms.

*Every* project and agenda has this potential. I can start a righteous cause and imbue it with contempt, providing me with an endless supply of enemies to blame. I can create a company that provably accomplishes good by our chosen metrics, but whose existence is rooted in my need to self-aggrandize. The people who join me sense it, and it subtly influences how they hold themselves—whether they're serving themselves or something greater. That then leaks out into the world via countless invisible channels, making it worse in ways that "require" me to assert even more control.

Two seemingly-identical actions, taken from different orientations, can produce vastly different outcomes. The more I see this, the less I can base decisions purely on predicted outcome. I start sensing into the self-deception that's driving them. Even if the action seemingly benefits me, I may still refuse it based on how it's likely to affect the *whole.*

Outcomes still matter, of course. Care doesn't tell me which policy will work. But no calculation can make me honest if I'm using it to keep some part of the situation out of view—which, I find, I almost always am, to one degree or another.

---

When most of us picture evil, we think of it as something people *have*: a drive toward harm, a dark side alongside their better nature, or in place of it. In other words, as a kind of *presence*. But I think it's actually an absence that *masquerades* as a presence.

A subtle turn doesn't look like evil. It looks like me going about my day, treating the world as something there to entertain me or meet my needs. Other people's wellbeing doesn't register as particularly relevant. They become "NPCs," and I'm the main character. I still help, of course—I'm a *good person*—but I'm more interested in getting their need out of my way than really being of service. I shave off a little extra for myself here or there, because who else will look after me?

This is a state I often find myself in, and I would take exception to calling it "evil." But just by being in it, I'm showing others what kind of world I think this is. They feel it, whether I register this or not.

Further along, I may help only when it benefits me, such as when others are watching. In private, I'm busy with projects that quietly serve me, making sure I can prove they're "for the greater good." My orientation is wrong, but nobody can prove it. Hell, I can't prove it myself. What "orientation"?

The horror is that I feel *more* righteous as I go. My cause is good; the people standing in its way don't count. The more harm I do for it, the more I need the cause to justify me. My reasons have taken the place of the care they once served, but from inside, I can't tell the difference.

Further still, even the need to call myself "good" falls away. What's the point of that charade? We all know that genuine goodness is a myth; a fairytale naive people use to avoid facing the cold, hard truth: that we're all in this for ourselves. I may still understand its language perfectly, even use it to manipulate others. But it no longer feels like something I answer to.

It's the same cynicism I taught my enemy—and I did it because part of me fears it might be true. After all, can you weigh goodness? Measure it? Can you even give it an *objective definition*? If not, then why should I believe it's a real thing? It's just another nebulous concept that we're each free to define for ourselves.

*That's* the deeper lesson I was teaching him: that all that matters is what we can get away with; what we can prove. Right now I'm proving that I'm the good guy, and *abracadabra*, it becomes so. My deeper knowing doesn't count, and yours shouldn't either.

What I'm not seeing is that I don't have the authority to redefine goodness. I *am* beholden to something.

The further I drift, the worse *other* people look. I don't recognize this as my own projection, because I think of projection as mere thoughts I can recognize and adjust for. I'm a smart guy, after all. I don't realize how my beliefs literally shape my reality, making me unable to see through them. I just think I'm looking *at* reality.

The worse other people look, the more they deserve my contempt. And because for some strange reason they see *me* that way, they feel morally compelled to do it to me.

---

My better nature is the only thing that warns me when I've turned from it. This is why the further I go, the worse my behavior, but the less I'm able to notice it. It's not that I become less *conscious* of it, exactly. It just goes deeper into limbo, so that it *feels* like I don't know it. My reasoning mind keeps telling me I'm on the right track.

Think of a wrong that was especially hard to admit. Was it hard because you knew less about it, or because there was more you couldn't bear to face? In me, the difficulty was a measure of how much I was keeping out. Getting used to not looking didn't mean there was less to see. It meant the opposite.

I think we're looking at the far end of this when we see an "evil" person. Part of them knows it's "wrong," but the meaning is inaccessible, so it doesn't feel, well... *wrong*. What's "right" is whatever they choose. Other people are just tools to get it.

We feel compelled to hate them because we can *see* that they know what they're doing. And they do. But they also don't. And we can see this—but also we don't. It is a *motivated* not-seeing; it's just that the motivation can be very hard to recognize.

Turning away takes upkeep, at least until it's practiced enough to run by itself. A grudge has to be fed: the reasons rehearsed, the case kept open, their face kept out of view. Being turned toward someone takes nothing like that. And when a grudge finally drops, it doesn't feel like starting a new effort. It feels like setting something down.

This is why I don't think evil is an equal and opposite force. It's what behavior looks like the further we drift from the baseline. There doesn't need to be an opposing baseline with its own qualities for it to *look* like one exists.

I can't prove that someone at the extreme still has a better nature, or how they got to where they are. What I can report is this: the more turned toward I am, the more I can perceive what's happening in them, and respond with fierce kindness. The more turned away, the more I see a foreign substance called *evil*, which I feel morally compelled to punish them for.

---

So then what is goodness? 

I think it's an orientation. If I had to name it, I might call it being *turned toward all of life*. Not one person, and not my side: the second stranger wasn't exactly serving you, and he wasn't serving himself.

But I hesitate to name it at all, because watch what happens when I do: *Goodness means serving life. This man isn't serving it. Therefore, hating him is good.* That's what I did to my enemy. It's the orientation turned into an idea, and it failed for the simplest reason: the man in front of me was part of the life I claimed to be serving.

*Every* attempt to define goodness leaves some loophole that lets me follow the letter of the law while violating its spirit. This is how most of my self-deception sneaks in: by following a definition, I can avoid feeling the *texture* of what I'm doing. Who cares about touchy-feely things like "texture" when I can literally prove I'm doing good?

But the opposite approach fails, too. When I tell myself that goodness has no definition, I end up at *who's to really say?* What's good is then whatever I decide in that moment.

What I find hard to hold is that there *is* an orientation there that I can recognize and am bound by, I can't pin it down in a definition, and that still doesn't make it mine to define.

---

As long as I can feel contempt for *anyone*, that dark urge still exists somewhere in me. So what is it that prevents me from sliding down that slippery slope into becoming the monsters I hate? Every person who took part in atrocities against their neighbors probably told themselves it could never be them.

The answer feels obvious: I know right from wrong. But that's not enough. I've always known that contempt for my enemies was wrong, and that hasn't ever stopped me. Or maybe I tell myself there are some lines I just won't cross—that I won't harm innocent people, for example. But that rule only helps if I can tell who's innocent, and I've been sure someone deserved what I gave them, then found out later I'd been fooling myself.

As long as I feel I'm made of *better stuff*, I'm leaving a door open. Under the right conditions, I can be nudged, bit by bit, away from my better nature in a way that makes me feel increasingly certain I'm still following it. I don't think there's a checkpoint where I'd notice I'd become the monster. The belief that there will be one is exactly what gets me in trouble.

As best I can tell, the only solution is to *stay oriented*, moment by moment. That's a process I can't delegate to my rational mind, because of how easy it is to fool myself. My rational mind must be in service of the orientation, not the other way around. The more I'm lying to myself, the more of my mind is organized around hiding it from me.

The first, smallest move I watch for is putting myself slightly above someone. That's an early sign that I'm shielding myself from the claim they could make on me, so I can get away with something. If I'd caught myself early with my enemy, I might have been able to course correct. But the longer it went on, the harder it was to admit what I'd been doing.

I can't tell you exactly what your self-deception feels like. Only you know for sure when you're lying to yourself—though someone else often sees it first, the way the second stranger did. But you may recognize a moment when you found a reason not to look at something, and felt relieved. Mine has a signature, and the more honest I get, the earlier I notice it.

---

This puts into perspective what the second stranger did. He didn't benefit directly. If anything, it cost him: it's hard to confront others, and he showed that he could be held to the same standard—that he wasn't allowed to make exceptions for himself.

What does that leave in you?

For me, it's the reverse of *why should I be the lone chump?* It reminds me that integrity has value even when it can't be measured. Later, I may be a little more willing to acknowledge something I'd rather not face, or do right by someone who will never know what it cost—because I know there are others out there.

He restored the recognition that *genuine goodness exists*, and that I still answer to it. It wasn't his invention, or mine. I might have disagreed with him on nearly everything, yet I recognized what he was serving—and that neither of us could commandeer it to suit our own needs.

People can refuse the lesson of contempt, of course. But whenever I have, what I drew strength from was the very thing the contempt was trying to make me give up on.
