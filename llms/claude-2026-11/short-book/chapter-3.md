# Chapter 3: What You Are

Recall from Chapter 1 that I said my better nature is the deepest part of me: what exists beneath my self-deception, which is why hiding misbehavior from it feels like hiding it from *myself*, and going against it feels like going against myself. Go back to the wrong you remembered. When you finally owned up to it, did it feel like losing something of yourself, or like getting something back?

For me, it feels like coming back to myself. That's the sense in which my better nature is the "real me." The darkness is what I do in my *absence*. It's why the person giving you a dehumanizing gaze feels absent, not more present.

This requires some care. We've all heard the excuse "that wasn't the real me!", and it can be used to avoid responsibility. But it's also true. I am conscious of what I do in that state, and fully responsible for the harm I cause from it. But while I am fully present, fully *turned toward*, this behavior cannot exist. Here's another test: have you ever been "snapped out of" being mean? Can you imagine a person being awoken out of decency back into their natural cruelty?

While I'm under its sway, it feels entirely like me. I don't feel less perceptive. I feel I've finally seen through my enemy. When someone questions my verdict, I defend it as I would defend myself. That person seems naive, stupid, or even evil themselves.

And look at the bargain I'm defending. I get the pleasure of hating my enemy, the relief of knowing he's the bad one, a little more freedom to serve myself. In exchange, I help make a darker world: people more trapped, institutions more corrupt, life more degraded.

But is that what I most deeply want? When I face the whole bargain, I can't want it. Yet I keep taking my part. So the whole trick is to keep the rest out of my sight.

When I begin to see what I'm doing, I feel ashamed. *What kind of person would do that?* That gives me a reason to look away again.

So this pattern benefits from being seen *as me*—by me, and by everyone else. When I mistake it for myself, I defend it. When others mistake it for what I am, they feel justified treating me with contempt. Their contempt gives me another reason to dig in.

Think back to the encounter in Chapter 1. When my enemy sees through one story, I have another ready. The verdict stays fixed. The darkness acts with apparent intelligence because it has my attention, my reasons, my eyes to work with.

And what comes out stronger through all this? The *darkness itself*. I become more practiced at turning away, my enemy feels more justified in doing it, and each of us has more evidence that the problem is in the other. The darker our world becomes, the more we feel we need our darkness to survive in it.

So here we have something that hides in my blind spots and lures me away from myself. While I'm turned away, it's the one in control. It feels like me, and I defend it as such. ## A note for people who were hurt

If the person you're thinking of is somebody who hurt you, or your child, or your people: your rage is right. Stopping them is right, and if stopping them takes force, the force is right. The fierceness comes from a good place, and nothing here asks you to feel less of it. But notice whether a second thing is riding along with it. Something that says: *I've been hurt, so I've earned the right to unleash a little of my own darkness.* If it's there, look at it. The verdict is the one thing you don't owe them. What they do is theirs. Your rage didn't cause it.

Seeing clearly what they are capable of is not the verdict. You need that sight, and nothing here asks you to doubt it. The verdict is the other thing: that this is all they are. I can only say what holding it did in me. It was the evidence my own darkness ran on, *see, this world is a war, and the only thing that works is power*, and it kept me from looking at my own case.

I want to say this part carefully, because if you were hurt, you have every reason to hear it as one more person telling you how to feel. You have earned things. Your rage, your refusal, your distance, and whatever it takes to make sure it never happens again: all of that is yours. Staying turned toward someone never means staying near them. If you're still living with the person who hurt you, or still within their reach, distance comes first. It is possible to leave, cut contact, and never see them again with no verdict in it at all. And nothing here demands that you turn toward them at all. That may come one day, or never, and there's no shame either way.

The only thing to notice is this, and it's only an offer. That license, the one that says you've also earned a little darkness of your own: look at where it comes from. It doesn't come from the hurt. It comes from the same kind of turning away that hurt you. That darkness wants you to take the license, because nothing proves its case better than a good person who was wronged and took it. And the pull to do so is strongest here, because here you can see the darkness more clearly than anywhere else, and it uses that clear sight as the hook.

And what actually frightens it is the other thing: fierce, unyielding refusal that carries no verdict. That isn't a nice idea from a book. As far as I can tell, it has a long record against power that needs consent, and none against power that doesn't; there, force is right. Where it works, my reading of why is that it does to power what the second stranger did to you: it gives the thing nothing to eat. It is also much harder than contempt, which is why contempt gets sold to you as the strong option.

And then there's the contempt you aim at yourself, which Chapter 1 asked you to set down. The way you hold your own darkness is the way you'll end up holding theirs, and you hold your own first.

## Collaboration

The "secret handshake" doesn't happen only between enemies. It often happens between friends.

Say a manager at work has humiliated a junior colleague in front of the team. Later, a friend and I are talking about it. We agree that it was cruel, and that someone should say something.

Then I do an impression of the manager. My friend laughs, so I exaggerate the manager's mannerisms until he laughs harder.

What are we enjoying?

Partly, it's the relief of finding someone who agrees that something was wrong. But there's also another pleasure: making the person who did it look small.

Bring to mind a conversation of your own that went something like this. Pick one where the criticism was justified. Recall what it felt like when the other person laughed, or added to what you'd said. Did their agreement make you more careful about being fair, or more comfortable going further?

Now imagine a second colleague interrupting: *I agree that what he did was wrong. But I don't think that's fair.*

How would you feel?

For me, there's a twinge of embarrassment, followed by defensiveness. *Did you not hear what he did?* I'd point back to the original cruelty. And the cruelty was real. That's what made it such good cover.

I deflect so that I don't have to look too carefully at what I was doing: using the situation to foster an ugly side of ourselves. We were in a kind of *joint limbo*: both secretly knowing what we were doing, and using each other as cover *not* to know. It was a shared *shadow space* in which both our parasites could come out and play. And when someone punctured that space, they both went back into hiding. On later reflection, neither of us will sense what really happened—how our parasites had been strengthening themselves.

And look how I construct that space. I start by testing the water with my impression, and when I get a laugh, that's my signal that it's safe to go deeper. When one person gets uncomfortable, or the wrong person walks in, then I scale it back or shut it down. It's like my darkness is scanning for *collaborators*, and testing how safe it is to fully emerge.
