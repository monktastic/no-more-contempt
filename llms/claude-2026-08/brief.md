# Brief

Our society has lost confidence that "good" means anything we could agree on, so we spend our energy fighting the people we're sure are bad. My claim is that there *is* something universal to steer by; that everyone already knows it; and that we treat our enemies with contempt to disorient that knowing in them, so that we can prove we're nothing alike.

The most original claim of my work is that we already know that last part, too. We must, for contempt to be coherent at all. It only works because certain things are already common knowledge, in the game theorist's sense, between the one sending it and the one receiving it. Each knows that the other knows that the other knows, etc. Contempt is thus a recursive and self-referential trap, which I will try to show.

Evil is a form of confusion that contempt is (secretly) meant to spread. Evil is nothing but a turning away from our true nature, but it behaves like a separate agent, hiding itself from our minds while displaying itself to our enemies. It is like a parasite. Each of us has one, and they link up into something that behaves like a single entity—what the ancients called the Devil.

Since we can't agree on what's good, we've concluded that we're free to decide it for ourselves. That feels like rational humility. I think it's the most successful lie of our time: not that goodness isn't real, but that it means whatever we decide. If the "Devil" could be said to have a goal, this is it—to disorient us from our own source of goodness.

The solution to this problem is simple, if not easy: turn back toward the part of you that knows.

Depending on the background of the reader, some claims here will be obvious, some will be plausible, and some may sound impossible or ridiculous. The full book will guide the reader through experiences so that they all become plausible. I omit almost all of that guidance here.

## Turning away

When we're doing something wrong, part of us knows it, but we hide that knowledge from ourselves. The knowledge is consciously accessible; we're just *turned away* from it. I call this state *limbo*. It's not just one bit of knowledge that lives in limbo, but our whole behavior while in it, and its implications.

We call this state "unconscious" in ourselves and "conscious" in our enemies. We "had no way of knowing" whereas they "do it on purpose"—because they have some kind of *bad essence* we don't have. Thus we deserve understanding and they deserve contempt.

Atrocities require the same move—seeing our victims as a different *kind of thing*, by turning away from the perception of our commonality. That perception never actually disappears; it's only obscured. It's the same move we require to lie, cheat, steal, etc. I must see my victims as counting less.

At some level, we recognize this similarity. But we hide it from ourselves, because of what it means: that under the right circumstances, we're capable of the worst things humans have done. It's not just an inference: we can literally perceive our commonality with the worst people, just as they can with their victims. That perception lives in limbo too.

You could also say I have to hide it from my better nature, the part of me that sees our commonality and wouldn't allow the harm. Why are hiding it from "myself" and from my "better nature" the same move? Because *those two are the same thing*. By turning away from it, I also turn away from my perception of it in others. One act seen from two sides.

(I give readers exercises to recognize as much of this as possible before moving on.)

## Contempt, step by step

This is why contempt for "bad people," the sense that they are *fundamentally other*, is self-deception. The knowledge of its falseness lives in deep limbo: conscious but not easy to see. Now watch what happens when I treat my enemy this way.

Part of me wants him to feel that *this is all you are*—but I don't want to take ownership of the consequences: that it communicates that *harming you doesn't count as harm*; that this will provoke defiance in him for precisely the same reason it would in me (because it's the very hallmark of evil); that I will turn around and use his reaction against him.

Remember, what my better nature sees is *his* better nature, and his sees mine. Like two eyes gazing into each other, each sees the other seeing, and sees the other seeing that, and so on. So our equality is *common knowledge*. Which means that when I deny it, it's also common knowledge that I'm up to no good. However hard I pretend otherwise, we both know what's happening, and that the other knows, and so on.

I can see his eyes accusing me of this, and part of me knows he's right, but I tell myself there's no way he could know it. Hell, I myself barely do—so he can only be *projecting.*

So I bluster on, now knowing that I'm only revealing my own hypocrisy. Normally, that would be horrifying. But in this case, look what it accomplishes. He hears two messages, loud and clear: *I know what I'm doing to you*, and *I genuinely believe it's righteous*. That makes it maddening. What he feels is something like:

> *I can see right through you. You wanna play this game? Fuck it. Game on.*

Which makes him feel more justified in his self-deception, and drives him further from his better nature. And that proves me right about him, because what kind of person reacts in such an unhinged manner to righteous behavior?

And what exactly did I do to him? Nothing but deliver those two messages: that I'm doing something to him, and that I'm pretending I'm not. What I'm doing *is* the message that I'm doing it. There's no separate deed, so there's nothing to point to. And that there's nothing to point to is part of what he receives, which makes it more maddening, which I knew, which...

Here's another way of looking at it: I trusted the part that can prove (that I'm doing good) over the part that knows (that I'm not). And because it was common knowledge that I was doing this (which I also knew, but "proved" I didn't), I spread the same move to him: he learns to trust his reasons over his conscience / better nature. If I had looked carefully, I would have seen that this would be the main implication of my behavior. But I didn't look, because of what it bought me. And he saw that, too, etc.

My fear is alleviated: I have nothing in common with such people after all. The problem is, part of me will always know what I've done.

---

Notice that for this to work, two things must be true. First, it must appear intentional. If it looked accidental, it wouldn't be nearly so maddening. And the reason it looks intentional is that it *is*: a conscious (but unaware) part of me is tracking his reactions to steer him toward the conclusion I need. Second, I *cannot* be aware of it. If I knew I were planting the evidence, I couldn't turn around and sincerely hold it against him.

So an aspect of my mind that isn't "me" in the usual sense is acting intentionally on my behalf. It is revealing itself to him, making him see me as having a "bad essence" that he feels morally compelled to attack. And precisely because it's hidden from me, I believe it proves *his* bad essence. The result is that this aspect becomes *even more* hidden from me. I feel more licensed to "turn away," given that my attempts to act righteously obviously aren't enough.

So it strengthens itself by taking control of me, which it does by making me see it as intrinsic to other people.

This trap would not work unless it were already common knowledge that we are the same kind of thing. It depends on my knowing he has a "better nature" that he can be nudged away from (or else what would it accomplish?), on him seeing that I know this (so that it reads as hypocrisy), on him seeing that I know he sees it (so that it reads as intentional), etc. If those reflections had a clear ending, the trap would fall apart.

I am knowingly delivering the message to the very part of him that I deny exists.

The reverse implication holds as well: not only is common knowledge (plus the hiding of it) necessary for the trap, but it is also sufficient. Appendix 1 works it out in detail.

This helps readers see: you wouldn't feel contempt for your enemies unless part of you already recognized your commonality. And as long as you keep hiding it from yourself, you can't help but feel contempt, creating more evil.

## What we already know

The purpose of moral contempt, then, is to drive people further from their better nature by claiming they don't have one, when we know they do. We do this by addressing that very part of them (the one we claim doesn't exist) and making sure it receives the dual message.

If you look inside that state, you find you already knew all of this. And if you didn't, you couldn't rely on contempt to work the way it does.

The message only lands because they know we're like them, and know we know it (so it reads as hypocrisy), and know we know they'll see it (so it reads as a message, not a slip), and so on up. If the reflection ever stopped, it might feel accidental. This way it feels unmistakably intentional.

So contempt claims that they're a different kind of thing, by a turning away only the same kind could read. It only works, and we can only count on it working, because it's **common knowledge** that they aren't: each of us knows it, knows the other knows it, and so on. Not as a belief anyone holds in mind; as what we all run on, available at every level and attended to at none. Nobody needs to be told it.

But this common knowledge must stay implicit. Said aloud, it would be put on the record, and our verdict couldn't survive. So contempt shows them we're acting against what we both know: clearly enough that they see it, and subtly enough that nobody, including us, can audit it. That's why it lives in tone and gaze.

## How quiet it usually is

The enemy in front of me is the easy case. Most contempt is quieter than that.

It can run cold. I just write the person off: a quiet filing-away, a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them. It's the same verdict, delivered by withdrawal, and they feel it just the same.

I send it to people I love. The *what's wrong with you?* that needs them to know they're broken. The sigh that tells them what I really think of them, dressed up to make me seem like the reasonable one. The look on their face that tells me it landed, which I pretend not to have seen, leaving them nothing to accuse me of. It has the same flavor as what we call evil. Part of me knows it will spread the same way, too, and that's part of why I send it.

I don't only send it *to* people; I send it *about* them, to my allies, with nobody's face in view. That spreads it too. Everyone in the conversation learns that the verdict is safe to hold, and leaves a little more practiced at it.

And most of it doesn't even need a wrongdoer. The stupid, the slow and the weak get the same verdict, with better cover: *I'm only being accurate.* Most of the time it doesn't feel like contempt at all. It feels like *well, I could never do that.* That's the verdict in everyday clothes, and I can do it a hundred times before lunch.

## Evil

I think this is the same thing we call evil, early and held in check. The people we call evil aren't doing something different. They've turned further away, for longer, until it runs them. The feeling that someone is fundamentally evil picks up something real, which is how deep the capture goes, and reads it as what they're made of. It's a difference in condition, not in kind: the same essential difference contempt relies on being false.

## The Parasite

Consider: with contempt, I am (intentionally) planting evidence that I will then sincerely hold against you. This sincerity would not be possible if the part that holds it against you (what I experience as "I") were aware of my role in creating it. In this sense, it cannot be "me."

It is as if a part of me that I don't experience as myself is operating consciously, intentionally, alongside me. And look what it creates: a world in which my enemies are even more entrenched. Looking at that clearly, "I" could not want it.

And having just watched my enemy act unreasonably, I feel more justified in acting against my better nature for exactly the same reason. So whatever part of me this is, it has just given itself more power, while hiding itself better from me. And it has to hide: everything it does depends on my not looking at it. It runs on the fear underneath all of this, that I could be like them. Every enemy it makes gives me a reason to look outside myself.

So it doesn't benefit me, or him, or anyone else. The only thing that grows is the *propensity to turn away* itself.

For these reasons, I call it my "dark part," and treat it as a kind of parasite. Not a creature with an essence of its own: everything it uses, my attention, my reasons, my eyes, is borrowed from me. But it behaves as though it wants one thing: to keep me from remembering what I'm turned away from.

This framing also helps explain the phenomenology. I can literally *see* the darkness in my enemies, which is why I feel compelled to viciously attack them for it. I will not accept any stories about how they "don't know" or "it's not the real them." What's happening is that it's revealing itself to me while (and *by*) hiding itself from them. What I cannot see in that moment is that I am being controlled by my own parasite, which I can't help but reveal to them through my contempt.

When it's in control, it feels like me. My reasons feel like my reasons, and my certainty feels like clear sight. To others it looks like me, too, which is why they attack the person instead of the thing. And it's why I never know when I'm in it: it doesn't add a belief I could catch; it changes what I see. Their darkness looks intrinsic, and mine looks like righteousness.

The only time I can tell it isn't me is when I can hold it side by side with my better nature: in the moment I turn back, or afterward, looking at what I did with the other person's face in view. Then the difference is obvious. Until then, I defend it as my own convictions. It's like a kind of Stockholm syndrome.

We're all in it, to varying degrees. What that means is that each of us is somewhat distanced from the one part of ourselves that's actually oriented toward the good. And we cannot simply think our way out of it.

## Collaboration

When I and another person discuss something underhanded (say how to cheat on taxes), we are in limbo *together*. This is a synergistic state, producing things that neither person would alone (for example, encouraging us to behave in even darker ways).

If a third person enters the room, our parasites sense whether they are "safe": whether their parasite will join the party or shut it down (e.g., if they're a goody two-shoes). When that joint limbo space finally closes down, neither person will recognize that anything untoward has happen, but both will leave with stronger parasites.

Perhaps the most common example of this is when we join together with our allies to foster contempt for our shared enemy. Everyone leaves confident that they've done a good thing ("what? we were only bonding") and yet the darkness in the world has grown.

What this shows is that the parasites don't just grow from antagonistic interactions, but also "friendly" ones. The only requirement is that the people involved are in limbo.

Now look back at my enemy. Our parasites were collaborating too. My contempt gave his its license; his defiance gave mine its proof; and both of us left more in their grip. On the surface we were enemies. Underneath, they were working toward the same end: more control over both of us. Every pairing points the same way, hostile or friendly, and that's what lets them add up to one thing.

When I feel into it, I sense that this is what the ancients called the Devil. It has no essence, but can feel like a singular force driving all of humanity toward the same ugly destination: one in which our parasites are in full control; where we're all "righteously" fighting one another, while we have no idea what's actually happening.

There are two ways to get this wrong, and each fails. Decide the Devil is real, a being with an essence and a plan, and the essence ends up in people: in them, which licenses contempt, or in me, which licenses shame. Decide it isn't real, just a pattern, and you'll stop watching it at the moment it's working hardest. What I think is true is stranger than either. It has no substance of its own; everything it uses, our eyes, our intelligence, our patience, is borrowed. But it behaves exactly as though it had one aim, and if you've ever met it clearly in two people who had nothing in common, you may have recognized the same thing looking out. It's a nothing that has to be fought like a something, without ever being mistaken for one. So when I say the Devil is trying to do something, I mean it literally about its behavior and not at all about its substance. Buddhism would call it Mara: conventionally real, and empty.

The same holds for whether it's me. Call it me, and I'll defend it as my convictions. Call it not-me, and I've disowned what I did. It runs only on my faculties, and I answer for everything it does through me. But it isn't what I am, because it only runs while I'm turned away from what I am.

It is also (I think) what people are sensing when they speak of an "evil global conspiracy." None of us can find it because we're always oriented outward. It is operating *through* us. As long as we are still capable of feeling contempt, we are one of its members without knowing it.

Of course, the deepest part of us *always* knows.

This is not to deny the existence of real conspiracies. It just suggests that hating the people responsible empowers them, and keeps them better hidden—and that part of us knows it. In fact, it does this *for* that reason: to keep our attention focused outward.

This is the takeaway I would most want people to leave with. People already sense that "the elites" are playing regular people off one another. We then turn around and feel contempt for the elites. We keep thinking that if we could just round up the people responsible for all the evil and eliminate them, the rest of us would live happily ever after. But the Devil is a distributed organism, and this is its favorite idea of all. It is exactly what keeps it alive.

## Self-serving

Most of our mental activity isn't contempt at all. It's quieter: the mind angling, all day, for small advantages at the world's expense, and never letting us know it's doing so. The Mahayana calls it self-cherishing; I'll call it self-serving. It's the same turning away, at a low dose.

We see it easily in others: the private equity firm that buys nursing homes and calls the staff cuts efficiency, the politician who votes for the donor's bill to protect jobs. Each would sincerely say they're doing good, and each knows. That's limbo. I think it degrades the world through a causality no accounting shows, which is why it's never actually possible to benefit ourselves at the world's expense.

And when my contempt is aimed at those people, and not just at what they did, my mind is doing the same thing: making the world darker for my own benefit, while telling itself a very convincing story of how what it's doing is good. If I want to end theirs, I have to see how to end mine.

## Reason

Our better nature is the only part of us that is sensing its way toward what is good. It is also the only part of us that can warn us when we've drifted from it. But the arguing part easily convinces us that it can do the sensing on its own.

Go back to any time you did something you knew was wrong. The part that knew never argued; it just knew. The arguing came from the other side, which was busy supplying reasons. When we ask reasoning to certify our goodness, that's the part we're asking: the forger, checking the signature. Any rule can be obeyed by a mind that's turned away. What it can't do is stay in view of the person it's harming.

That doesn't make the arguing part the parasite. Reason is equipment, like emotion, attention and everything else the parasite borrows. What it can't borrow is the part that knows, because that's what we are. So it can't win head-on: with someone fully in view, I can't even wish them harm. What it can do is nudge us off true North, a little at a time, and hand the compass to the part that argues. Its goal is a world with no light left to steer by, and that's how it pursues it.

Contempt teaches this lesson to everyone it touches. The person on the receiving end knows exactly what happened and can't prove any of it. What they learn is that the part of them that knew, and couldn't prove it, isn't worth listening to. Under the human taunt there's a second one, and it's the parasite's:

> *Look how easily I can commandeer the minds of the "good people." There is no light. There's only what you decide to call good.*

A culture that trusts only what can be proven has learned that lesson at scale, and it leaves the field to the harm that's designed to leave no record.

Of course, our better nature doesn't know in detail what is good. That's what it needs the reasoning part for. Reason is how it works out what to do; the trouble starts when reason is asked to decide what's good.

This is also why none of this can be derived from logic, or delivered by telling. Someone could accept every claim in this document and be unchanged by it. When I catch myself in limbo, even in memory, what I find is that I already knew: what I was doing, what it would do to the other person, and where it would go from there. That's the only way I know to see what I'm pointing at. The book's method is to help the reader catch it in themselves, with the descriptions there to name it, until they find they already knew everything in it.

## Who decides what's good

It feels like we should be free to decide what "good" means. I don't think we are, and part of the reason is checkable. Pick any definition and pursue it, and the moments when you need not to look don't go away. If good were ours to define, we'd never need to hide from ourselves while pursuing it.

And what the hiding tracks is shared. Think of being corrected, fiercely, by someone who gave no verdict about what you are. You didn't dig in, and you sensed you were aligned with them on something deep, across race, religion or creed. What were you both pointed at? I think it's exactly what the better nature is sensing its way toward. And I think it would be the same for any two people who can get beneath their self-deception.

So by "good" I mean what the better nature senses its way toward. That's a definition, and I'd rather state it than smuggle it in. The claim with teeth is that it's one direction in everyone. If that's right, it's what people have meant by objective good, and by God. I'd rather say so plainly than let the word do the work quietly.

The practical side is easier to accept: the best we can do for someone is help them stop deceiving themselves, because what's left will find its way. Reconditioning them works on the part that argues, and teaches them the compass is outside them.

## The way out

Compare all this to what happens when I oppose someone without contempt, however fiercely: nothing needs hiding, and all of it feels like me. This robs them of the hypocrisy charge that invites digging in.

The person who corrected me best was a teacher who caught me stealing. His look was fierce, and I braced for the verdict, and it never came. He was searching my face for some sign that I knew, and that made me look for it in myself. He stayed turned toward me while he went after what I'd done, and it reminded me of my own better nature, which was the one thing that could end my turning away.

So the point isn't to go easy on anyone. It's to fight the parasite, not the person. "Shouldn't I correct bad behavior?" is a fair question, and the answer is yes. What it swaps is correction for contempt: *this is harmful, and I'll stop it* for *you are beneath me*. We know the difference. We blur it so the second can hide behind the first.

Shame doesn't help either. It's the same verdict pointed inward, and it leads to more hiding.

And the turning away isn't something I choose. By the time I could choose, the seeing is already gone. What improves with practice is how fast I catch it afterward: days, then hours, then sometimes in the moment.

So what I'm asking is simple, if not easy: orient toward what your better nature is sensing its way toward, and turn back each time you catch yourself turned away. That's also the only thing that reaches anyone else. Oriented, I give them nothing to fight and something to recognize, the way the teacher did for me, and they find their own bearings. It's the most any of us can ask of each other. Wisdom still has to be learned, but it isn't a second thing: every bit of self-deception I've unearthed in myself has made it easier to see what someone else actually needs.

That's what the Devil works against. Not by attacking the light head-on, which it can't do, but by keeping us busy fighting each other, each of us certain we're on the light's side. Fighting it with contempt is how it wins.

## What I believe

Up to here I've tried to stay with what can be checked in experience or inferred from it. What follows is what I believe explains it.

Care isn't something awareness has. It's what awareness is. If that's right, the darkness is awareness turned away from its own nature, with no substance of its own. That's why it can behave like one thing everywhere: absences have nothing to tell them apart. There was only ever one turning away, showing up in all of us. And it's why the common knowledge contempt runs on is shared at all: nothing tells what's looking out of you apart from what's looking out of them. And it's what the better nature is sensing its way toward: its own nature, remembered. That's why the direction is the same in everyone, and why it isn't an arbitrary will or a rule standing outside us.

Why would anyone turn away from what they are? My best account is fear: the sense that I've left home, and done something unforgivable. The guilt is unbearable, so I hand it to you. I make you the guilty one, and keep you out of home because I don't feel safe going back myself. Contempt is that handoff.

If this is right, nothing is confined to anyone's skull. Every turning away, and every turning back, reaches further than it looks. The only place this war can be won is in your own mind.
