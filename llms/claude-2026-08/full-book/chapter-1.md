# Chapter 1: Limbo

[TODO: Doing wrong always causes the limbo state.]

Contempt is the claim that you're a lesser kind of thing than me. But it only works because you're the same kind of thing, and both of us know it. I want to show you why.

I'll make some claims before I've earned them. Hold them the way you'd hold puzzle pieces you haven't placed, and if they don't fit what you find in yourself, drop them.

---
## Limbo

Have you ever done something you knew was wrong at the time, but couldn't fully admit it to yourself until later? Maybe you took credit for someone else's work, said something cruel to someone who'd done nothing to you, or cheated on a test (or even a partner).

Recall what it was like to finally own up to it. The knowledge that it was wrong: did it feel like new information? Or like something you had always known, but somehow hadn't *let* yourself know?

We like to call such knowledge "unconscious," but this wasn't. It was right there to see—fully available to consciousness—you were just *turned away* from it. It lived in a bizarre kind of limbo, in which you simultaneously knew and yet somehow didn't.

Any time someone came close to exposing it, or you started getting close to it yourself, did you find a way to deflect? Did that happen passively? Looking back, did some part of you know you were doing that, even as you did it? It did in me. So intentional behavior can take place in limbo, too. When we're in limbo, we both know it and we don't.

Now try to recall how you knew it was wrong in the first place. Did that knowledge arrive before or after you'd reasoned it through? The part that knew might not have been able to say how it knew. Meanwhile, another part was busy providing reasons. *Everyone does it. It's not a big deal. Just this once.*

The part that knew may have lost the battle, but it never budged an inch. It kept on quietly knowing what it knew.

Why did one side have to argue and the other not?

---

Could you do wrong in full view of your conscience—no shielding, no justifications? I find I can't. It's not just what tells me that I've done wrong; it's what warns me when I'm about to. Ignoring it is what puts me in limbo.

When I realize I've caused harm, I feel remorse. But I've never felt it over something I did out of decency. What about you? I've felt regret that it cost me, anger at being taken advantage of, and guilt at breaking a rule I was raised on—but never *remorse* for those things, that sickening sense of having gone against myself. Betraying my conscience feel like going against *myself*. Why?

Can you imagine the reverse in anyone: a bad person who does secret acts of kindness, and then lies awake at night, wracked with remorse for having gone against his evil nature?

Conscience seems to point in only one direction: away from harm. Maybe some can't hear theirs—even to the point of seeming like they don't have one—but it never seems to run in reverse.

If, in anything that follows, you catch yourself thinking *I'm a terrible person*, hold off on that judgement. It turns out to be one more instance of the thing we're studying. It's no truer of you than of anyone else, and no more useful.

---
## The Two Strangers

Go back to your example above. Imagine that while you were in the limbo state—doing wrong, justifying it, half-knowing—a stranger had caught you and publicly chastised you for it. Not just for doing something wrong, but for *being the kind of person who does*.

You've probably been in this kind of situation before. Really try and visualize it.

Does his behavior make you want to come clean, own up to what you know, and make honest amends? Or does it provoke defensiveness and a desire to hide more—even if it stops your behavior in the moment, or even forces you to make things right?

For me it's the latter. I feel something like *Who the hell is he to judge? He's clearly the kind of person who does this, too—things he knows are wrong, while not admitting it to himself.* Have you ever felt that?

Where does that impression come from? Is it just projection, or might you be sensing something real—and if so, how? You'd never met him. You know nothing about him. All you have to go on is that he's standing there, doing what he's doing.

---

Now imagine a different stranger had caught you—but instead of treating you with contempt, he treated you with dignity. Not more gently. Just that his anger was directed at what you *did*, and not what you *are*.

Would you have dug in? Vowed revenge?

The problem wasn't being caught. In fact, he could have been *harsher* than the first person—said things that stung more, in front of more people, left you less room to wriggle out—and it still wouldn't have made you dig in the way the first one did. You probably wouldn't find yourself obsessing over his flaws.

It's like he was reminding you of something: that *you're better than this*. That's the opposite of what the first stranger did: claiming that *this is all you are*. [TODO: forced you to confront yourself.]

You might have disagreed with him on nearly everything—politics, religion, creed—and it wouldn't have mattered. Whatever he was standing up for wasn't *his*. It wasn't a rule, or a side. It was something you were both already on the side of, and he was only reminding you.

I had someone like this once. A teacher who caught me stealing. His look was fierce, and I braced for the verdict, and it never came. He was standing for something, and searching my face for some sign that I knew it too. That made me look for it in myself. I knew I couldn't win against it—and more importantly, I didn't want to.

What is that thing that two people who agree on nothing can both be serving?

---
## The Encounter

I've been the first stranger more times than I'd like to admit. I'll narrate what happens in my mind when I am. My target has just been cruel to someone who didn't deserve it. The threat is over, and now I'm confronting him.

I've decided he's a *bad person*—not just someone who does bad things, but who *is* bad. My eyes are saying: *I'm a better kind of thing than you.* And I want him to feel the full weight of that judgment, almost as a kind of punishment.

Part of me senses something dark in my message. If I were on the receiving end, I'd recognize it instantly: *this is all you are* carries an implicit threat: *and therefore your wellbeing has ceased to matter. What I do to you no longer qualifies as harm.*

I would defy a message like that too, and violently.

But if I admitted that, it would undermine the verdict I feel strangely attached to: he's **bad**, dammit. So I push it out of mind. I'm only holding him to account, and if he reacts poorly, that only proves my point. It's not like I'm actually threatening him. If anything it's an *invitation*, to return to the realm of those who matter!

But there's nothing inviting about my tone or gaze. How could there be, when I'm trying to convince him that *this is all he is*?

And yet he can't prove I'm doing anything wrong, can he? No. And *neither can I*. So if he accuses me of it, it can only mean one thing: he's projecting.

But there he goes, accusing me of it! Not in words, but through his eyes. It's as if he's saying: 

> *Don't pretend what you're doing is noble. We both know what you're really up to.*

*Shit.* He's onto me. But how?

I have no option but to press forward. I don't even want to believe I have this kind of darkness in me—that I would forsake a fellow being just to prove I'm nothing like him. So I push it out of mind harder.

He's *bad*, dammit, and I'm just proving it. Sure, the way I'm going about it may be a bit underhanded, but it's for the greater good. It's the *good* kind of darkness—the kind that all good people must harbor, to fight the real kind!

Yet for some reason, this only makes him dig in harder...

---

Part of me knew the whole time that I could have done what the second stranger did: served justice without adding that "little something extra." But when I look back on it later, I won't realize what I've done. My enemy will have become a slightly worse person, and that will validate the story I started with: that he was beyond help to begin with.

I tell myself I'm just exposing the evil in my enemies. Part of me knows I'm secretly reinforcing it.

---

What he felt was probably similar to how I felt with the first stranger:

> *What a hypocrite. If this is the kind of world we live in—where people can act in dark ways, call it "righteous," and get away with it—then why the fuck shouldn't I? I'm not going to be the lone chump.*

Think back to the wrong you remembered at the start. Did some part of you feel you *deserved* something? That everyone does it, and only a fool plays it straight?

We never tell ourselves "I'm going to ignore my conscience." Our justifications simply feel more convincing, more righteous, as though they *are* our conscience. *I mean, who can really say whether hating my enemy is good, as long as it works?*

I sense his hardening, and feel a brief flicker of satisfaction, vindication, even relief at being proven right about him—just before I relabel it disgust or dismay:

> *Ugh, I knew it. People are even worse than I thought.*

If I'd looked carefully, I'd have seen my role in causing it. So I didn't—and that *was* my role in causing it.

Any doubt I had about him is now erased, so I'll feel more emboldened to trust my own justifications. Who cares about my own minor misdeeds when there's *real* evil out there to fight?

The corruption I produced in him: it's happening in me, too.

---

Bring to mind your worst enemy. If the one who comes to mind is someone who abused you, pick someone else for this; that face can wait, or never come.

Say you had two buttons in front of you. One delivers them the vengeance they so richly deserve. The other gives them a pill that instantly changes their heart. Imagine no one will ever know you were given this choice, let alone what you chose. Is some part of you *itching* to press that first button? I'll be honest: there is in me.

Now think of the last time you watched someone you despise get what was coming to him. Was the pleasure only in the harm stopping, or also in the punishment itself?

Something in me finds it primally, viscerally satisfying to make bad people suffer. I don't want to admit that urge exists in me, but it's there. I tell myself it's just part of being human. And it's not like I'll ever actually act on it. it's confined to my skull, so what harm could it possibly cause?

And that lie is exactly what lets it leak out my skull, through my tone and gaze, provoking them into becoming more of what I need them to be.

---
## The Need

The reason I don't have to argue myself into doing the right thing is that in the absence of fears or justifications, it's what I naturally desire. Check if that's true of you, too. 

Earlier I asked why going against my conscience should feel like going against *myself*. This is why. In a sense, it's the deepest part of me; what's there in the absence of self-deception. We can also call it my *better nature*.

It needs no rules; it's the part of me that sees myself in others. And when I can see myself in them, it's much harder to harm or use them for my own ends. That's why I must *turn away* from it to cause harm.

Part of what it sees in others is that they, too, can only cause harm by *turning away*. But when I'm turned away myself, I can no longer see this. Other people look defective, as if they were just bad. Harming them then feels righteous. But the deeper perception of our similarity never truly disappears. It only goes into limbo, so that it *feels* like I can't see it.

Have you ever been on the receiving end of a dehumanizing gaze? Could you feel the sense in which the other person was somehow absent or turned away from themselves? What were they turned away from?

But part of me refuses to believe I'm anything like the *bad people*. I need their behavior to come from something I don't have: some alien quality called "evil" that I could never hope to understand, only valiantly fight. This doesn't feel like a need, but the truth: I can literally *see* the darkness in their eyes, and it's just what they *are*. What I don't see is that I can only believe this while turned away myself—and that in that state, I look like that too.

Except that part of me *does* know I look like that. I just don't *let* myself know, because of what it helps me accomplish.

My enemy sees I'm turned away, even though I don't. *Because* I don't. And he sees it as *evil*, for the same reason I see him that way. I'm showing him, through my tone and my gaze, that the darkness I see in him lives in me too, and that I can use it against him and get away with it. It doesn't look like an accident to him—because in a sense, it isn't. 

Notice how my stories kept changing, but my conclusion never did. When he saw through one, the next was ready. When he called me on it I wasn't embarrassed or confused, as I would have been if it were truly unconscious. Some part of me was tracking his reactions and steering around them, the same way yours did, if you deflected when someone came close to revealing it. My behavior took place in *limbo*—intentional and consciously accessible, but disowned.

If you've been on his end, you know how it closes around you. Deny it, and you're the kind of person who does. Get angry, and that's proof too. Stay calm, and you don't even care. Apologize, and it's too convenient. Plain malice might frighten you. This maddens, because it's malice that won't admit what it is.

Notice how limbo is required for this to work. I have to be present enough to plant the evidence of his irredeemability, and absent enough not to recognize it as planted. By doing so, I nudge him one step closer to being the kind of person who can only be dealt with in *one way*—a way that part of me secretly craves.

This is how my need to be different and special gets itself met. By revealing itself to my enemies while hiding itself from me, it turns them into what it needs them to be while letting me feel righteous for it. Part of me knows that this self-deception is the same basic trick they use to do wrong, but I prevent myself from seeing it—because of what it might imply about me if I were to see it fully.

I desperately want to call this behavior _unconscious_ (if I can admit to it at all). Only *bad people* knowingly cause harm. If I've ever done it, it was an honest mistake, or I didn't really know. That's why I deserve understanding, and they deserve contempt.

---
## The Mechanism

Now look at what this trap depended on.

Part of me had to recognize our similarity: that he had a better nature I could push him away from; that he would recognize my tone and gaze as me being turned away from mine; and that this would license him to depart from his, which I could use against him because I was turned away and refusing to see it.

It's as if I'm saying to him:

> *I know exactly how you cause harm, because I'm making you watch me do it to you **right now**. And we both know I'll get away with it—because I'm only "fighting evil," and how could that ever be wrong?*

It's like a silent communication taking place *beneath* the words—one we both know is happening, but that neither of us can prove. He can't name it without sounding unhinged, because it's plausibly deniable, *even to myself.* There is no specific wrong to point at: the look is the deed.

How can a look carry all that information? 

It only works if we both know we're the same kind of thing—beings who must *turn away* to cause harm. I recognize it in him; he recognizes me recognizing it in him; I recognize that too, and so on. It's like a hall of mirrors.

All I have to do is evoke this shared knowledge, which is why it can be carried entirely by tone, gaze, and manner—which have the benefit of being unquotable. My claim that I'm a *better kind of thing* is maddening precisely because we can both see it's false. It's what we felt in the second stranger, and why we felt so certain he does it too: part of him *wanted* us to.

Do I know I'm doing this? Yes and no. I am in limbo about provoking him, and revealing that I am in limbo is itself the provocation. (If the recursion tickles you, see Appendix 1.)

What does our sameness mean? Not that we agree about what's good: we don't. And not that we're equally culpable: we're not. It's that we both know how to *orient* toward goodness, and that turning away means we've departed from it.

Notice how I never think, *he's turned away from his better nature, that must be good.* Whatever he does while turned toward it—as the second stranger was—I *do* trust to be aimed at the good, even when it misses. So my enemy doesn't have to read my thoughts to know I'm being deceitful. He just has to see that I am listening to them instead of to my better nature. I don't even have to reveal it: that state reveals *itself*.

Underneath the argument, we were never fighting over whether a shared ground exists. We were fighting over which of us still stood on it.

---

Now look at where my message was aimed.

Recall the last time someone's contempt really got under your skin, and feel for *where* it was trying to reach. Not just your pride: an insult to your pride is easier to shrug off. This reaches further in, for the *you* that knows you count: the same you that you'd have to turn away from to harm someone, and the same you the second stranger was speaking to.

**Love and contempt are addressed to the same place.** One says *I know you're in there,* the other says *there's nobody in there*, and they both say it to the somebody who is.

Not everyone defies it. A rival refuses it, and the refusal hardens him. A child can't refuse it; he believes it, and carries it for years. It reaches the same place in both.

So I am speaking directly to the part of my enemy that I claim doesn't exist—his better nature—and letting him know that I can see it, and that I'm free to smother it without compunction.

I've laid this all out as an argument, but for me it wasn't one. I saw it in myself the way you found your wrong in the opening: as something I had always known, but hadn't let myself know. For me, it was revealed in layers—levels of increasingly subtle limbo.

On the surface, it felt entirely righteous: I was just holding a bad person to account. Underneath was something less noble: I was wielding my own darkness to do so—but at least it was still "for the greater good." Under that, I recognized it as good old-fashioned vengeance: I wanted him to *suffer,* but in a way that nobody could blame me for—not even myself.

And then, finally, the truth: I needed others to carry the darkness I hid from myself, and he was an ideal victim because of what he had done.

I was knowingly smothering his innocent part—a part I *knew* could be rescued—and I felt a rush of satisfaction when I got away with it.

It's hard to describe the crushing pain of seeing this. The state I'd been in felt *malevolent*. In a flash, I saw how people could commit genuine evil and feel fine about it, just by hiding the weight of it from themselves. But worse, I saw that I had always known this, and had been using it to my advantage, in exactly the same way as the people I hated. That's what I had been showing him: *the darkness that lives in you lives in me, too—and I can use mine against you while getting away with it.*

No wonder he had dug in.

There's another half that's much harder to describe, because it can sound like an excuse. In a sense, my state had been utterly innocent. Not as in blameless. But I hadn't turned away out of malice, or because I'm a bad person. I had done it out of fear; out of an innocent desire to know myself as good. I had lost contact with the aspect of myself that *is* good—my better nature—and in that profound confusion, was willing to sacrifice another being, to prove to myself and the world that I was still in touch with it. It is that same sense of "innocence" I was smothering in him.

This is what I find hardest to hold. Evil doesn't have to originate from some kind of *bad essence*. It only requires a certain kind of confusion. What begins as fear or pain deep in our minds can emerge as malevolence. When I recognize it in another person', a deep part of me *knows* what's happening in them, and that I can be of service in ending it, even if I don't know how. But another part sees it as a golden opportunity to punish them and get away with it, as a clever way to hide my own darkness in them.

Even though it was conscious, I doubt I would have been able to really *see* it in that moment, even if someone had been kind enough to point it out. I find that the darkest parts of myself require tremendous care to be brought into the light of full awareness. How that became possible for me, I'll describe later.

The underlying innocence in no way excuses my behavior. I'm still fully responsible for the harm I've caused while turned away from it, and it's my duty to stop. Nor is it your duty to be *nice* to me if I do it again. But it does reveal another way of dealing with evil. It has to do with never losing contact with the deepest part of myself. Only by doing that can I recognize the darker aspect of my mind for what it is: a lie.

This, I think, is how I gain the wisdom and courage to act as my teacher had done for me, and as the wisest people are able to do in the face of far greater evils: by staying true to the part of myself that *knows*. It's the only way I can stop mindlessly spreading the very evil I've been so sure I'm fighting.

---
## The Quiet Kind

What I narrated was the hot kind of contempt, but it often runs cold. I just write the person off: a quiet filing away, a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them. It's the same verdict, delivered by withdrawal, and it's meant to be felt just the same: by them, or by whoever's watching. Everyone who witnesses it is being taught the same lesson.

And it needn't be about right and wrong. Someone slow, ignorant, or clumsy can get the same verdict—*you're a lesser kind of thing*—and I write it off as "just being honest."

Most of my contempt is quiet: a vague sense of *well, I could never do that*. It doesn't feel like contempt at all, and it can happen a thousand times a day without my noticing. I even send it to people I love. The *what's wrong with you?* that needs them to know they're broken. The sigh that tells them what I really think of them, dressed up to make me seem like the reasonable one. The look on their face that tells me it landed, which I pretend not to have seen. For one second the verdict flashes through my eyes, and I make sure they see it, and then I go back to loving them as if nothing had happened.

Even this book can carry it: sometimes I'm writing it *at* people, instead of *for* them.

What I need in order to look is what my enemy needed from me, and what the second stranger gave: to be seen as more than what I do. And only by looking do I have a shot at getting back in touch with that deeper part of myself.

---
## The Other Look

Have you ever been the second stranger? Think of a time you had to stop someone: a child about to hurt another child, a colleague cutting a corner, a friend being cruel about someone who wasn't there. Look for one where you had to be a little fierce.

While you did it, were you focused on what they'd done, or what they were? Was the wish to stop them still at full strength? Was anything riding on it, any need for them to come away smaller?

Now think of a time you felt the extra coming, the sigh loaded or the *of course you did* on its way out, and you didn't send it. What did you do instead, and what happened in them?

What you said went to the same place my look went in my enemy, and it said the opposite: *I know you're in there.* They were met by someone who hadn't given up on them, which is what the teacher gave me. Maybe it worked, maybe it didn't. But what it probably didn't do is give them license to embrace the part of them that felt the need to keep doing it.

---
## The Orientation

The first stranger's allies would have called what he did "good," his enemies would have called it "evil," and yet both would have recognized, on some level, the badness woven into it. What does that tell you?

It tells me there's something we serve that transcends sides.

In ourselves, we call it our *better nature.* But this raises a curious question: why should all of ours be pointed in the same direction? What direction *is* that?
