# Chapter 5: What You Are

I cannot give an account of why the first turn happens, but once it does, it becomes a self-perpetuating loop. Augustine said there was no cause to find, and still called the turn an act.

Here is what keeps the loop going. You turn away from someone because you suspect, below words, that care might *not* be what you are. If you knew it was, you'd have nothing to prove and nobody to prove it against. The suspicion is what makes goodness feel like something you have to earn, and you can only be good *relative* to someone worse. So as long as you don't feel at home, you can't afford to let anyone else get there. You're *forced* to keep them out, because if they arrived you'd lose the only proof you had.

I think we're all trying to find our way home, not realizing that this cannot be done at another's expense. That closes the loop. The suspicion makes us turn away; turning away is the one thing that would confirm the suspicion, if we looked; so we don't look, and we go on needing bad people. Under the suspicion is fear: fear of what you might be, which in public looks like pride.

That's what makes it possible to stay turned toward someone at their worst: under the contempt there is someone afraid, and you know that state from the inside.

---

Go back to the memory from Chapter 1, to the knowledge you hadn't *let yourself* know. Look at the grammar: you hid it from *yourself*, not from some part of you. So what was it hidden from? When you finally owned up, did it feel like losing something of yourself, or like getting something back? And if you could keep only one of the two parts—the one that knew without arguing, or the one that justified—which would you keep?

What you hid it from is what you call "I" when nothing is being hidden. I've called my better nature a part until now, but it's what's left when the hiding stops. I'm not claiming there's a thing in there you could point to. Only that this is what the hiding was from, and what my contempt was aimed at in him. I never had to produce the care. It was there until I turned from it, and the turning was all I did.

---

Bring back the stranger's face, and put the face of someone you love in its place. Then the stranger again. Then an animal you know, and then one you don't. Notice whether the recognition has an edge, a place where it stops and what's in front of you turns into stuff. For me it doesn't stop at people. It only gets fainter.

Here's a check that needs no metaphysics. Somebody cuts down a tree that has stood for a thousand years, for fun. Somebody takes a hammer to a stone arch that took ten thousand years to form, for the pleasure of watching it fall. Notice what you feel, and whether it arrives before any story about who was deprived. The tree has no conscience and the arch has no inside. Whatever you're registering, see whether it lands in the place where the violation of a person lands. If it doesn't, take what follows as my report.

When I look most deeply into what I am, I find life. Not a thing I have; what's doing the looking. Whatever is looking out of your eyes right now is alive, and it's the same kind of thing that looked out of the stranger's. That is what I mean by seeing myself in others, and I mean it literally. Not that he is like me. Look for what would tell the two apart: not the faces, the histories, the stories, but what's doing the looking. I can't find anything.

And, as far as I can tell, life naturally wants to serve life. That's what you felt when the stranger's wellbeing started to register without your deciding it should. Nothing was added to make that happen. It's what's there when nothing is in the way, and with nothing in the way at all, what's there is a fierce care for everything, even beyond the things I'd normally call "alive." Each thing has its place. A sidewalk is for walking on, and a lion eats the zebra. But there's a way of holding the world in which it's all just stuff, *who cares, none of it matters*, and this is the opposite of that. I'd call it sacred, except the word turns it into an idea, and we saw how that goes off the rails. What I mean is a perception: the one that arrived at the tree before any story did.

Nobody gets to that perception by merely thinking harder. Thinking can at most convince me that I *should* be oriented this way, and then I'm holding an idea again; ask almost anyone whether they're trying to serve life and they'll say of course. The orienting itself happens deeper than the intellect, and staying oriented takes a patience and honesty that no book can hand you.

It also explains how the knowledge in Chapter 1's trap could be common—not merely shared, but something each of us can count on the other already knowing. If what's looking out of us has nothing to differ by, then to know this in yourself is already to know it in them, and to know that they know it in you, and so on. If that's right, the knowledge is exactly common; if not, it's close enough for the trap to work. Either way the trap needs it. Otherwise, there would be no violation to recognize, no hypocrisy to expose, no taunt to make land. Darkness can communicate only by borrowing the light as common ground.

So nothing in the encounter belonged to a separate thing called the darkness. Everything doing anything, the sending, the hiding, the reading, the answering, is the light: one faculty, in two bodies, turned against itself in one of them. The message that denies the sameness is made of the sameness, and nothing else could receive it. Goodness is one thing too. Being turned toward, seeing yourself in others, love, goodness: all one. And since what you turn toward in yourself is what looks out of everyone, turning toward yourself and turning toward everyone are the same act. It's only the whole thing when it includes everyone. Not because partial love is fake; it's the seed. But love for one person at the expense of another, love that needs someone else to be worthless, isn't love.

---

This is why awakening another person doesn't mean teaching them something new. It means helping them stop turning away from what they were already using—even in their darkness—to understand you. Words can point, but the deepest reminder travels the way the contempt did: you stay turned toward them, and show openly what both of you already know. That's the look you gave the stranger's face, held when it's hardest to hold.

The question is whether we can oppose the darkness, fiercely when it takes that, without identifying the person with it—because that identification is how the darkness passes between us. And the only way to do that is by remaining unflinchingly *turned toward* them.

No amount of turning away ever produces a turning back. That's the other big lie: that enough contempt, aimed well enough, will make the world better. It can frighten someone into stopping, and now and then into thinking again. But when they do turn back, it's through the very awareness the contempt was attacking, not through the contempt. King said it: *darkness cannot drive out darkness*. It's not just a slogan. The most reliable thing I know of that turns anyone back is someone who stayed turned toward them while refusing what they did. That is kindness, and it can be the harshest thing in the room.
