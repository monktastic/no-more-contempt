# Chapter 2: Good and Evil

Imagine looking into a stranger's face until it stops being something you're looking at and becomes a place somebody is looking out of. Don't try to add anything on. Just stay with the face, and see what comes.

If I stay there long enough, here's what comes first for me—not a conclusion, but a recognition:

> *There's someone there. This is real from the inside, the way I am.*

Now notice what happens to their wellbeing while that stays in view. For me, it starts to register. Not necessarily as affection, but as something I can't treat as nothing. Using them for my own ends feels *wrong*, in a way I didn't have to be taught.

This is what I mean by being *turned toward* them. Notice whether it requires any stories to maintain, or if there's something natural about it.

From this place, try to wish them harm. For me it can't be done. Something has to go out of view first, or be covered over: I must *turn away* from that perception—usually by adding a story about them and what they are, even if I'm still looking them in the eye. Accounts of atrocity return again and again to the same preparation: the hood, the number instead of the name, the word for vermin.

Not everyone you pass is in view in this sense, but that doesn't make it contempt. The hundred people around you in an airport are in the background; if one of them dropped a bag and started crying, they'd come into view, and you'd feel the pull. Turning away is keeping someone out of view when they'd come forward, because their reality would otherwise place a claim on you.

Out of view doesn't mean out of sight. A torturer has his victim in plain sight, and the cruelest watch the face closely. What they've turned from isn't the person's presence. It's his claim: that the one looking out of that face counts the way they do. That's the only sense of "in view" I'll use, and it's the sense contempt was using all along, when it spoke to the one who's in there to tell him nobody is. The torturer's turn is the one I make, smaller, to feel okay cheating on a test.

Now put your enemy's face there instead (if it's someone who abused you, pick another), and try for the same recognition of sameness. If it won't come, notice what arrives in its place, and how fast. For me the story is there before the face is. That's the turning away, live.

The care that came with the face is what I'll call the *light*: what I turn from to cause harm, and what anyone is turned toward when they aren't turned away, in themselves and in everyone else. For now, that's all the word will mean.

---

So what is it that my enemy and I agree on?

Not a definition of good. We may never agree on one, and we don't need to. It's an *orientation*. The part that knew without arguing was pointed in it, the second stranger was when he pointed you back, and we instantly knew the first stranger wasn't.

If I had to name that orientation, I might call it being *turned toward all of life*. Not one person, and not my side: the second stranger wasn't exactly serving you, and he wasn't serving himself. I hesitate to name it at all, because watch what happens when I do: *Goodness means serving life. This man isn't serving it, so hating him is good.* That's what I did to my enemy. It's the orientation turned into an idea, and it failed for the simplest reason: the man in front of me was part of the life I claimed to be serving.

---

It's similarly hard to define evil.

Start with where you'd already use the word: a politician who lies his country into a war, executives who keep selling a drug they know is killing people, someone who tortures an animal for fun. What is the word tracking?

It isn't the outcome alone. The driver who hits a child who ran out between parked cars will carry it for the rest of his life, and nobody calls him evil. The driver who *tries* to hit a child and misses surely deserves the word.

The friend who spilled red wine on your fancy couch did more "objective" damage than the one who made a small cutting remark, but the latter is the one you're still thinking about a week later.

And even when there is harm, it isn't just about the size. The man who tortures a mouse hurts one tiny creature—a few grams of matter—and nobody hesitates over the word. Smaller still, watch what cutting in line does to you. The cost is thirty seconds, but the heat is out of all proportion, because what you're reacting to isn't the cost. "Evil" is too strong a word for that case, but it somehow lies on the same axis. What is it trying to capture?

Think back to your own wrong in Chapter 1. If it was cheating on a test, what did you have to do to feel okay with it? For me, I have to feel that *the rules don't apply to me.* To treat myself as worth more. I make myself an exception, the same way the politician does: other people's children can die for his ambition. The executives make one for their company, the man with the animal for his own fun, the line-cutter for his time.

But we can't simply define it as *feeling like I'm worth more* or *making myself an exception*. A human *is* worth more than a mouse, in a meaningful sense, and sometimes I must rely on this fact to do good. And when my back is hurting, I can take the last seat on the bus without it being "evil" in the slightest. Definitions fail to capture "evil," just as they do for "good."

What all the evil cases have in common is that the person wasn't truly taking others into account, in the way they do for themselves. Seeing them as *beings that matter* in their own right. In other words, they weren't really *looking.*

Here's what I find in myself. When I turn away from my better nature, I turn away from the life in others, and also from the full implications of my behavior. They're all facets of a single move. It is that move that I call *evil.* That's why the smallest evil carries a whiff of the whole: there's nothing in it but the move, and it covers vastly different scales.

The padded expense, the lie to the insurer, the joke that makes someone smaller, the slur, the order to clear a village: each needs someone held out of view so that I, or mine, can be the exception. That's why making myself an exception came so close to defining it: the exception is what the turn is for.

---

Most of us picture evil as something people have: a drive toward harm, a dark side with wants of its own, stronger in some people than in others. But I think it's fully explained by the turn. The craving is real; I felt it in Chapter 1, with the two buttons. What I doubt is that it's anything but the turn. Check it against the cases you'd already use the word for. 

The further I'm turned from you, the more acceptable (or even righteous) it feels to harm you. Recall the dehumanizing gaze check from Chapter 1, and how it felt like the other person was somehow *turned away* from themselves. Now try to picture the worst person you know of doing their worst while seeing their victim the way the second stranger saw you. I can't make the picture hold. If you can't either, then whatever drove them had to get the person out of view first.

The drive toward harm isn't like hunger. Hunger is there before I look at the bread, and doesn't have to look away from it. The craving to harm someone isn't there while he's fully in view. It arrives with the turn, and it has no more substance than the turn: it's what my wanting looks like once I've stopped looking, the way the story about him is what my thinking looks like. Decency needs none of that. That asymmetry is what it means for care to be the baseline: what needs no turn and no story is where I am when I'm not doing anything, and turning away is a departure from it, not a second baseline of its own. 

Turning away takes upkeep, at least until it's practiced enough to run by itself. A grudge has to be fed: the reasons rehearsed, the case kept open, their face kept out of view. Being turned toward someone takes nothing like that. And when a grudge finally drops, it doesn't feel like starting a new effort. It feels like setting something down. If turning away were the mind at rest, I'd expect it the other way around. So darkness is not an equal and opposite force.

Badness is the result of turning away, not its cause. When I tell myself the bad people don't have a conscience, or that they choose to turn away from it due to some "bad essence," I'm turning it back into a cause. Or I tell myself that sure, sometimes I do wrong too, but my justifications are reasonable, or at least forgivable. One way or another, something in me needs there to be an essential difference between us.

But despite having no essence of its own, evil isn't just nothing. Call it that and I'll underestimate it: I'll stop looking at exactly the moment it's crucial to look more closely. It tracks, it aims, it fights to stay hidden. It's an absence that behaves like a presence: it has no substance of its own; everything it uses—my attention, my reasons, my eyes—is borrowed from me. Every faculty is still at work, and the other person's life is no longer allowed to count the way I still know it does. When it's operating, it looks and behaves exactly like you'd expect a "bad essence" to look. It's what I saw in my enemy, and what I showed him in return.

If darkness were an essence, I'd be right to be afraid of it, and ashamed, and I'd have to keep proving it's in others and not in me. But if it's something I *do*, then I can stop.

---

This is why I say contempt is evil. It is evil's way of spreading in secret, through people who sincerely believe they're fighting it.

When I hate the animal abuser, my hatred for him is the same move he's making with his victim. By choosing not to see this, I make him *more* likely to do it, not less. The point of chapter one was to show you that this isn't some abstract idea: when I treat him with contempt, I'm revealing to him that I *know* it's the same move, and that I'm using that fact against him.

That doesn't mean that hating animal abuse itself is bad, of course. It just means I have to see whether my behavior is actually stopping it, or blindly reinforcing it. And I can only do that by taking a closer look at what I'm actually doing—whether I'm actually oriented toward the good, or just telling myself I am.

---

What about cases where nobody is seemingly hurt? Is the turn still wrong?

Ask a soldier whether it's wrong to desecrate an enemy's corpse, even if nobody is watching and no one will ever find out. Most will say yes, without hesitation. But why? Where's the harm, exactly? A corpse can't be hurt, and his loved ones will never know.

Look what it does to the soldier himself. It encourages him to degrade life. And not just the life of his enemy, but *life in general.* If his allies were watching, it would teach them the same thing.

Can you feel that?

We can't pinpoint where or how (or even if) that lesson will manifest as physical harm to anything living, and yet we sense the wrongness anyway. It's as if the action isn't just *teaching* him to degrade life, but is *itself* degrading life. We don't need to trace the consequences to know that.

Now think back to the two strangers. Both would have said they had "good intentions," and both might have shut you up or stopped your misbehavior, so both could have said they produced a good outcome. And yet one of them did wrong. But where's the wrong?

Recall the lesson contempt teaches: *why should I be the lone chump?* Does that make a person *more* likely to benefit themselves at others' expense, or less? To lie, cheat, and steal—perhaps in ways that can't be caught? To treat others with contempt or dignity?

Say I'm an oil executive who's been harshly criticized for deceiving the public—in one case, with my dignity intact; and in another, with contempt. A new proposal comes across my desk that will benefit me while potentially degrading a sensitive ecosystem. There's no conclusive proof that it will, and my PR team has provided ample counter-evidence. How am I likely to weigh it?

If I'm honest, I'm far more likely to license myself to act out of self-interest in the latter case—because *fuck* the self-righteous hypocrites who use my product and then throw me under the bus for it. What about you? 

The way I'll justify it is by subtly giving the counter-evidence more weight. *I'm not a monster, dammit. It was a genuinely gray area.* 

Even when contempt suppresses public misbehavior, there are countless other channels for it to express itself. This is one of them. Another is that I might have gone home and taken it out on my wife and kids.

Contempt doesn't always make its target worse, of course. People still have a choice in how to respond. But if you've ever chosen to become better after someone's contempt, was it *because* of how you were treated, or despite it? For myself, I find it takes effort to say *I'm not going to be the one who passes this on.* To stop the spread of the infection.

What the first stranger did wrong is that he also degraded life. Yours directly: he held you out of view, the you that knows you count, and made sure you saw him do it. And as a result, *life in general*: like the executive, you carried that lesson into whatever you did next, and it took effort not to pass it on. They come as a package, and I think part of us knows it.

---

When we measure the outcomes of our behavior, we have a powerful incentive to draw the boundaries of what we measure in a place that makes us look good. And because so much of our long-term impact comes from how we affect people's minds—which is effectively impossible to measure—we have every justification to draw that boundary in a way that excludes most of its actual effects. We can simply say "I couldn't have known."

But couldn't we?

The people who shamed me above can count what they did as good: a liar ruthlessly exposed. They’ll never count the ecosystem—and they don't have to, to know that it's wrong. When I'm in their shoes, my conscience is warning me about *something*. It's as if it already senses that my behavior will darken the world. My rational mind tells me not to listen, because how could I possibly know such a thing? And yet somehow I do. I don't know where or when it will come out, but I can feel what I'm putting into the world.

---

When I'm the first stranger, notice what I do: I make sure the facts are on my side, and *sneak in* the thing that I know will harm him. If I really believed the visible facts told the whole story, why would I need to sneak in anything? Part of me *must* know better, and I'm counting on being able to claim otherwise.

This is how my mind, with enough cleverness, can inject *massive* amounts of darkness into the world in ways that look objectively "clean" on the surface, in ways that benefit myself. Consider how the politician lies his country into a war to line his own pockets. Does he know he's doing this? I think the answer is yes and no, just as it is with myself. Do *I*, the voter, know he's doing this? When his lies happen to produce a world that's aligned with my own selfish interests, I have a powerful incentive not to detect it.

When someone's project benefits me, I stop looking at the orientation they're bringing to it, choosing to focus on the "objective facts." So here's what I've decided for myself. If they're turned away, then even if the project benefits me, they're probably still causing vast amounts of harm I can't (or won't) see. And that's a trade-off I'm no longer willing to make.

And it isn't only politicians who can put darkness into the world and keep the books clean. Anyone with enough reach can, and so can we. In fact, when I look at it, I think the *vast majority* of the effects we see in the world are the result of intentions we don't see, or rather, don't let ourselves see. I think we know this, but have learned to use this hiddenness to our advantage.

This, I think, is what's behind our ecological crisis. Its physical causes are many, but the movement beneath them is the same: an accumulation of countless choices, made across billions of people, to _turn away_. Some are as direct and consequential as mine as the oil executive. Others are further upstream: my willingness to defend him because his business benefits me, or to stop asking questions about something I buy because I like the price. Our power and responsibility vary enormously. But wherever I keep some part of life out of view because letting it count would cost me, the move is the same.

I don't have to want a forest destroyed, a species gone, or a river poisoned. I only have to find the reasons that let me keep taking my particular benefit without facing my share in the cost. Across enough people and enough decisions, those refusals accumulate into a world none of us would have chosen to face all at once. Each of us has an explanation for our part, while the living world carries the sum.

---

There are several tricks I use to give myself permission to turn away.

The first is to have a rule. Any time I try to define goodness, I give myself something to blindly follow instead of orienting. It gives the self-serving part of me a way to follow the letter of the law while ignoring its spirit, allowing me to cause tremendous harm in the name of "good."

The other way is to have none. When there's no letter to follow, the same part says: nobody has ever managed to pin down _good_ and _evil_, so I'm free to define them as I choose. In my own mind, it sounds more innocent: _who's to say whether this behavior is good or bad?_ Have you ever felt that “who's to say?”

The hard thing to hold is that there *is* an orientation there, I can't give it an objective definition, and that still doesn't make it mine to define. [My only choice is whether to admit it's there or not, and even that's something I'm not sure "choice" is the right word for. The more obvious it is what I'm choosing into, the less willing I am to defy it.]

I often tell myself is that I am the one in charge, sitting between the angel of my better nature and the devil of self-interest, deciding which one gets to win today. I treat the former like an advisor offering an opinion, which I can weigh against other practical concerns and set aside if the price is right.

But it's not an advisor. When I look at what is right and say _not today_, I'm already turning away. The reasons are there to make that refusal feel like a considered choice.

In that case, I don't get to look down on my enemy from above. I'm just another person who knew what was right, decided it was negotiable, and then looked for someone worse than me to prove I wasn't so bad after all.

They say integrity is what we do when no one is watching. Whenever I've broken mine, it was by telling myself that no one was. But someone always was: me. Not the part of me busy with reasons, but the part I'd turned away from, so I wouldn't feel it watching. And I knew it was there the whole time.

---

I don't think I ignore my conscience first and spread the harm afterward. My refusal to listen is already harm. I tell myself it's fine as long as nobody else knows—but this is just a further act of self-deception, reinforcing the lesson I teach others: that *this is just how the world works.* That genuine goodness is a myth, and all that counts is what we can get away with; what we can prove. In such a world, our inner knowing doesn't matter.

The knowledge of what I've done goes somewhere deep, even if I manage to spend my life avoiding it. But what I refuse to face in myself keeps finding expression in the world—as if one were the reflection of the other.

I think this is what's meant by _the road to Hell is paved with good intentions._ The first stranger, the contemptuous critics, and I as the oil exec would all have called our intentions "good"—and yet part of us knew better. I think I'm going to have to face that one day. In my more honest, reflective moments, I can feel that at some level I already am.

Compare that to the second stranger. He had nothing to hide. He didn't benefit directly. If anything, it cost him. It's *hard* to confront others. And he showed the world that he could be held to the same standard—that he wasn't allowed to make exceptions for himself. In a way, that healed his outer world. He doesn't know when, or how, that will come back to him. But he doesn't need to. A healthier world is its own reward.

---

The executive carried it home, and that can't be the only road out of the room. Where else does it go? And part of me knows what it puts into the world as well as the second stranger did. So why do I keep choosing what the first stranger chose?
