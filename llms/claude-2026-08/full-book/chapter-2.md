# Chapter 2: What Evil Is

So what is evil, if it isn't a kind of person? Start with what you'd already call evil: a politician who lies his country into a war, executives who keep selling a drug they know is killing people, someone who tortures an animal for fun. What is the word tracking? A hurricane can kill thousands, and nobody calls it evil. The same death can be murder or an accident, depending on what was meant. It needn't be big. Watch what cutting in line does to you. The cost is thirty seconds. The heat is out of all proportion to thirty seconds, because what you're reacting to isn't the cost. It's the exception: somebody decided you didn't count, to get ahead, and let you see it. And it isn't the damage. The friend who forgot your birthday did more damage by most accountings than the friend who made one small cutting remark, and the remark is the one you're still thinking about a week later. Ask a soldier whether it's wrong to desecrate an enemy's corpse, with nobody watching. I expect a yes at once, and no answer to who's hurt. The dead man is past damage. What the yes is tracking is what was meant: that this man doesn't count.

And what was meant, every time, is an exception. The politician makes one for his side: other people's sons can die for it. The executives make one for their company, the man with the animal for his own fun, the line-cutter for himself. Cheating and contempt are one move from two ends: *this doesn't apply to me*, and *you're not really one of us*. When I skirt my own conscience and someone tries to shame me for it, I *know* they're only using my all-too-human failure to throw me under the bus, and there's no way in hell I'd let them get away with it. But when I'm the one doing the shaming, somehow it's different. That's the privilege I grant myself: to treat others with contempt for falling victim to their darkness. Not every advantage I take is an exception. I can take the last seat on the train with the man behind me fully in mind, and feel fine about it. I can't cut in front of him in line that way. Why not? Start with a face. [TODO(Aditya): Claude's draft (the bridge to the face; the last-seat check is key-points' F0 under "conscience detects the assertion of asymmetry"). Rewrite in your words or cut.]

Imagine looking into a stranger's face until it stops being something you're looking at and becomes a place somebody is looking out of. Don't try to add anything on. Just stay with the face, and see what comes.

If I stay there long enough, here's what comes first for me—not a conclusion, but a recognition:

> *There's someone there. This is real from the inside, the way I am.*

Now notice what happens to their wellbeing while that stays in view. For me, it starts to register. Not necessarily as affection, but as something I can't treat as nothing. Using them for my own ends feels *wrong*, in a way I didn't have to be taught.

Call this being *turned toward* them. Notice whether it requires any stories to maintain, or if there's something natural about it.

From this place, try to wish them harm. For me it can't be done. Something has to go out of view first, or be covered over: I must *turn away* from that perception—usually by adding a story about them and what they are, even if I'm still looking them in the eye. Accounts of atrocity return again and again to the same preparation: the hood, the number instead of the name, the word for vermin.

Not everyone you pass is in view in this sense, but that doesn't make it contempt. The hundred people around you in an airport are in the background; if one of them dropped a bag and started crying, they'd come into view, and you'd feel the pull. Turning away is keeping someone out of view when they'd come forward, because their reality would otherwise place a claim on you.

Out of view doesn't mean out of sight. A torturer has his victim in plain sight, and the cruelest watch the face closely. What they've turned from isn't the person's presence. It's his claim: that the one looking out of that face counts the way they do. That's the only sense of "in view" I'll use, and it's the sense contempt was using all along, when it spoke to the one who's in there to tell him nobody is. That makes part of this a definition, and I'll own it. What isn't a definition is what you checked: that your wrong needed a story, and your decency didn't. The torturer's turn is the one I make, smaller, to feel okay cheating on a test.

It's also the difference between the seat and the line. I can take the last seat with the man behind me in view. To cut in front of him, I have to put him out of view first. [TODO(Aditya): Claude's draft (the last seat brought back, so the exception meets the turn). Rewrite in your words or cut.]

Now put your enemy's face there instead (if it's someone who abused you, pick another), and try for the same recognition of sameness. If it won't come, notice what arrives in its place, and how fast. For me the story is there before the face is. That's the turning away, live.

The care that came with the face is what I'll call the light: what I turn from to cause harm, and what anyone is turned toward when they aren't turned away, in themselves and in everyone else. It's all the word will mean.

---

Most of us picture evil as something people have: a drive toward harm, a dark side with wants of its own, stronger in some people than in others. But I think it's fully explained by the turn.

The further I'm turned from you, the more acceptable (or even righteous) it feels to harm you. And recall the dehumanizing gaze check from Chapter 1, and how it felt like the other person was somehow *turned away* from themselves.

A drive wouldn't need the turn. Hunger doesn't have to look away from the bread. Harm has to look away from the person: I can't do it with him fully in view, it needs a story. Decency needs none of that. That asymmetry is what it means for care to be the baseline: what needs no turn and no story is where I am when I'm not doing anything, and turning away is a departure from it, not a second baseline of its own.

Turning away takes upkeep, at least until it's practiced enough to run by itself. A grudge has to be fed: the reasons rehearsed, the case kept open, their face kept out of view. Being turned toward someone takes nothing like that. And when a grudge finally drops, it doesn't feel like starting a new effort. It feels like setting something down. If turning away were the mind at rest, I'd expect it the other way around. So darkness is not an equal and opposite force.

It holds at the far end too. Try to picture the worst person you know of doing their worst while seeing their victim the way the second stranger saw you. I can't make the picture hold. Whatever drove them had to get the person out of view first.

If darkness were an essence, I'd be right to be afraid of it, and ashamed, and I'd have to keep proving it's in others and not in me. But if it's something I *do*, then I can stop.

Badness is the result of turning away, not its cause. When I tell myself the bad people don't have a conscience, or that they choose to turn away from it due to some "bad essence," I'm turning it back into a cause. Or I tell myself that sure, sometimes I do wrong too, but my justifications are reasonable, or at least forgivable. One way or another, something in me needs there to be an essential difference between us.

---

There is one kind of evil, and it's the turn. By *evil* I don't mean damage. Damage can be an accident. I mean the not-seeing that licenses it, and every harm done while the people it touches are held out of view has it in it. Check it against the cases you'd already use the word for; I haven't found a second kind. It was never a thing some people have. It's the turn itself.

It's an absence. Every faculty is still at work, and the other person's life is no longer allowed to count the way I still know it does. [TODO(Aditya): adapted from Appendix 1's "So evil is the turn" paragraph.]

The padded expense, the lie to the insurer, the joke that makes someone smaller, the slur, the order to clear a village: each needs someone held out of view so that I, or mine, can be the exception. Now widen it. What looked out of your enemy's face was alive. So was what looked out of yours, and one refused the other. And the same refusal is in everything that is eating the world. A man held as a thing, a forest as nothing but lumber: each needs only a living thing held as stuff, by something alive that has turned from what it is.

But despite having no essence, evil isn't just nothing. Call it that and I'll underestimate it: I'll stop looking at exactly the moment it's crucial to look more closely. It tracks, it aims, it fights to stay hidden. It's an absence that behaves like a presence: it has no substance of its own; everything it uses—my attention, my reasons, my eyes—is borrowed from me. When it's operating, it looks and behaves exactly like you'd expect a "bad essence" to look. It's what I saw in my enemy, and what I showed him in return.

---

To wield contempt, we have to already know that it's evil, and that its target is redeemable: the verdict was aimed at pushing them further from the light, and nobody can be pushed from a place they aren't in. And for the message to sting, they had to read it as evil, not as harshness. So it wasn't *the good kind of darkness* I told myself it was in Chapter 1. Harshness alone would have landed like the second stranger's correction, and left my enemy a way back. Mine told him the door was closed. Contempt is evil. That's not a value judgment.

And contempt is the turn made into a message. **It's evil's way of spreading in secret, through people who sincerely believe they're fighting it.**
