## Preface

> *For so long as the root of wickedness is hidden, it is strong. But when it is recognized, it is dissolved. When it is revealed, it perishes. ... It is powerful because we have not recognized it.* —The Gospel of Philip

Bring to mind the worst people in the world. Maybe someone from a news story that made you feel truly enraged; one where innocent people were hurt. *How can people do this and sleep at night?*

Maybe you ascribe it to a force called _evil_—something they have that we don't. Or maybe you see it as a profound form of confusion. Either way, there's a feeling: there's something _fundamentally different_ about them. _I_ could never do that. I'm made of _better stuff._

If we're honest, there's a part of us that would love nothing more than to see them hurt—*quite independently of any good it might do*. Check and see if that's true.

Or here's a test. Say you could either deliver the punishment they so richly deserve, or give them a pill that magically enlightens them—ending the threat, but no punishment at all. Forget the "right" answer. Isn't there a flicker in there just *itching* to mash that first button?

I know there is in me.

But who cares, right? It's not like we'd ever act on it. And if it's just sitting there, confined to our skulls, then it doesn't matter. There's no way it could have any effect on the world.

Except that it does. That belief—*I'm nothing like them*—compels us to behave in ways that reproduce the very thing we hate. That create a world full of "evil people" who we can be sure we're nothing like. And the belief that it's confined to our skulls: that's how we avoid seeing what we're doing.

You may have heard versions of this before but dismissed it. *Where's the evidence?* 

I want to show you exactly how it works.

Nothing here will ask you to soften what you see, forgive what shouldn't be forgiven, or pretend the harm isn't real. If anything, you'll end up seeing more clearly what's wrong—what the cost is to keep fighting it the way we do.

I can't argue you into this, and you should be wary of anyone who tries. What I can do is take you to the places where you can see it for yourself—in your own memory, where you have evidence I couldn't have planted.

If you look and recognize what you find, *that's* the evidence.

Let's start with small examples, since that's where this is easiest to see.

---
## Chapter 1: Limbo

Have you ever done something you knew was wrong at the time, but couldn't fully admit to yourself until later? Maybe you made a cruel comment, took credit for someone else's work, or cheated on a test (or even a partner).

Recall what it was like to finally own up to it. The knowledge that it was wrong: did it feel like new information? Or like something you had always known, but somehow hadn't *let* yourself know?

We like to call such knowledge "unconscious"—as if we had no way of knowing—but that's not entirely accurate, is it? It was *right there* to see if you had looked; fully available to consciousness. You were just *turned away* from it. The knowledge existed in a strange kind of **limbo**, in which you simultaneously knew and yet somehow didn't.

And it wasn't just sitting there, idle: it influenced your behavior. Any time someone came close to exposing it, or you started getting close to it yourself, you found a way to deflect, right?

Looking back, can you sense that *some part of you knew exactly what you were doing, even as you did it?* That **behavior** took place in limbo, too. But again, we normally call it "unconscious." Do you see what we gain from calling it that?

---

Try to recall how you knew it was wrong in the first place.

Whatever it was, it arrived *before* you'd done any calculations, right? The part of you that knew: it couldn't say exactly how it knew. Meanwhile, another part of you was busy providing justifications. *Everyone does it. It's not a big deal. Just this once.*

Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?

And which side did you listen to—the part that "just knew" or the part that could prove?

I mean, *obviously*. Why trust something that can't even justify itself?

And yet, despite "losing" that battle, notice that it never budged an inch. It kept on quietly knowing exactly what it knew.

That's why you needed limbo in the first place. If all you could hear was your own conscience—no justifications to hide behind—could you have even done what you did?

---

By its very nature, limbo isn't the kind of state we easily recognize or willingly remember. And if you have to enter it for even small misbehaviors, how much the more so for the big ones? Would those be easier to recall, or harder?

How much of human behavior might be taking place from some degree of limbo?

---
## The two strangers

Imagine that while you were in that state—doing wrong, justifying it, half-knowing—a stranger had caught you and publicly chastised you for it. Not just for doing something wrong, but for *being the kind of person who does*.

You've been in that situation before. Really visualize it, and how it plays out. When you try to exonerate yourself—not your behavior, necessarily, but what kind of person you are—what happens? Does he just let you go?

And that behavior: does it make it *easier* or *harder* to confront what you already knew?

Harder, almost certainly. But why?

Because *who the hell is he to judge*, right? He's *obviously* the kind of person who does this too—things he knows are wrong, while pretending they're not.

You'd take that bet at any odds. But on what evidence? You'd never met him. You knew nothing about him. All you had to go on was the way he was standing there, doing what he was doing.

---

Now imagine a different stranger had caught you—but instead of treating you with contempt, this one treated you with dignity. Not more gently. Just that his anger was directed at what you *did*, and not what you *are*.

Would you have dug in? Vowed revenge?

So the problem wasn't being caught. In fact, he could have been **harsher** than the first person—said things that stung more, in front of more people, left you even less room to wriggle out—and it still wouldn't have triggered the same reaction.

Why not? Again, try to put yourself back in those shoes.

It's like he was reminding you of something: that *you're better than this*. Meanwhile, the first stranger was claiming that *this is all you are*.

It's almost as if this one was giving voice to your own conscience. You knew you couldn't win against it—and more importantly, you didn't want to.

If you had to guess: is *this* the kind of person who sneaks around getting away with stuff?

---

Both people may have stopped your misbehavior, and so both will leave confident that what they did "worked." But one of them made it easier to turn away from what you knew, and the other made it harder. And that change might be small, but it will long outlast this interaction.

It won't feel like a change to who you are—it will feel like you've learned something about the world: whether it's the kind of place where people look out for each other, or one where they pursue their own self-interested needs.

Later, you may face a small choice—say, whether to give a stranger the benefit of the doubt—and that change will inform your decision. And if you make the "wrong" choice, it won't *feel* wrong. It will just feel like what you must do to protect yourself in this selfish world.

We never say "I'm going to ignore my conscience." Instead, it feels like the world is forcing our hand. Our justifications simply feel more convincing, more obvious, more *righteous*—as though they *are* our conscience. The two become just a little harder to tell apart.

---
## The Cause

But what exactly did the first stranger do to cause this? And did he know he was?

First, what does contempt license? Once you believe "this is all you are" about someone, how does that enable you to treat them? Is there any bottom to it?

But it's worse than that. Recall what happens when you try to exonerate yourself in such situations. Deny it, and you're exactly the kind of person who denies it. Get angry, and there it is—proof. Stay calm, and you don't even care. Apologize, and it's a little too convenient.

Every road funnels into the same conclusion.

Contempt doesn't just permit harming someone who deserves it. It shapes them into the kind of person who does.

We all know this is how it works on some level. But did he know that while he was doing it?

---

On one hand, what he was doing certainly *felt* intentional, didn't it? Something about his tone and gaze gave it away instantly, even if his words didn't.

How could it be purely accidental? He has to track your reactions, navigate around obstacles, and prevent you from wriggling out and exonerating yourself.

It's as if some part of him *needed* you to be exactly what he said you were, and was working hard at proving it.

On the other hand, how could it actually *be* intentional? If he knew he was planning to use your reactions against you, how could he turn around and sincerely hold them against you? You can't trust evidence that you know you planted.

So he couldn't be fully aware of his own need, let alone that he was working so hard at fulfilling it. To him, it didn't feel like a need; it just felt obviously true.

And yet his behavior couldn't have been fully unconscious either. Not only could it not be so precisely guided, but he'd be embarrassed or confused if you pointed it out. But that's not what would have happened. It would have *intensified*, in exactly the way needed to keep itself hidden.

What does that remind you of?

*Limbo.* He knew what he was doing, and he didn't.

So you weren't just sensing that he's the kind of person who deceives himself in general. You were sensing that he was doing it right then, in the act of judging you. He was judging you *from* the very state he was judging you *for*. 

---

Nobody enters limbo because they're doing something they feel is right. They do it because they sense it's *wrong*. So you could sense he was up to no good *by his own lights*. 

But what was "wrong" about it? As far as he knows, he sees a genuinely bad person. What could be wrong with scolding them? Making them feel how *defective* they are?

On the other hand, why is he working so hard to prove something he supposedly already knows?

There's a contradiction there, and he can feel it. His conscience is warning him about something. He can't hear what it's saying, exactly, but he knows he has to quiet it.

At some level, he knows he can't actually silence his conscience. But he can damn well try.

So he does what he must: "proves" to it that you are what he says you are.

---

But what tools does he have? Look how tight his constraints are.

He can't do anything that will reveal that he's doing anything wrong—crucially, *even to himself*. Otherwise, when he looks back on it, he'll realize it was his fault. So he can't be overtly cruel, even if he wants to.

That doesn't give him much to work with. Nothing that can be audited. Which leaves... what, exactly? His tone; his gaze; his manner. Exactly the things you used to detect it.

And think back on exactly what it was that made you dig in. You felt both that it was intentional, and that there was no part of him you could appeal to, right? Both halves worked together to produce that maddening quality that made you dig in. You *knew* he was up to something, but couldn't prove it.

If it had looked unintentional, you might have forgiven or pitied him instead. That wouldn't have given him what he (or this part of him) needed.

And if he had offered you something to appeal to or accuse in return, that would have taken the sting out of it: even if he denied your accusations out loud, you would at least have known that it registered somewhere. That would have felt satisfying, wouldn't it? It would have relieved the tension.

So that precise combination: appearing intentional—as if there's "someone behind the wheel"—and yet with no part of him to appeal to, was both necessary to accomplish the job, and as we saw, sufficient.

In other words, in addition to being in limbo, he simply had to *telegraph* that he was.

But how the heck do you intentionally telegraph a state that, by definition, you cannot be fully aware you're in?

---

Let's zoom out.

Whatever it is in him that's doing this, it seems intentional; goal-driven. And it *presents itself as such* to you while hiding itself from him. In fact, not just *while* hiding itself from him. It reveals to you *that it is* hiding itself from him.

So it can't exactly be him in the usual sense—and yet in another sense, it is.

It behaves like an agent, so we'll refer to it as one. I'll call it his "dark" part, or just his "darkness"—both because it's hidden from him, and because of what we're about to see.
 
---

So it's counting on you to recognize it for what it is—and you do, instantly. But why?

*Because you've been there yourself, haven't you?*

We all have. We all know what it's like to hide from what we know. We've watched our enemies squirm under our "righteous" gaze, half-knowing that their reaction to it will convict them, not us. And it doesn't feel wrong, because we're doing it for a *good reason.* We can even "prove" it.

But why *half*-knowing? Why the quotes around "righteous"? Because *you couldn't have done it while looking straight at it.*

So something in you recognizes what he's doing, instantly, without ever having had a word for it. You know that flavor from the inside.

And he *knows* you know it—he must, for his plan to be coherent. Again: why?

Because it is something **we all know**. It is common knowledge.

Think back to the opening, where I knew that you had to be in limbo to cause harm—despite not knowing anything about you. You weren't *surprised* that I knew that, were you? *Why not?*

Because a deep part of you already knows this about everyone. I didn't teach you that no one can cause harm without first turning away from a deep part of themselves. I just said out loud something you were already carrying—something that's in limbo for most of us. (And if you're wondering: *what about psychopaths?* Don't worry, we'll get to them later.)

His whole approach *depends* on it being common knowledge. So he doesn't have to communicate what he's doing in words—he only has to gesture.

---

If we all know this about each other (at some level), why do we act like we don't?

Well, what would it be like to consciously recognize that your enemies aren't fundamentally different from you—that they cause harm using essentially the same mechanism you do? That they have a conscience, don't actually want to turn away from it, but sometimes feel like they must?

It would mean that they're redeemable, and that your contempt for them has only been making them worse—driving them further from their conscience—all for the "benefit" of proving that you're nothing alike.

*Hell no.* That just can't be.

And that reaction—**that's** what this whole process relies on.

---

His darkness is evoking this common knowledge in a way that renders it totally unmistakable, yet without giving you anything to grab onto to prove it.

It's like a parallel layer of communication happening *beneath* the words—one that can't be recorded on any ledger or in any transcript, and yet is louder than the stuff that can.

Could you have named what was happening out loud? What would you have said—*you're being mean?* How would that have turned out?

You both knew that, too.

---

So when he looked at you, he wasn't actually seeing "bad person." He was seeing someone struggling to follow their conscience. Someone ultimately just like him.

But whatever harm you were causing, he couldn't bear that it was caused by something he has inside himself. So he *had* to see you as "bad person." To turn away from the part of himself that saw the truth—and therefore from your humanity.

But that only makes the problem worse. Because now he isn't just looking at someone whose behavior he himself is capable of; he's watching himself engage in that very behavior while blaming them for it. Accusing them of being *fundamentally different*.

Which forces him to overlook your humanity even harder. Which his conscience registers and warns him about. Which increases the pressure to prove he's nothing like you. Which is what reveals to you—and to part of him—that he is, because it's his only way to make you *bad*. Which you clearly sense, threatening to expose the whole ruse.
 
Which forces him to overlook your humanity even harder...

---
## The Taunt

There is no natural stopping point—because as much as he tries, he never stops hearing his conscience. And the worse you become, the more he's forced to see this plan all the way through. To push you all the way to the end, where (he believes) his conscience will finally see the "truth."

Or, to see it from the other side: he's sure he sees darkness in you—and he knows how evasive it is, because he has firsthand knowledge of it. And he's not going to simply let it get away. 

At some level, he registers that his way of dealing with it is underhanded. That there's a better way. *But sometimes you have to get your hands a bit dirty to fight evil...*

---

By turning away from his conscience and remaining so, he gives rise to an agent that does his "dirty work" for him. And it doesn't merely act *on* you. It *addresses* you—without giving you anything to address in return.

Here's what it communicates:

> *We both know exactly what I'm doing to you, and that nobody can prove it. Which means anything you try to do to wriggle out of it will be turned against you—because even "I" won't know what I've done wrong.*
>
> *I know **exactly** how maddening that is, and what it will drive you to do. And when you inevitably do it, I'll be right here, waiting to do it again.*
>
> *I am going to provoke you until you become the very thing I need you to be. Which means that when I'm finally forced to do to you what I must, nobody will feel sorry for you—because you'll only have shown them what you really are.*
>
> *I know you can see this, but you can't prove it. Good luck.*

Which you know but cannot prove. Which turns you into what it needs you to be.

Which **is** precisely the "what" that he was doing to you. But good luck proving it.

---

His darkness is **seducing** you—turning you away from what you know and toward what you can prove. Making you trust your reasons over your conscience.

And it can all be communicated through a single glance. We all know what it means on some level, even if we've never put it into words or felt the full weight of it before. And it works precisely *because* we all know it, but cannot prove it. That's why the glance only has to point at it.

You may have felt this before, as a kind of "hall of mirrors" effect, where you both know exactly what's happening, and that neither one of you can name it.

This is what is being communicated from inside that tunnel.

---

It feels like this on the receiving end: *if this is the kind of world we live in—where people can be cruel and the world calls it "righteous"; where we all know exactly what's happening but pretend not to—then **fuck it**. Game on.* 

That's what teaches you to do it too. To demote that inner knowing—because what is it worth?

Again, you don't tell yourself you'll ignore your conscience. Your justifications—what you can "prove"—simply become more reasonable. More practical. As though they *are* your conscience. The two become just a little harder to tell apart.

The very notion of a conscience starts to feel more shaky. The one faculty you have to detect this corruption of your mind slowly becomes just another voice among the noise, with no special status.

And the thing is, it doesn't feel like a loss at all. It feels like you've gotten a bit wiser. A little less naive about how this world really operates.

Or, to put it another way: *your* darkness has just gotten a bit stronger.

---

That's why it feels so good to him. Not the harming—he'd never allow himself that. But being _proven right_, in real time, by you. And from the inside, being proven right and being right are the same experience. The only difference is who produced the evidence, and that's the part he can't see.

And look at what he thinks he's been proven right about. Not that you're careless, or rude. That the thing behind everything wrong with the world just surfaced in front of him—and he's the one who caught it. Which is why it never mattered how small the thing you did was. It only had to be _evidence_.

Which is why he can't see what he's doing. Certainty, urgency, the sense that he must not let this go—those are what facing real evil actually calls for. What's missing is his felt connection to what they were meant to serve. *Life*. The thing he's overlooking in you right now. The thing he's disconnected from in himself.

---

And watch what it does to him.

He just watched you get worse in response to his "righteous" behavior, convincing him that people really are as rotten as he feared. So he gives that part of himself more power; learns to trust it more.

A part that's making his enemies _more_ entrenched. Harder to fight. Creating more of the very thing he hates. If he were *fully conscious* of what it is creating: could he really want it?

So whose goals is it pursuing, exactly? His—or its own? 

---

And yet it certainly _looks_ like he wants it, doesn't it?

And what do we call people who obviously want to cause harm—especially when pretending it's good? How do we treat them?

With *contempt.*

Which settles what he is. And once that's settled, whatever we do to him isn't harm anymore. It's what he *deserves.*

Treating him that way gives that part of him *more* power, because you'll have done to him the very thing he did to you.

**His dark part: it _benefits_ from you seeing it as something he wants. As something he *is*.**

That's how it keeps itself alive: by convincing him it doesn't exist in him, while ensuring you see it *as* him.

Which is why none of us ever recognize it operating in ourselves. It hides itself perfectly from our own minds while revealing itself to others, compelling them to righteously attack us for it, enabling ours to hide itself better from us.

That's what contempt for people **is**. And it only works because we all know this, at some level, but don't *let* ourselves know it—individually, or as a culture.

Something in us is benefiting from keeping this knowledge hidden.

---

The world has gotten slightly worse as a result of this interaction, and yet there's not a trace of objective evidence of anything wrong ever having happened. And the objective facts are what we have learned to trust *more*. So the very thing causing the problem has hidden itself better.

It's not that the deeper part of you has been silenced. That's not how it works.

It's still there, quietly knowing what it knows, patiently waiting to be remembered.

Just as it is in him.

---

I cannot prove any of this to you, but I suspect some part of you already knows it. If you recognized it, that's the evidence.
