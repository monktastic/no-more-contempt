# Chapter 9: The Satisfaction

**What it's for.** Chapter 3 ("The Sacrifice") already carries the wanting from the delivering end in the first person. This chapter is the reader's version: the pleasure of punishing evil, why part of us doesn't want corruption to stop, the strategic-darkness excuse, bad people as the place we put what we can't bear to see, and the relief when the verdict lands. It should be the chapter a reader can't finish without having caught themselves once.

**Seeded in Chapter 5 by:** relieved or robbed; nothing more satisfying than punishing evil; part of me doesn't; bad people as dumping ground; the darkest idea.

## Outline

1. Nothing more primally satisfying than violently punishing evil. Pause and feel it. Bring someone real to mind. You want them to *know*. (Key-points §6; Chapter 3 opening.)
2. Why would I want to stop corruption when I feel so good judging it? Reform versus eternal punishment: which button. (Preface; key-points §6.)
3. "My darkness is the strategic kind." The whole trick in one line. (Chapter 2, "From the delivering end"; key-points §1.)
4. What we make people unsafe *for*: so they can't look closely enough to see their darkness isn't them. Bad people as where we put what we can't bear to see. The message under the words: somebody has to carry this. (Chapter 3, "The sacrifice"; key-points §8.)
5. The need to look like good people produces the incentive to create bad people. The special privilege: how you feel when someone shames you for skirting your conscience, and why you think it's different when you do it. (Key-points §6.)
6. The check: relieved or robbed. The relief is fast and dressed as clarity; the first several times you'll find nothing. (Map §10, §12; interlude.)
7. The darkest idea: round up the bad people. Every atrocity begins there; genocide requires seeing the victims as less than human, and that isn't rhetoric. (Map §9; key-points §8.)
