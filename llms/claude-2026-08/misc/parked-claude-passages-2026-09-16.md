## chapter-4.md: the in-the-moment instruction

**Sat after:** What is yours is how fast you catch it afterward: days, then hours, then sometimes in the moment. That is trainable, and…

**Stand-in left:** And when you do catch it in the moment: look at the contempt itself, not the person. Where is it? What's it made of? In my experience it doesn't survive that, because there was nothing there to fight.

**Full text:**

And when you do catch it in the moment: don't look at the person, and don't look at what you're doing to them; that comes after. Look at the contempt itself. The heat. Where is it, what's it made of, does it have an edge or a location? Look at it with the same eyes you held the stranger with at the start. In my experience it doesn't survive that, not because you fought it but because there was nothing there to fight; what it was made of was the same thing the stranger's eyes were made of, turned the wrong way. The traditions say this is what every affliction is made of, and Appendix 3 says it in their words. Past that there is a further stage, which I can describe but haven't stabilized: the contempt arises and is seen before the verdict forms, and hands you the information (the harm is real, stop it) with the verdict already gone. That's what the people we call wise are doing. It comes from familiarity, not effort, and it's less exalted than it sounds from outside. It doesn't feel like wisdom. It feels like noticing you were about to reach for a second biscuit. And the verdict still forms on bad days, and then it's looked at too.

## chapter-4.md: the ends-justify-the-means paragraph

**Full text:**

"The ends justify the means" is the same kind of phrase. I'm not going to argue against it as a principle; my own claim is that there's no rule that can't be followed while turned away, and that one is no exception. It has a benevolent reading: sometimes a hard thing has to be done, and the doing of it isn't the point. And it has the other one, and the other one is this. You tell yourself that what matters is the outcome, not what you intended. They stopped, or at least they felt it; that's the outcome; good. But look at what it takes to hold that thought. You have to not look at what you intended. And not looking at your intention is the very thing that lets it go out through your eyes and become an outcome of its own, one you can deny, because you weren't looking. The means was never separate from the ends. It was the means that leaked, and it produced an end you never wrote down.

That's the second answer to why, under the horror at the end of Chapter 1. Under both of those is fear: fear of what you might be. I don't think fear is the bottom. The traditions say that first there's a not-noticing with no motive in it, then a someone, and fear is the first thing that someone feels; Appendix 3 says it in their words. Nobody who felt safe in their own nature would need any of the three.

## intro.md: two of Aditya's paragraphs moved out of the introduction, 17 Sept 2026 (every round of readers named the intro as doing too much; Chapter 3 carries the first, Chapters 2 and 4 the second)

One piece of this is stranger than the rest. It's how the darkness in our minds doesn't only operate *through* us, but acts as though it collaborates *across* us. The corrupt structures we see around us are its outward form. This is the origin of the archetype of the "evil global conspiracy," or what might also be called the Devil. That claim comes last, in Chapter 3, after you'll have watched it happen between two people.

Our attempts to define *goodness* are folly. No matter how carefully we try to define it, the dark aspect of our mind finds clever ways to subvert it: to obey the letter of the law while violating its spirit. We recognize this error in others easily, but in ourselves we can't, because following definitions has been made to feel exactly like being turned toward our true nature. Thus, the folly of trying to pin down *goodness* is not an accident. Getting us to believe our thoughts and definitions—as though they could substitute for staying *turned toward*—is precisely what the darkness *is*.
