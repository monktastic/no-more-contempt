## Intro

This piece makes a claim that will sound impossible:

- You already know the source of all wrongdoing.
- Pretending you don't **is** the source of all wrongdoing.
- And you already knew that, too.

By _pretending_, I do not mean consciously telling yourself a lie. I mean holding knowledge out of view while keeping enough of it to aim the harm, call the harm righteous, and recognize the same maneuver in others.

You already know what goodness is. To do wrong, you make yourself _not know_ it. Then you make yourself not know that this is what you have done. But the second concealment is not a different maneuver. It is the same maneuver turned upon itself. **The source of wrongdoing keeps itself hidden by doing, one level up, exactly what it is.**

So this piece can't give you the answer as new information. It works by asking you to check some things in your own case, and then asking what best explains them. The claims it will argue for are these:

- Contempt is evil: not as a verdict on anyone, but as what the word has always meant.
- Kindness is its opposite. It isn't niceness, and it's the one stance that never itself feeds the thing.
- Pretending we don't know either of these *is* the darkness.

If that's right, then the source of wrongdoing and the way out are the same act seen from opposite directions. To do wrong, you must hide from what you already know. To stop, you must stop hiding. You don't need a better moral system. You need to recognize what every moral system was trying to write down, and what none of them can make you look at.

One warning. The piece asks you to look at things about yourself that are uncomfortable, and it can only point; if it doesn't match your case, it doesn't, and you should say so.

# Part I: What you already know

## 1. Limbo

Have you ever done something you knew was wrong at the time, but couldn't fully admit to yourself until later? Maybe you took credit for someone else's work, said something cruel to someone who'd done nothing to you, or cheated on a test (or a partner).

Recall what it was like to finally own up to it. The knowledge that it was wrong: did it feel like new information? Or like something you had always known, but somehow hadn't *let* yourself know?

We like to call that knowledge "unconscious," as if we had no way of knowing. But it was right there to see if you had looked. You were just turned away from it. It sat in a strange kind of limbo, where you knew and somehow didn't. Try and remember what that was like.

And it wasn't sitting there idle. Any time someone came close to exposing it, or you came close to it yourself, you found a way to deflect. Did that happen by itself? No: that behavior took place in limbo, too—simultaneously conscious and unconscious.

Now try to recall how you knew it was wrong in the first place. Did that knowledge arrive before or after you'd reasoned it through? The part of you that knew couldn't say how it knew. Meanwhile another part of you was busy supplying reasons: *everyone does it, it's not a big deal, just this once.* Why did one side have to argue and the other not? In this case at least, the side that argued was the side with something to get past. And which side did you listen to?

One more thing. If all you could hear was the part that knew, with no reasons to hide behind, could you have done it at all?

## 2. What that already tells you about yourself

Try it now, in your head, with something small. Pick something you could do today that would cost somebody else: the padded invoice, the credit quietly taken. Now put that person in front of you, in full view, as somebody who matters, with no justification to hand, and feel for whether you could still go through with it while looking at them. Notice what happens. (If the thing you picked has nobody in it, nobody you'd have to look at, hold onto that; it's the other way of doing harm, and it comes back later.)

So here is what your own case shows, and none of it is theory:

- You knew.
- You kept the knowing out of view.
- You had to, because it wouldn't go while they were in view.

Call the looking away, and everything done from it, *the darkness*. That's all I'll mean by the word.

Notice what those three facts rule out. When you did the wrong thing, it didn't point to a "bad nature" that you have or are made of. Something in you knew better the whole time, and the wrong could only happen by hiding from that something. If the darkness had been all there was, there would have been nothing to hide from, and no need to.

And you can see this now. The part that knew is the part reading this sentence. It didn't go anywhere. It was overruled, not destroyed.

That's as far as your own case takes you, and the limit matters. It doesn't yet prove that nobody has a rotten core. It proves that *you* don't: doing wrong and being rotten are two different things, and you know it from the inside.

## 3. Now bring in the person you can't stand

Think of someone whose harm was real. Not a stranger who was rude to you; someone who actually qualifies, from your life or from the news.

Whatever they did, notice that they could only have done it by looking away from the person they hurt. Check that. Can you picture even the worst person doing their worst while seeing their victim the way you'd see a friend, fully, as somebody who matters? See whether that picture holds. It's why "he looked me in the eye and did it anyway" is the most chilling thing you can say about someone. He saw you. My claim is that even then the seeing was partial, that something in him had to not see you or he couldn't have done it, and I can't show that from the phrase. I can only ask whether it matches your own case.

And you know why people look away. It's the only way to do the thing you couldn't do while looking. Which is what you did. Looking away from the person and looking away from what you knew aren't two different things, because what you knew was that they mattered.

So you already know two things about them that you never had to be taught. They knew. And they hid it from themselves. That's the shape of your own case, and you're so sure of it that you say it every time: *they knew better.* You wouldn't bother saying that about a rock, or a shark. You say it about someone who could have known, and did.

If you want to say *but they chose to look away, and that's where the rot is*, check that against your own case too. Did you choose to look away while still looking? There's no such moment. The view went, and then the reasons came, and only afterward did it feel like something you'd wanted. If it went that way for you, that's the best reason you have to think it went that way for them.

None of that lets anyone off, you included. It just moves responsibility to where it actually lives. It never lived in a clear-eyed moment of choosing to do wrong, because there wasn't one. It lives in how fast you catch the looking-away, in what you repair when you can see again, and in what you do to give it less room next time. That's a heavier standard than the usual one, not a lighter one, and it applies to them exactly as it applies to you.

Two people look like counterexamples, and they deserve more than a wave. The first is the sadist, who wants his victim to know, and so must be seeing them. But look at what he's seeing. He needs their personhood the way contempt needs the part of them that knows fairness: as the thing to press on. Seeing someone as the place where your pleasure lands isn't seeing them; it's the same looking-away with heat added. Run the test from section 2 on him: can you picture him doing it while wanting them to be all right? The second is the person who never looked at all: the executive who signed off on the schedule, the official who never met anyone it would hurt. There's no limbo in him, because there was never a view to hide from. That's true, and it's the other way of doing harm: arrange never to acquire the view. It's colder than contempt, and most of the harm in the world runs on it. This piece is about the hot form, because that's the one you can catch in yourself. But the move is the same in both: real people, kept out of view, so the harm doesn't register as harm.

## 4. What contempt is for

Now look at what you do with that.

Picture them in front of you, and look at what you're showing them with your eyes. Is it just anger? Just *I will stop you?* Or is there something darker in it, something that says: *you are less than the rest of us, and you've lost the right to be treated otherwise.* Is there some part of you that wants that to land? Not just to be heard, but to register, deep inside them?

If there is, that's contempt. It isn't the judgement that they did wrong; you can judge a wrong, and stop it, and serve justice, without a trace of it. Contempt is the need for them to *know what they are*.

Now notice who that message is addressed to. It only stings a person who can still tell the difference between being counted and being written off. A rock can't be insulted. Someone with nothing good left in them couldn't be either; there'd be nobody in there to receive it. So the message is aimed, precisely, at the part of them that still knows what fairness is. It says *there's nothing good in you* to the only part of them that could hear it.

Stay with that, because it's the whole thing in one move. The part that can hear it *is* the good in them. What else would the good in a person be, if not the part that knows other people matter? Contempt finds that part, speaks to it directly, and tells it it doesn't exist, so that it will go quiet and stop interfering. That's what you're doing when you make the verdict land: you're talking the good in someone into hiding. If you want to know what you've always meant by *evil*, it isn't harm. Harm can be an accident. It's this: finding the good in a person and smothering it, while it looks at you.

Now ask what it's like to be on the receiving end of that look. Who wouldn't dig in? Wouldn't you? A verdict on what you are doesn't make you sorry. It makes you dig in, and worse. (Being told hard what you *did* is a different thing, and we'll come to it.) If you'd have dug in, you had a way to know they would. So notice how it feels when they do. Like relief. *There it is. Exactly what I'd expect from someone like that.* As if you'd finally caught hold of a piece of what's wrong with the world, and you weren't going to let it get away. And then you use their digging in as the proof. You made the bed and then pointed at them lying in it.

That's a rigged test. It's set up so that they fail, and I'm going to claim their failing is what you were after. If that sounds too strong, here's a way to check. There is nothing more satisfying than punishing evil; pause and feel that, whether you'd endorse it or recoil from it. Now say you could do one of two things to this person. Deliver the punishment they so richly deserve. Or give them a pill that ends the threat by making them see what they did, with no punishment at all. Forget the right answer. Is there a flicker in you itching for the first button? There is in me. And that flicker is after something that justice doesn't need. Stopping them doesn't need it. Their victims don't need it. It wants them to hurt, on its own account, and it would be a little disappointed if they simply got better.

Listen to what you tell yourself about it: *they made their bed. They chose this. I'm only making sure they can't wriggle out. Sure, it's a bit underhanded, but theirs is real darkness and mine is the good kind, only there to fight it. You can't be soft on evil.* If you could send them the message out loud, it would be: *I'm going to provoke you into showing your worst, and then use it against you, and I'll get away with it, because even I won't know what I did.*

What makes it work is something you've met already: limbo, aimed at a person. You don't let yourself know it fully. You keep just enough of it out of view that you can feel righteous, and just enough in view that you can aim the look, predict the reaction, and hold it against them. It has to be this way. If it were open malice, they could name it and answer it, and you couldn't have sent it at all, for the reason section 2 found. When I ask myself whether my contempt ever made anything better, the answer that arrives first is *well, who can really say?* It sounds reasonable. It took me years to notice that it's the shrug I hide behind, and that I'd never accept it from the person I was despising. See whether it arrives for you.

And they can often see it. They don't read your mind; they recognize the shape, because they know what it is to look away and keep a reason handy. So they can see that you're doing something, that you've arranged not to know it, and that nothing they say will get through, because you'll turn any accusation around: *how dare you, you're just projecting.* What are they going to say? *He's being mean?* You both know the shape of it. Neither of you can prove any of it. And that's exactly what contempt needs.

And before you file this under things we do to enemies: we send it to the people we love. The *what's wrong with you?* that needs them to know they're broken. The sigh that tells them what we really think of them, dressed up to make us the reasonable one. The look on their face that says it landed, which we'll pretend not to have seen.

Most of the contempt you carry, though, is for people who will never see your face: the politician, the celebrity, the stranger in the comment section. There's no room, and nobody digs in because of you. So who receives it? Two parties. The people around you, who learn from your face how much of a person the enemy is, and pass it on. And you: each time it goes out, the part of you that knows the enemy is a person gets overruled once more, and it's the same part you use on everyone. The test works at a distance too. If he quietly got better tomorrow, relief or robbed?

So this is what contempt is. It exists to exploit the fact that we all know how this works and can all pretend we don't. It's a way to do to someone, with a look, what you'd never be caught doing, and to have them know it, and to have nobody able to say so.

## 5. What all of this points to

Put the pieces on the table.

- You can recognize limbo in yourself: doing wrong while knowing, and hiding the knowing.
- Your knowing survived the wrong; it's how you can see it now.
- You know how to wound someone with a look, and you know what the look will make them do.
- They can tell what you're doing, without proof, because they know it from inside.
- You have to feel righteous while doing it; open malice wouldn't work.
- You use the reaction you caused as evidence of what they are.
- You read your own wrongs differently from theirs: as things you did, not proof of what you are. (If you don't, if you're the type who reads them as proof, that's the same move aimed inward, and it comes up at the end.)

What single explanation covers every one of these? Try the alternatives first, and one of them deserves more than a sentence.

*You were honestly correcting bad behavior.* Then why does their getting worse feel like being proven right, instead of like failure?

*You didn't know what you were doing.* Then how was the look aimed so well, the reaction predicted so exactly, the evidence collected so fast?

*You're just cruel.* Cruelty wants the hurt. It doesn't need the hurt to feel like justice.

*Everyone has a double standard.* True, but a double standard doesn't explain why the message lands, or why they can see through you without a word being said.

*Some people are just different in kind.* Then why do you do the same thing they do, and why does your contempt only work by relying on the part of them that isn't different?

And the serious one: *we're social animals, and this is status.* Contempt marks somebody as demoted. The pleasure when they dig in is a coalition confirmed; your side now sees them as you do. The righteousness is reputation management, for the group and for yourself as your own audience. And the self-deception never suppressed anything; the inconvenient belief just never got formed, because forming it was costly. This explains a great deal, and I don't think it's wrong. I think it describes how the looking-away gets done. Where it fails, in my own case, is the confession. When I finally owned what I'd done, it didn't feel like forming a belief for the first time. It felt like something I'd been stepping around. You can call that hindsight, and I can't prove it isn't. That's why what follows is the best explanation I can find, and not a proof.

Here is the explanation that covers all of it. There is one thing in both of you that registers another person as mattering, and neither of you can switch it off; you can only look away from it. Wrongdoing doesn't come from a second thing that wants harm. It comes from turning away from the first thing, and it has to keep borrowing the first thing the whole time: to aim, to hide, to feel justified, to be received. The darkness has no equipment of its own. Take away the knowing and there's nothing left to turn away from, and no wrong that could be done.

Two claims have been running through this, and stated side by side they look circular:

1. Your contempt is evil because they're redeemable, and you know it, and you're making them worse.
2. You know they're redeemable because you know exactly how a person who isn't rotten does evil. You did it.

Each is something you can check on its own. Neither is derived from the other. Together they are the thing that needs explaining, and one explanation covers both: there's nobody in this who is made of darkness, and contempt is the trick by which we hide that from ourselves, provoke the darkness in someone else, and call the result a discovery.

## 6. Why you can't calculate your way to goodness

Go back to section 1 for a moment. The part of you that knew it was wrong couldn't say how it knew. The part that could give reasons didn't know. That's not a quirk of one bad night. It's the shape of the thing: the part that knows can't prove, and the part that proves doesn't know. Every calculation you'll ever do about whether you're in the right is done by the second part, from wherever you already are. So a calculation can't tell you whether you've looked away. The looking-away happened before the calculating started, and the calculating was what it produced. And there's a tell, which you can use today: when you're acting from contempt, notice how *sure* you are that it's good. The real thing doesn't feel like that. It doesn't need to be sure.

You might want a rule out of this. *Never treat anyone with contempt.* *Always keep the person in view.* The trouble is that any rule can be followed while turned away. You can say the words, do the deeds, and hold the person out of view the whole time; the same sentence can come from a person turned toward you or away from you, and it sounds the same. This is what the reasons were doing in section one. They weren't there to persuade the part of you that knew. They were there to persuade *you* that the part that knew was just one more voice.

So the check can't be *can I justify this?* You always can. The check is closer to: *am I willing, right now, to let this whole person, and what I'm doing to them, come into view?* And notice that checking is the same act as fixing. You can't find out whether you're turned away without turning toward. The test changes what it measures.

What's left after you turn toward may look the same from outside. You may still stop them, still be fierce, still refuse to let it slide. But it's no longer contempt, and both of you will be able to tell. You've probably been on the receiving end of both.

Think of two times you were caught doing something you shouldn't have: cut a line, took credit, made the cruel remark. Two different people catching you. The first lets you know what you are. Not what you did; what you are. Nothing they say could be quoted against them, since the words are all about the behavior. But you can feel it, and you dig in, and you leave a little worse: a little more sure that this is the kind of world where people look out for themselves. The second is, if anything, harsher. Says more, in front of more people, leaves you less room to wriggle. And it lands as something else: a reminder that you're better than this. Which one do you remember, and what did you do afterward? With the second, most of us don't dig in. We leave a little more ourselves.

Almost the same words; opposite results. Nothing in any record would show the difference, and both of them walk away sure that what they did worked, because it did: you stopped. Only the first has something he'll never look at: the flicker of pleasure when it landed. You felt it go by. He needed you to. That's the difference between kindness and niceness, and it's why the opposite of contempt was never being nice. The second person wasn't nice to you. They were turned toward you, and it let you look at what you'd done.

## 7. The world it builds

None of this stays between two people. They dig in, and now they've learned something: that the good people do this too, that the world runs on it, that the part of them that knew better is naive. So they do it to the next person. Groups form around shared enemies. Institutions keep the categories. And the world gets a little darker, in a way nobody planned and nobody can be blamed for, which is why it never stops.

Then we look at that world and say: see how dark it is. Contempt doesn't just describe a dark world. It builds one, and then cites the world it built as proof.

Why does it keep coming back? Because we can't stop wanting to be good. And if we don't trust that goodness is already what we are, we have to manufacture it, and the cheapest way to manufacture goodness is by contrast. *I'm good because I'm not like him.* That requires a him. It requires him to stay what he is. Which is what the contempt was for all along.

Follow that one step further, because it says what the way out has to be. As long as your goodness is something you have to secure, you'll need somebody worse to secure it against, and you'll need them to stay that way. No amount of trying to be good gets you out of that, because trying to be good is itself a way of securing it. The only goodness that doesn't need securing is one that's already yours. And section 5 already found it: the thing in you that registers other people as mattering, the thing you couldn't switch off and could only look away from. That was never something you earned. It's what's there when you stop looking away. So the only way to stop spreading this is to recognize that, as something you check rather than believe: when you're fully turned toward someone, is the goodness something you're doing, or something that's simply there?

That's why this can't be done by trying harder, and why it isn't hopeless either. What's yours is how fast you catch yourself looking away: days, then hours, then sometimes in the moment. That improves with practice, and it's the only part that does. And one more thing, so that this isn't mistaken for a promise: seeing someone fully tells you they matter. It doesn't tell you what to do. You can be turned all the way toward a person and still be wrong about everything else. Kindness isn't wisdom. It's only the one stance from which wisdom has a chance.


## 8. What this comes to

Three things. You've seen the first two from inside; the third is what they imply.

Contempt is evil. I don't mean that as a verdict on anyone, least of all you. I mean it as an identification: the thing you've always meant by the word, the thing you saw in that person's eyes and couldn't let go of, is what you were sending back. It makes people worse, and if the flicker was there, that's what it was after: a worse enemy is proof you were right, and you're left free to say you were only trying to help.

Kindness is its opposite, and it's the one stance that never itself feeds the thing. It isn't niceness. Nice is what you get from someone who needs you to like them; kind is what you get from someone who needs you to be all right. The second person who caught you wasn't nice. Kindness is keeping the person in view while doing whatever has to be done, and it's the only stance that doesn't feed the thing, because it's the only one that doesn't need anybody to stay lost.

And pretending you don't know this is the darkness. So is pretending that kindness means being nice, so that you can call your contempt "not being nice." So is the most common contempt of all, the one aimed at yourself: *you are what you did.* It feels like accountability, and it produces what contempt always produces, which is hiding. And so is the search for the exception, the one person so far gone that contempt is finally warranted. If you find you want there to be one, that wanting is worth a look, because it's the same wanting this piece has been about.

So the way out isn't a better moral system. It's to stop hiding. You didn't know the theory. But you knew the truth well enough to violate it precisely. You knew equal standing well enough to know how its withdrawal would wound. You knew the goodness in the other person well enough to know how to drive it into hiding. And you knew darkness well enough to call it forth and recognize it when it came. Nothing here is new information. It's the knowledge your behavior has been using the whole time.

# Part II: A frame you can take or leave

Most of Part I was checked in your own case, and none of it depends on what follows. What follows is a way of picturing the thing, which is mine. I'll say why I use it, and then you can decide.

## 9. What do we call a thing like that?

Go back over what you watched in section 4 and put it in one list. It aimed the look, anticipated their reaction, and had the evidence ready. It hid from you while showing itself to them. When you came close to catching it (in section 1, every time somebody got near the truth), it pushed back, and it got stronger, not weaker, the closer you came. It was working toward something you would never choose, a darker world. And while it had you, it felt entirely like you.

Now: what do we call a thing that lives off a host, hides from it, works against what the host wants, and defends itself when exposed?

A parasite.

I don't mean a literal creature, and nothing above depends on there being one. I mean that this is how the thing behaves, and the word fits the behavior better than any other. There's even a plain reason an absence would act like a presence. A looking-away can only last while it goes unseen. So anything that comes close to exposing it has to be pushed off, and pushing off takes aim. It doesn't need a mind of its own to act as if it had one.

## 10. Why the frame is worth using

Here's the strongest reason, and it's the one thing in this part you can check: look at what happens when you don't use it.

There are two ways not to. The first is to say the darkness in someone is simply *them*: they are what they did, they want it, that's who they are. You've watched what that does. It's the verdict from section 4, and you've watched what holding it does to you. The second is to say the darkness is nothing much, a lapse, an absence, nothing to fight. That gets you niceness, and someone deep in it will use your niceness against you.

The frame is what's left between those two. It lets you say: there is something in this person that has to be fought, with force if it takes force, and it isn't the person. That's a hard thing to hold without a picture, and the picture is what makes it holdable. So the parasite isn't a fact I'm asking you to accept about anyone. It's a stance, and you've now watched what its alternatives do to you.

It can fail in the other direction too. If you merely *believe* somebody is in the grip of something, without seeing it, you'll go soft, and you'll be wrong. The only thing that works is to actually see it: the looking-away as looking-away, and what's under it. Under every one I've found in myself there has been fear, of what I might turn out to be if I looked. And how much of that you can see in another person seems to track how free you are of your own.

## 11. They work together

Now the part of the picture that Part I couldn't reach.

When you sent that look, it wasn't only yours at work. You went after that person in the first place because something in them answered something in you. Their darkness provoked yours; yours provoked theirs back; and afterward both were stronger and both were better hidden from their hosts. It's as if the two of them shook hands underneath the two of you.

Watch it at scale. Two people, or two sides, at war, each certain the darkness lives in the other. Both grow. Nobody ends up less sure. Martin Luther King said darkness cannot drive out darkness, and the reason is that it was never trying to. Its purpose is to provoke the darkness on the other side. It doesn't care who you hate, as long as you hate.

Which also tells you what you're feeling when you feel contempt. It isn't only the sign that you've spotted evil, though you have. It's a tug, to be recruited into the thing you're looking at, and it uses your accurate perception of it as the hook.

Picture a locker room. Ten men, gathered around. One makes an off-color joke, testing the water. Nobody objects, and a space opens in which each of them is a little bolder than he would have been alone. The wrong person walks in and it shuts. Nobody keeps a record of what just happened. And if somebody had objected with contempt, that would have fed every one of them, the objector included.

Most contempt isn't even performed. It's consumed. Somebody getting exactly what they had coming, delivered with relish: it feels good, and it wants more. That's the thing, watching. It's why this wrong, out of all of them, spreads: it leaves no record, it arrives dressed as duty, it's rewarded in public, and it's pleasant to watch. Comment sections, the shared post, the outrage we pass around. Each one is nothing, and together they set how dark everybody assumes the world is.

## 12. Why they all want the same thing

Why would separate parasites want the same thing? Because the darkness has no substance of its own. It's a looking-away, and two lookings-away have nothing in them to differ by. That's why looking into the eyes of two very different "evil" people can give the uncanny sense of looking at the same thing, and why every tradition that looked hard ended up naming one adversary. Mara. The Devil. They were seeing something real. Their one mistake was to grant it substance.

So there's a paradox to hold with both hands: it isn't real, and it must be fought. Treat it as a being and you get contempt. Treat it as nothing and you get complicity. Held correctly, the picture explains the oldest suspicion people have about the world, that something is running things from behind the scenes. Something is. It doesn't need a single conspirator. It's the same looking-away, running through everyone, with everyone convinced they're fighting it.

## 13. The person at the bottom

One last thing the frame lets you see, and it's the one that matters most for what you do next.

Bring back the worst person you can think of. The part of us that would notice we've gone wrong is the part we've turned away from, so the further gone someone is, the less of it they can hear. That much is obvious. What the picture adds, and this is the picture talking, is that the knowing doesn't shrink. It grows, because there's more harm for it to register. The most lost person is carrying *more* of that knowledge than you are, and holding it down harder, with more of himself committed to the holding.

So when you look at such a person and feel that particular horror, ask what you're looking at. It isn't a hole where a person should be. It's something under pressure, pressing to get out: a conscience under crushing load, showing through. The thing holding it down is using your contempt to help; every verdict you send presses on the one part of him that could end this. You think you're looking at a monster showing you what it is. You're looking at a hostage, held up to a window.

And the hostage isn't struggling, because from inside, the thing holding him doesn't feel like a captor. It feels like his judgment, his seriousness about what's wrong with the world. That's why the people who seem to want their darkness are the ones most fully taken. Being cruel to him is siding with his captor, and the urge to do it comes from yours.

That's why, on this picture, "they need the most love" isn't sentiment. It's what follows. They're carrying the most, under the most pressure, with the least access to relief, and every instinct we have adds to the load.

If you can't use the picture, drop it, and keep Part I.
