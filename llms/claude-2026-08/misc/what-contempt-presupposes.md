# What contempt presupposes

*For the reader who wants the argument laid out. Nothing in the book rests on this page; the book rests on what you found when you looked. But what you found has a shape: three things you checked, two things that best explain them, and everything else following. Here it is in that order. The last section says where the traditions come in, and it's marked, because the deduction reaches the door and doesn't go through it.*

## Three things you checked

**O1. Intending harm to someone requires holding them out of view.** Chapter 1, The How. You tried to give a stranger the gaze and couldn't. Not a rule, not a decision; you could see them, and the intention lost its footing. To keep it, something had to be excluded. That's the direction that holds without qualification. The converse holds with a purpose attached: when a person is held out of view so that something the view would stop can go ahead, the holding-out and the permission are one event seen from two sides. The airport crowd isn't held out; it's in the background, and comes forward when somebody cries. People also turn from each other to escape grief, or fear, or a claim they can't meet, and that isn't this page's subject.

**O2. Holding someone out of view takes work, and the work rises under exposure.** The Tell and The Loop. The stranger tracked your every exit. Pointed at, the behavior didn't get embarrassed; it intensified in the direction needed to stay hidden. And your own case, from When You Send It: the flash had to be aimed, had to land, and had to go unrecorded, and none of that was calculated. It was there in one motion.

**O3. Contempt lands as a knowingly disavowed double standard.** The Righteous Appearance and the Taunt. When it landed on you, you didn't only feel threatened or disapproved of. You recognized that he was doing the thing he condemned you for, that he meant you to feel it, and that he'd deny it if you named it. That's what made it maddening rather than merely hostile. And notice what he was appealing to. The sting needed you to recognize the violation, which is a capacity; and the verdict said you were the kind of thing that has no such capacity. He was addressing the very thing he was denying was there. And from the sending end, in When You Send It, you counted on exactly that recognition: for it to sting the way you needed, they had to read it as the real thing. A threat, a status blow, a loss of face would each explain a bad reaction. None of them explains hypocrisy, and hypocrisy is what you felt.

Those three are Tier I, in the sense the book uses: each is a claim about experience that you checked in your own case, and each is open to a reader who looked and found otherwise. Everything below is what they imply, and it is only as strong as they are.

## Two assumptions, and why they're the best explanation

**A1. Contact carries care.** When you see another person, you recognize them as like you and want them to be all right, before any thought. Chapter 2's opening is the evocation. O1 is the evidence: if seeing someone were neutral, harming them wouldn't require breaking contact first. The fact that it does means the seeing has a direction. Care isn't something added to perception. It's what perception of another is like when nothing is in the way.

**A2. The care isn't removed by turning away. It's obscured.** O2 is the evidence. Nobody builds machinery that elaborate to keep something from a part of themselves that doesn't care. If the care were gone, there would be nothing to hide from, and the hiding would stop. It doesn't stop; it escalates. So the thing turned away from is still there, doing what it does, and the size of the hiding measures it.

A rival explanation exists and should be named. Evolved moral emotions plus the drive to reduce dissonance would also predict O1 and O2: we flinch at harming what we see, and we rationalize what we've done. The deduction below doesn't need to beat that rival. It needs only that the care is present and obscured, which the rival grants under other names. Where the rival and the traditions part is over what the care is, and that question is held to the end.

## What follows

**(a) Turning away from them is turning away from yourself.** By A1, the care is in the seeing. So to hold them out of view is to hold your own caring out of view, which is what "turning away from your conscience" has meant since The How. One act, not two. This is why a dehumanizing gaze reads as someone absent from themselves: the same absence, seen from outside.

And with O1 it gives the book its definition of evil. Every intended harm is done from that state, and there is no other state it can be done from. So at the level of the person, "evil" in this book names no substance and no essence. It names the turning: your own caring, held out of your own view, so that theirs can be held out too. You can only do this to them by first losing it in yourself. There is no other way in.

**(b) Limbo, and its asymmetry.** By A2, the turned-away person still knows what he knows. So intended harm can only be carried out from a particular state: enough contact with what he knows for something to be held away, and not enough ownership for that knowledge to stop the act. That's the form of "knew and didn't let himself know," and it holds for any intended harm. Contempt needs a more specific version of it: enough operative knowledge to aim the verdict, predict the reaction, and use it as evidence, without enough ownership to recognize that the evidence was planted. Both halves are forced. Full ownership would make the planted evidence worthless to him; no operative knowledge would leave him unable to aim, and embarrassed when caught rather than escalating. And the state is hidden from him for the same reason it's visible to you: the faculty that would show it to him is the one he's turned, and yours is intact. What he hides was never the deed. It's what the deed reveals: his sameness to the person he condemns.

**(c) The state shows.** By (a), what's absent in him is the seeing. And you can tell whether somebody is seeing you only because you're seeing them; that's the faculty that reads it. So his absence is what you perceive, with the very faculty he's turned away from. No message is needed for this. It's what a turned-away face is.

**(d) Contempt.** Turning away, made legible to its target on purpose, carrying the verdict *this is all you are*, under a righteous disavowal. The cold form shows without being aimed. Contempt aims, because the sender needs the verdict to land. The disavowal is forced by (b): he can't own it, so he denies it, and the denial isn't a tactic but the shape of the state.

**(e) Protest becomes proof.** Also from (b). A sender in that state reads reactions as confirmation, because the evidence he's collecting is the evidence he's producing, and he can't see the second half. So any answer the target gives is used, and both of them can count on that in advance.

**(f) The target can be moved.** By A2, the faculty is present in the target, obscured, and turnable. That's what the sender is counting on: the message is meant to push them further away. But the faculty that can be pushed further is the faculty that could have turned back. A rock doesn't need provoking. To be pushed from the light, you have to be somebody the light was available to.

Call a person **fundamentally innocent** if they can harm only by turning away, and can turn. Not harmless, and not incapable of harm. By A1 and A2, everyone the book calls redeemable is this, and only this.

## The turn

Start with what the act presupposes directly, before any interpretation. By O3, for contempt to land the target has to recognize the same turning away in the sender that the sender condemns in them, and feel it as a double standard. That requires two things of the target: that they can still tell a turned-away face from a looking one, and that the standard being violated is one they hold. And the sender counts on both. So the act, on its own, presupposes shared standing: both people recognize the same move as intelligible in each other. And it presupposes that the target is not exhausted by the verdict, because its effect depends on a capacity the verdict says they don't have. Its explicit message denies equality. Its successful operation presupposes it. That much doesn't need A1 or A2.

Now add them, and the shared recognition takes a shape. By A1, what still tells turning away from looking in the target is the care itself, since the care is in the seeing. By A2, it's present and obscured, not gone. By (f), it can be moved, and the sender is counting on that too. So the message *this is all you are* can function only by addressing the part of them that knows it isn't. If nothing in them still made the distinction, there would be no one for the message to reach and no conscience for it to corrupt. Contempt works only on the fundamentally innocent, in the sense defined above, and on nobody else.

The sender relies on all of this holding in the target. Not as a theory he holds; as what the act runs on, known the way (b) says he knows things, enough to use and not enough to own. And the target relies on the same in the sender, since reading the state is reading a faculty of the same kind, turned. That's why the knowledge is common and not merely shared. This isn't a ladder of propositions that either of them climbs. The levels arrive together, in the recognition of an intention: to receive something as a message is already to recognize that the sender meant you to recognize it and was counting on your doing so. And none of it needs to be provable to a third party. Contempt can sometimes be shown plainly; what its operation requires is deniability between the two of them, and the deniability in (d) is what it runs on.

The act presupposes what it denies. Not as a by-product, and not as evidence a careful observer could collect afterward. As a condition of its possibility. Contempt can portray darkness as someone's nature only by exploiting the shared knowledge that it isn't.

## The title

This book was originally titled *Contempt Is Evil*, and here is the sentence in the form the deduction gives it.

By (a), evil at the level of the person names the turning: your own caring held out of view so that theirs can be. By (d), contempt is that turning made legible to its target, on purpose, with the verdict attached. So contempt isn't a response to evil, or a symptom of it, or a lesser cousin. It's the thing itself, made into a message. That's the identification, and it isn't a judgment of anyone.

Two things the word usually carries, and both are wrong, and you already know they're wrong. The first is size. We reserve "evil" for the extreme, but watch what cutting in line does to you. The cost is thirty seconds. The heat is out of all proportion to thirty seconds, because what you're reacting to isn't the cost. It's the exception: somebody put you out of view to get ahead, and let you see it. Chapter 1 found the same thing from inside, in the flash that was shameful to admit out of proportion to its size; the smallest morsel carries the whole. The second is outcomes. We think "bad" is about damage done, but a friend who forgets your birthday and a friend who makes one small cutting remark have done unequal damage in the wrong order, and you know which one you're still thinking about a week later. The corpse case has no damage at all and everyone knows it's wrong. Two acts identical from outside can have opposite effects. What you're tracking, in every one of those, is the turning, and you were tracking it before this book named it.

And O3 says you already knew it. For the message to sting the way you needed, they had to read it as the real thing: not harshness, which the second stranger had more of and which landed as a reminder, but a person putting them out of view and letting them watch. The cover, *mine is the good kind*, is refuted not by the fact that it stung, since a clean confrontation can sting too, but by what they recognized in the sting: the turning away, the intention that it land, and the disavowal. When you checked from the sending end, you found the same three. If it had been the good kind, there would have been nothing of that shape to recognize, and it would have landed as his did. So every time your contempt worked, both of you were relying on a shared recognition of what it was, and what it was is the thing the word has always pointed at. The title isn't a claim the book asks you to accept. It's a claim your own contempt has been counting on.

## A prediction, and what supports it

If contempt were an increasingly accurate perception of badness, then the clearer the observer, the more of it they'd hold toward worse people, or at least the more they'd converge on it. If the verdict is the sender's own turning away showing itself, clarity should do the opposite: preserve or sharpen the perception of the harm while dissolving the verdict. The wise person sees the wrongdoing at least as clearly as you do and sees less essence, not less harm.

The second is what we see. Two strangers looked at one act, and one delivered the verdict and one didn't; the act didn't decide it. The people we call evil hold the most contempt, not the least; contempt for their victims is how the harm was done. And the people we'd call wise are the ones who can look at the worst person, seeing everything you see, without the verdict forming, not because they're soft, but because nothing in them needs it. That last one is intuitive rather than measured, and I'm not going to dress it up. But it's a differential prediction, and the perception account gets it backwards.

The same gradient is the fear account in Chapter 16 from the outside. The more a person has turned away, the more feared essence there is to put somewhere, and the more faces it needs.

## What that settles

Contempt sets out to prove one thing: *this is what you are.* Given what you checked in O1 to O3, and A1 and A2 as the explanation, its machinery implies the opposite, and has to. If darkness exhausted anyone's nature, neither the target's original act nor the sender's reply would have needed a turning away; both would have been done while looking. Both required one. What this rules out is the verdict's exact claim, that this is *all* they are. That there might be care and a harmful disposition side by side, both intrinsic, isn't ruled out here; Chapter 4's argument, that the darkness has no equipment of its own, is what goes further. That's Chapter 4's argument, that the darkness has no equipment of its own, reached from the other side: not by inspecting the stranger and finding only borrowed faculties, but by noticing that his act could only work if there was nothing else there to find.

And it settles one more thing, which the book has said in several places without deriving it. Chapter 1 ends with the part that knows "patiently waiting to be remembered." The map says you don't build it and you don't earn it; you remember it. The trip in Chapter 2 says the revelation didn't feel like new knowledge. Here is why that has to be so. Whatever recognition recognizes, when the turning back happens, must already have been there in limbo, because contempt was already using it. What turning back adds is nothing. It makes explicit, at the level of the person, what was already being counted on between the two of them. The second stranger did that once, between two people, without an argument.

## The finale, with its seams

Here is the sentence the whole page is for, and then the seams in it.

Every time your contempt landed, you were relying on the person in front of you not being exhausted by their darkness: on their recognition, and their responsiveness, still being there to reach. A1 and A2 say what remained is care, present and obscured. The further reading says the care isn't something they have but what they are. If that reading is right, then the book's central premise, that nothing has to be built and the only work is turning back, isn't a hope laid over the evidence. It's the deepest account of what your own contempt was already counting on. Every act of it that worked was coherent only because you already knew this, and kept it where you couldn't own it.

The seams, one per sentence above. That you were relying on their recognition and responsiveness is Tier I: if the flash landed, they read the double standard, and you counted on their reading it, and you checked the flash. That what they recognized it with is care is the A1 and A2 reading, which you checked as the best explanation of O1 and O2 but did not observe. That the care is their nature, rather than a disposition they happen to have, is my reading further still, offered in the last section, with its evidence in Appendix 3. Why the reading isn't arbitrary: it makes a prediction the other reading can't, that contempt tracks the sender; it matches the shape four traditions reached by other roads; and it survives the hardest case so far. And two things the sentence does not say. It doesn't say the turning is easy, or that not-seeing can be reversed by deciding to see; what's trained is how fast you catch it. And it doesn't say the turning is enough. Being free of self-deception makes you honest, not wise, and what to do from there is a second skill. What it says is only this: everything you'd need was already in the room, because you were using it.

## The correspondence, marked

*From here I'm saying what I believe about what the care is. The deduction above reaches this door and stops. Nothing practical depends on going through it.*

Anyone who practices will have seen the shape several paragraphs ago, and Appendix 3 names the correspondences one by one. Stated against the argument: A1 is the claim that compassion is the nature of mind, not a quality some minds have. A2 is the teaching that the obscurations are adventitious; they never touch the nature they obscure. Turning away has the shape of *marigpa*, non-recognition: not a substance, and not a faculty of its own. And the turning back is recognition: revealed, not created, because there was never anything to create.

What the deduction adds to those, and it's the reason this page exists: the traditions arrived at them by practice or revelation, and this book arrives at their shape from the anatomy of a bad act. Contempt is coherent only if the sender already holds, in limbo, that the target's nature is care, that it's obscured rather than absent, and that it can be turned back. That is the soteriology, held implicitly, by the one person who'd deny it most. And the further claim, that awakening is awareness turning back toward itself, is on this account the same event as the implicit becoming explicit: what was common knowledge between two people at the level of what's looking becomes sayable at the level of the person. Appendix 1's Part Three says this in the vocabulary of common knowledge, and the anti-ritual belongs there: contempt is the act that keeps the sameness from ever being said while both go on knowing it.

Now the limit, stated plainly, because this is where I'd overclaim. The deduction gives *present, obscured, movable*. It does not by itself give "care is intrinsic to awareness," as against an evolved disposition of human beings that could have been otherwise and could be missing in some. A naturalist can accept every step above under the second reading. So I offer the first reading lightly. What supports it, and the test that would count against it, are in Appendix 3, under "What's mine," where the traditions' claim and the book's road to it sit side by side.

What decides it, in the end, isn't the argument. It's what you find under your own hiding when the hiding stops, and the book can only take you to where you can look.

One more thing I don't claim, because I don't believe it in the strong form. Not that the awarenesses are one. Only that nothing distinguishes them in nature: whatever is looking out of you and whatever is looking out of him have nothing in them to differ by, which is all the mutual recognition in (c) requires and all Part Three uses.

## What is not claimed

Not that turning away is sufficient for harm. It also produces numbness, avoidance, and paralysis, with nobody harmed. The direction that holds without qualification is that harm or use requires it. The converse holds with the purpose clause attached, which is how O1 states it.

Not the cold form. The turn is about contempt, the aimed form. The general rule for harm and use rests on O1, checked in both directions in The How, not on this page.

Not universality, from this page. O1 to O3 are claims about experience, stated as holding for everyone and open to anyone who looked and found otherwise; the interlude says what that finding would count for. The deduction itself needs one working instance, and your own, from When You Send It, is enough for it: if the flash landed, the structure held in that room, for both of you. That it holds in every room is what the rest of the book argues, case by case, and this page doesn't.

Not that every target can be reached in practice. In principle, (f) entails it: the faculty is there to be turned. What the page says nothing about is how much has to be true around a person before turning back is survivable, and Chapter 12 holds that for some it may not happen in a lifetime. Movable in principle is not the same as near.
