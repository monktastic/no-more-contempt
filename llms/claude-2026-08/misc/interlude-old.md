# Interlude: Where This Is Going

_A note for early readers. Everything up to the end of Chapter 4 was built so you could check it against your own memory. What follows is a map of the rest of the book: the claims I still have to earn, in roughly the order I plan to earn them. Some of them you'll be able to verify the same way. Some are inferences. A few are simply how I've come to hold the whole picture, and I'll mark those when we get to them. I'm laying it all out here so you can see the whole shape before deciding whether the pieces work. This interlude will not be in the finished book._

## What we have so far

Four things are in place. Everything else rests on them.

First, **limbo**. You have done things you knew were wrong while not letting yourself know it. The knowledge wasn't missing. You were turned away from it. And the turning away wasn't idle: it steered you around anything that might have exposed it, including your own attention.

Second, **contempt is what limbo looks like when it's aimed at someone**. The first stranger wasn't harsher than the second. He was doing to you what you had been doing a moment earlier: acting from limbo. Judging you from the very state he was judging you for. And it produced exactly what it accused you of. Every road he left open funneled into proof that you were what he needed you to be.

Third, **the part of him doing this was hidden from him and shown to you, and those were one act, not two**. It could only work if you recognized it, and it could only continue if he didn't. It benefits from being seen as _him_. That's how it keeps you from seeing it as anything else, and how it keeps him from seeing it at all.

Fourth, **our darknesses collaborate**. Mine gives yours evidence that the world is dark and you'd better look out for yourself. Yours gives mine evidence that you're beyond help. Two enemies, one handshake, both leaving more certain than ever that the problem lives in the other one.

And one thing to hold onto through everything that follows: you are fully responsible, _and_ the harmful thing is not what you are. The mind wants to drop one side. Keep both. If you can't, everything after this will either feel like an attack or like a pardon, and it's neither.

Here is what they're for.

---

## 1. The counterfeit

There are two things the word "righteous" can mean, and the whole trap lives in the gap between them.

The first is a feeling you already know. There is a part of you that wants nothing more than to be of service to what's alive: to protect it, to defend it, to keep it from harm. You can feel it when you're connected to it. If you want to locate it, think of the people who have most restored your faith in humanity. That's what you were recognizing in them.

The second is an _idea_ of that. "The best way to serve life is to destroy these particular people, and here's why." It arrives with the same word attached and, at first, most of the same feeling.

What the book will show is how the first turns into the second beneath your notice. Not because they look alike. Side by side, they don't. It's that you can only choose the counterfeit by forgetting what the real one feels like. The way someone can only choose junk food if they've never been offered real food. And the further you follow the counterfeit, the less access you have to the original, so the less able you are to compare them. Eventually there's nothing to compare it _to_. That isn't a failure of judgment. It's a trance.

One thing you can check right now, before we get there: the next time you're acting from contempt, notice how _sure_ you are that it's good. The real thing doesn't have that quality. It doesn't need to be sure. It's just doing what's in front of it.

---

## 2. Why you can't calculate your way to goodness

Recall from Chapter 1 that one side had to argue and the other didn't. The arguments were never there to convince your conscience; that doesn't work. They were there to convince _you_ that conscience was just one more voice in the noise.

Now scale that up.

Every grand plan for doing good—every ideology, every agenda, every "I'll accumulate wealth and power now and deploy it for the world later"—is built by the arguing side. Which is the side whose job is to route around what you already know. So when you ask "is this good?" and your mind hands you a proof, you haven't learned whether it's good. You've learned that the part of you that manufactures justifications has been put to work.

That's why, after every disaster, someone asks "how were we supposed to know?" You could have known. Something in you did. The proof existed so that you wouldn't have to listen to it.

Several things follow from this, and each will get its own treatment.

We build rules and moral systems less to make ourselves good than to let ourselves stop paying attention. It's easier to follow a rule than to stay fully present to what a situation is asking of you. Rules let you say "I did the right thing" without feeling the weight of it. That's their appeal, and it's why goodness can't be formalized: any definition of "good" can be obeyed to the letter while betraying its spirit, and the dark part is extraordinarily good at finding the letter.

There's a small example I keep coming back to. Ask a room of hardened soldiers whether it's wrong to desecrate an enemy's corpse. They'll say yes, nearly all of them, instantly. Then ask them what the harm is. The person is dead. Nobody's watching. No rule is being enforced. They won't be able to tell you, and they won't budge either. That's what conscience looks like when you catch it in the act: it knows, it can't prove, and it doesn't need to.

The belief that metrics can tell us whether we're on the right track is the same belief, moved outside the skull. Inside, it's the belief that your thoughts can tell you whether you're doing good.

Here's the test I'll offer for any grand narrative: does it require you to have contempt for what's right in front of you—this person, this place, this moment—in service of some future good? If so, it is almost certainly not coming from the part of you that cares, and it will not do what its proof says it will.

Two acts that look identical from outside can have opposite effects, because they come from different places. This is the hardest thing in the book to accept and the one everything depends on. It's also why our arguments about policy, which are arguments about the auditable stuff, so rarely touch what decides the outcome.

None of this is an argument against thinking. Thinking is indispensable, _in service_ of the part that knows; it's how conscience gets anything done. What it cannot do is replace that part, or tell you whether it's been hijacked. You need something deeper than thought to check that, and the book will say what.

I'll be accused here of defining conscience as whatever happens to agree with me, since I can't define it or prove which inner voice is which. The honest answer is that I can give you clues but not rules, and that the impossibility of a rule is the whole point. A rule is exactly what the counterfeit needs. The clues are in section 11.

And one more thing here, which I've come to think is where the civilizational picture begins. Every act of contempt teaches both people the same lesson: that the inner knowing is worthless. If good and evil can't be told apart from the outside, and the world only rewards the outside, why listen to the part that can't prove anything? That is how a culture that trusts only proof gets built, one interaction at a time. And a culture that trusts only proof is one in which the single faculty capable of detecting this whole thing has been demoted to the "spiritual," where it can be safely ignored.

## 3. How much of your life is in there

So far we've looked at one kind of moment: being caught, or being the one doing the catching. The book will now look much more widely, and I'll warn you that this part isn't comfortable.

The claim is that limbo isn't a rare exception. It's the ordinary condition of most of what we do.

Consider the feeling of an action that serves two purposes. "Yes, this benefits me, but it _also_ helps others." You can even feel the balance; it feels like a reasonable compromise. Now check whether a deeper part of you knows that the "helps others" half is the fig leaf, and the other half is the point. Not always. But far more often than we'd like.

Consider the small thrill of getting away with something, the sense of having pulled a fast one over the world. And notice that the thrill is itself the evidence: some part of you registered exactly what you did.

Consider how easy it is to see all this in your enemies and how hard in your allies, and that the difference has nothing to do with who's doing it more.

Consider how subtle it can be. Being unwilling to really hear someone's experience. "That person is stupid for feeling that way." Looking at the people around you, on some days, as though they're extras in your movie. Each of these is a turning away from the life in someone, and each carries the same signature, however faint.

And consider a move I made for years, and I suspect you have too: _I can see self-deception so clearly in other people. If I were doing it, I'd see it in myself. I don't, so I'm not._ The clarity about them became the proof about me. That's the mechanism using its own detector as cover.

The point of this section isn't to make you feel rotten. It's to establish scope. What we're about to look at—shadow spaces, contempt parties, whole industries and worldviews—only makes sense once you've seen that what happened between you and the stranger is not a special event. It's the water we swim in.

## 4. Shadow spaces and the background hum

Here's something you've felt. Someone drops a cynical comment, testing the water. Part of you instantly knows which signal to send back. If you match their frequency, something opens.

The book will call those openings **shadow spaces**: temporary alliances in which our dark parts recognize each other and coordinate, while our conscious minds keep the story clean. The guy who "forgot" some cash income on his taxes and points at corporate loopholes as though they license him. The remark about a colleague that is _testing_ something, and the reply that isn't "that's not okay" but "careful, HR's everywhere." Gossip dressed as concern. Performed virtue, with everyone quietly relieved they won't have to actually change. "Those people" moving into the neighborhood.

None of these are bad people. They coach little league. They volunteer. They have real moments of integrity. And each of them, having turned away from their conscience in one place, finds it a little easier to turn away in the next. The content is completely different. The move is identical.

Then there are the rooms where we do it _together_. The strategy meeting about the other side that slides, one defensible sentence at a time, from "we need to stop them" to "honestly, fuck these people." Relief floods the room. Finally we're being _honest_. Nobody decided to be cruel; the minutes will look fine. What happened is that our dark parts recognized each other and handled the details, while our conscious minds kept their deniability. That's the handshake from Chapter 4 at the scale of a room. And it's how war rooms work, and campaigns, and activist groups sliding from justice into vengeance.

And underneath all of it, the background hum: the casual contempt we hurl at each other all day, most of it too small to notice. Comment sections. "Play stupid games, win stupid prizes." Each one negligible on its own. Together they set the baseline: how dark everyone assumes the world is, how much everyone feels licensed to protect themselves. The executive making his harmful decision, the politician telling her lie—they're swimming in that. It has made the world just dark enough that their self-deception is easy to justify.

Now the uncomfortable part. Our small shadows _need_ the large ones. If integrity were the norm, your small acts of self-deception would stand out, and you'd feel pressure to confront them. As long as there's systemic corruption to point at, your dark part has cover. So it doesn't merely believe in corrupt elites. It needs them. Every time we point at their corruption while indulging our own, we make a trade: we keep our small shadow spaces, and in exchange we feed the large ones.

Check whether some part of you has always known this: that your contempt for them isn't putting them out of business. It's keeping them in it.

## 5. The evidence, at scale

If that's true, it should have left marks on the culture, and the book will walk through them.

It's received wisdom that humans are _fundamentally_ selfish and greedy. Does that belief make anyone kinder? Can you feel that it's sending the same message the first stranger sent—_this world is a dark place, look out for yourself_—and having the same effect? If the thing draws power from one person doing that to another, what does it draw from an entire species believing it together, as something impolite to question?

Hollywood produces revenge fantasies by the hundred, each one reminding us that irredeemable people exist and are best handled with primally satisfying violence. Did anyone need to plan that?

Wise people across every tradition tell us to have compassion for wrongdoers, and our culture treats it as naive.

And deepest of all: we see reality as a lifeless collection of stuff over which we hold dominion. Resources. Objects. Purposeless matter following meaningless laws. The industries that poison rivers and strip mountains benefit from that view, but they didn't publish a memo. It's enforced by norms far beyond their borders. The book will argue that this is contempt at the foundational level: the same turning away from the life in something, applied to everything. Contempt for a person and contempt for all of life are not received as different things by the part of us that receives them. That's not an accident. It's what lets the one turn into the other. And the collapse of the biosphere is what that looks like from outside: our self-deception, externalized.

One consequence of Chapter 1 becomes important here. The first stranger's constraint was that he could do nothing auditable. Now ask what kind of harm an institution can do. Policy, procedure, incentive, omission—channels in which nobody ever has to look at anybody. If the dark part needs channels that leave no mark, institutions are what it would build. And when someone with a great deal of unintegrated darkness operates at scale—runs a company, holds a platform, designs a system—that darkness travels through whatever they build: the contempt in their public statements, the rationalizations baked into the culture, the assumptions built into what they make, the people they hire and the incentives they set.

There's a name for this arrangement, and it's derivable straight from Chapter 1: plausible deniability. One part of him did his dirty work while another kept its hands clean and truly didn't know. Now scale that. A society has the same structure: agencies that do what the citizens would never sanction, so that the citizens never have to. The shadowy operator in the basement of the government isn't a different kind of person. He's the physical form of a part we've all disowned, doing our dirty work so we can keep our hands clean. We know, and we don't, in exactly the proportions the first stranger did. That's the bridge from the parking lot to the civilization, and the second half of the book crosses it.

This is the "conspiracy." Not a room of people planning it. Something that doesn't care who you hate, as long as you hate. Something that never fights itself, because it's only ever on its own team, and all it has to do to win is convince you to use it as a tool for your noble agenda. It drives whole populations toward situations in which everyone is violently proving how righteous they are, and nobody suspects their own part. Christians call it the Devil. Buddhists call it Mara. I'll use those words sparingly and mark them when I do. I don't need you to believe in an entity. I need you to see the coordination.

And the darkest idea it has ever produced is the one that sounds most like a solution: if we could just round up the bad people and be rid of them, the rest of us would live in peace. The self-deception required to hold that thought guarantees the darkness stays behind, in full force, changed only in shape. Every atrocity ever committed began there.

When I say contempt _is_ evil, I don't mean it as a value judgment. I mean it as an identification. This is the thing the word was always pointing at.

## 6. Evil people

I promised we'd get to them.

Evil is the _result_ of turning away, not its cause. That's the claim. We all shield ourselves from someone's humanity a little, in order to cause them a little harm. Turn that all the way up—shield completely, so that no life is perceived in the other at all—and what's left is what I was dropped into in Chapter 2. Not a different thing. The same thing with the guardrails off. You would know what it's like to _be_ evil, and it would not feel evil. It would feel like appetite. Like righteousness. Like the obvious thing to do.

The book will trace that progression, because the gradient is what makes the claim checkable. The further in a person is, the less it feels like they're in anything. More harm, more knowledge of the harm, more shielding, and correspondingly _more_ certainty. That's the pattern you can see from outside in every "evil person" you've ever watched. Look again and see if it isn't there.

Here I need to separate two things the word "contempt" has been carrying. The first stranger was turned away from you while still looking at you. He could still hear the protest. That's why he needed you to be bad, why he had to keep watching you, why he worked so hard. Call that contempt proper: an incomplete turning away, with the fight still on.

But there's a second form, and most of the world's harm runs on it. The turning away is so complete that nothing protests anymore. No need, no gaze, no heat. The other simply isn't in view. The manager signing the order. The shopper not thinking about the feedlot. The planner for whom a village is a number. From the inside it doesn't feel like contempt, because it doesn't feel like anything. From the outside it looks like nobody home: the same absence you saw in the dehumanizing gaze, minus the fight. Cold isn't milder than hot. It's further along. What I was dropped into in Chapter 2 was the cold form with appetite added: no life perceived at all, so nothing left to stop it.

I'll keep "contempt" for the hot form and mostly say "turning away" for what the two share, because what they share is the whole point. The sanctity of the thing in front of you is not hidden. It's right there in your experience, now. Hot and cold are two distances of not looking at it.

Two objections belong here.

First: _some people know exactly what they're doing and enjoy it._ Yes. And notice what even they are hiding. Not the harm; they'll tell you about the harm. What stays hidden is the deeper thing: that the superiority isn't real. That the wall between them and their victim is a story. That the enjoyment is available only _because_ something has been turned away from. Conscious malice still requires limbo; it's just limbo about something further down. The book will develop this, and I'll say plainly that I haven't got it fully worked out.

Second: _psychopaths._ I'll say here what I'll say there. I don't know. Some people may have very little of the faculty we've been calling conscience. Nothing in this book requires that everyone have it in equal measure. What it requires is that _you_ have it, that most people do, and that the people you thought of in the Preface—the ones who made you truly enraged—are overwhelmingly not psychopaths. They're ordinary people in deep limbo. That's what the rage was reacting to. That's what it could see.

And I'll say what I can and can't prove. I can't prove that other people are, at their core, the thing I'll describe in section 9. What you can verify for yourself is that believing they aren't leads directly to contempt, and you've now seen what contempt does. My own stance is that it's simply the nature of consciousness. You don't have to take that. You only have to watch what the opposite belief produces.

And then the thing I hold at the center, which I'll state now and spend the rest of the book earning: **all evil is ultimately innocent.**

Not excusable. Not harmless. Innocent in a specific sense: the one committing it had to stop seeing something in order to do it, and nobody chooses to stop seeing while they can still see. You cannot fathom the distortion a mind has to undergo to become someone who apparently chooses evil. If you saw all of it, your heart would break, and then break again. This isn't a plea for softness. Softness would make things worse, for them and for everyone. It's only that if you really saw it, the urge to shove them deeper could never again feel like anything but what it is.

There but for the grace of God. Not as a platitude. As a perception.

## 7. Contempt from below

I have to say this carefully, because it's the hardest thing in the book. When the Preface asked you to bring someone to mind, some of you didn't think of a stranger from the news. You thought of someone who hurt you. Or your child. Or your people.

Your rage is right. The fierceness is right. Stopping them is right, and if stopping them takes force, the force is right. Nothing here asks you to feel less, soften anything, or grant an inch you don't want to grant. None of that is on trial.

One thing is on trial: the verdict. _This is what they are._ And I'm going to ask you to look at something about that verdict that you will not want to look at. I'm asking because, of everyone, you've earned the right to know it.

Look at who benefits when you hold it.

When you've concluded that the one who hurt you is irredeemable, something in _them_ is relieved. Not the person. The thing in them that needed you to see it that way. Your contempt is the evidence it runs on: _see, they're just what we said; this world is a war; the only thing that works is power._ You know this already, because you know what the first stranger's contempt did to you. It didn't make you better. It made you dig in. Now you're the stranger, and it doesn't matter that you're right about what they did.

That's the handshake again. Here's the part that took me longest. The thing that fools you into believing that contempt-from-below is good—that it's the one clean contempt, the deserved one—is the same thing that's running the person above you. Not a similar thing. The same one. It doesn't care which direction contempt flows. It only needs it to flow. Every time it gets you to hold the verdict, it has recruited the person with the least reason to serve it.

Of course you don't want to feed that. You, of all people.

What actually frightens it is the other thing: fierce, unyielding refusal that carries no verdict. That isn't a nice idea from a book. It's the most consistently effective force in the history of people facing power they couldn't match. When King said hate was too great a burden to bear, he wasn't being sentimental; he was describing a strategy, and the record bears him out. It works because it's the second stranger. It gives the other side nothing to dig in against while giving the thing in them nothing to eat. And it is enormously harder than contempt, which is why the thing sells you contempt as the strong option.

Notice what the second stranger produced in you. It wasn't shame. It was remorse: what you feel about what you did when what you are isn't in question. Remorse repairs. Shame hides. That is the entire difference between the two strangers, and it turns out to have been measured.

There's one more form of contempt, the most common of all, and nobody talks about it: contempt for yourself. The verdict, turned inward. It feels like accountability. It produces what contempt always produces: hiding. The way you hold your own darkness is the way you'll end up holding theirs. If you can only look at what you did while what you are isn't on trial, that's true of them too. And it's true of you first.

## 8. Why we do it, all the way down

Chapter 1 asked why he _needed_ you to be bad. Here's the full answer, in my own case.

Every bone in my body needed to believe that the problems of the world were caused by something alien to me: inherently defective people whom I could never understand, only fight. Then I could look at what my contempt had made of them and know, with total certainty, that evil was real and that it wasn't me.

That's the need. And underneath the need is not, finally, a wish to be superior. That's its shape. Underneath is fear: of being the one who deserves what I'm dealing out; of what I'd find if I looked. Nobody is innocent for wanting to stand above someone. Everybody is innocent for being afraid. The need fed on my fear, my pride, my insecurity, and it never once felt like any of those. It felt like clarity.

And it's why we don't mock and shame our enemies _despite_ it making them dig in. We do it _because_ it does. Which is how "evil people" become what Chapter 3 called the communal dumping ground: the place where all of us can unleash without guilt. The message we deliver while doing it is _sorry, someone has to carry this, and as long as I keep you down there, I'll know that mine is safe._

There are layers to how this hides itself, and I want to lay them out plainly, because the last one is the only one that matters.

Layer one: _I have no darkness._

Layer two: _I have some, and I'm using it strategically to fight real evil._ This is where most thoughtful people live. You catch glimpses, and you tell yourself the glimpses are under your control.

Layer three: the darkness is convincing you it's too horrible to face—that if you looked, you'd find out you're defective—so that you'll keep it hidden. And it knows that keeping it hidden is what spreads it. It shows you layer two so that you'll never suspect layer three. You think you're using it. It's using you.

Layer four is the way out, and it's what I've been seeding since the Preface. What you actually are is not the darkness. The darkness is nothing but the turning away. It has no eyes of its own and no material of its own; everything it uses, it borrows from the faculty it's suppressing. It is an absence, maintained. Which means there is nothing to be ashamed of at the bottom. And shame was its last line of defense. The moment you can look without your worth being on trial, it has nothing left to work with.

I need to be careful with my own vocabulary here. I've called it a "dark part," and elsewhere I've called it a parasite, and I don't want either taken literally. Here is the sense in which it's like a parasite and the sense in which it isn't. It's like one because it lives off a host, and the host is what you are: the part that cares. Everything else is extrinsic. The darkness has no substance of its own; it borrows your intelligence, your perception, your certainty, and turns them slightly away. It's _unlike_ a parasite because a parasite is something, and this is nothing at all. A hole in a hull isn't a thing either, and it sinks the ship. That's the seriousness, and it's why "it's just an absence" must never become "it's not serious." An absence masquerading as a presence is more dangerous than any presence, because a presence can be fought.

And that's also why it behaves like one thing across minds, which is the part I've struggled hardest to say. If there were eight billion darknesses, their coordination would be a miracle. There aren't. There's one thing being turned away from—the same faculty in every one of us, the one that recognized itself in the stranger's eyes—so there's only one way to turn away from it. Every shadow in a valley points away from the same sun. Nobody coordinates the shadows. They aren't many things. They're one absence in many places, each borrowing the same light, which is why they line up. Mara. Satan. The Accuser. Those names didn't come from nowhere. They're what it looks like from inside a mind that can see the alignment but not the reason for it. I don't need you to believe in an entity. I need you to see that its coordination costs nothing, for the same reason the mutual knowing in Chapter 1 cost nothing.

So there are two distinctions, and I'll lean on both. You and not-you: the darkness is not what you are. And real and not-real: the part that cares is the only thing in the whole picture with a light of its own. Everything the darkness seems to be, it is reflecting.

That's why the medicine could show me what it showed me. It held me in something while it did. When darkness is exposed without that, we feel threatened and can't look. Held, we can, and what we find is that it was never ours. That's what the second stranger did for you, in miniature. It's what this book is trying to do.

## 9. What remains

_Here the tier changes. From this point, I'm telling you how I've come to hold it. Nothing practical in the book depends on you sharing it._

Take away the hiding and something is left. It's what was underneath the noise all along: the part that cares, profoundly, about other people, other beings, the world. I've been calling it your conscience. The traditions call it the Light. Whatever you call it, it isn't something you have. It's what you are when nothing is being turned away from.

Goodness is simply what remains in the absence of self-deception. Nothing has to be added.

You already know how to find it. Think again of the second stranger: the absence of a story, the cleanness, the sense that he was serving something larger than himself and that you wanted to serve it too. Not because you calculated it. It was just obvious. Or think of the one person who saw the good in you when you couldn't see it yourself. That's what you can be for someone.

Some things the book will say about it.

It is not nice. Kindness, in this sense, can be fierce. It can even be violent, if that's what a situation requires. Its only defining characteristic is that it's free of self-deception: it doesn't misperceive evil as intrinsic to anyone, and so it's incapable of contempt. It goes after the confusion, not the confused being. The Dalai Lama's version: if someone is trying to shoot you and you have a gun, shoot back. Just never out of malice.

Its supposed weakness is a lie the darkness tells to keep you from noticing its power.

It is dangerous to _pretend_ to see the light in someone when you can't. They will use it against you. Both overlooking it and faking it fail, in different ways. The only thing that works is learning to see it for real, along with everything that's covering it. That's a skill, and the deeper someone is in, the more skill it takes.

Being free of self-deception does not mean knowing what's best. It means acting with clean intention. You still need wisdom about what helps and what harms, and the book won't pretend otherwise.

And just as the darkness in me handshakes with the darkness in you, so does this. When someone confronts you cleanly, something in you flickers in recognition, like being reminded it has a partner in a fight it thought it was fighting alone. You may stay enemies on the surface for a long time. Underneath, something else is happening.

I saw this from both sides in the experience I described in Chapter 2. I was shown myself drifting, further and further from the thing I'm describing, trusting the counterfeit more with every step. And I was shown how the people around me responded: most grew more contemptuous the further I went, exactly as I'd done in their shoes. But a handful never did. They made it completely clear that what I was doing was unacceptable and that they'd stop it, and equally clear that they would not hand me over. They weren't refusing to let me misbehave. They were refusing to let the thing have me. That's the difference the book is about.

You have already had moments of this yourself. Moments you helped when nobody knew. Moments you confronted someone fiercely while seeing them as confused rather than evil. Moments you knew—not thought, _knew_—that something was right, and it cost you, and you did it anyway. The book will ask you to recall them and to notice that they felt different from everything else. That difference is the only one that matters.

## 10. What you are, and what follows from it

_Still marked._

At the deepest level I've been able to see, there aren't others. One thing, looking at itself through countless eyes, pretending it isn't. I can't show you that this follows from anything above. I can tell you that you've tasted it: when you felt gratitude toward someone who helped strangers you'll never meet, or gave to something that could never benefit you. When a story about being every person who ever lived landed somewhere real. Something in you already knows that whatever happens to the world happens to you.

And this is where the fear from section 8 bottoms out. The version I've found clearest is Kenneth Wapnick's reading of _A Course in Miracles_. The whole thing begins with a fear that makes no sense: the fear of having left Love; of having separated from what you are. Having lost apparent contact with absolute goodness, you go looking for it in the relative, which in practice means finding someone worse than you. Contempt is the search for your own goodness in the wrong place. That's why it never satisfies and always needs one more. The ego, in that reading, is nothing but the belief in separation, and its one strategy is to keep the guilt "out there." It's the same picture as this book in different words, and I'll say where it connects.

If that's true, a few things follow, and they're the things that decide how you live.

You cannot pull a fast one over the world, because you are the world. Every act of hiding is itself what darkens it. Exploiting nature cannot work, however cleverly it's done, because we are nature. Not as a metaphor.

Everything you do makes a difference, through networks of cause your conscious mind can't trace, nudging things toward remembering or toward forgetting. This is why what you do when nobody is watching doesn't fail to matter. Your private integrity bolsters the resolve of strangers you'll never meet, at the moment they're about to break. I can't prove that. I'm reporting it as the thing that, if it's true, makes it worth staying committed to your light at literally any cost. Not to your _idea_ of the light, which is always contaminated. To the thing itself.

That's also the definition of good I'll be working with: whatever brings us closer to remembering what we are. Evil is whatever pushes us further from it. Which is why the greatest gift you can give the world is your own seeing. Until then, some part of you is reinforcing the thing you're fighting. That's not a moral failing. It's just how it works.

And the trap I fall into most: contempt for people who still have contempt. Watch for it. It's the same thing in new clothes.

## 11. The choice, in practice

The last part of the book comes down out of the metaphysics into the moment where all of this happens.

Anger is natural, and often right. But it almost always carries a passenger: malice, contempt, self-righteousness. They feel healthy too. They aren't.

Judgment, outrage, punishment: each of those words covers two different things. There's a contemptuous version and a non-contemptuous one. The contemptuous versions are always evil; that's the identification again, not a value judgment. The non-contemptuous versions are context-dependent. Whether they're wise is a further question, and that's the skill this book can't give you, only point at. At one level the two versions are easy to confuse. At a deeper level the difference is obvious, and it's only the dark part that makes it feel hard.

Here's what I check, and what I'll ask you to check. Does this need them to be bad? Would I feel _worse_ if they got better? Is there anything in it I wouldn't want narrated out loud? Does it have to argue? Is there a hit of pleasure in what it wants to do to them that's separate from any good it would do? Any yes, and something has been turned away from.

Trying to be a "good person" is itself the trap. "Good person" is an idea, and any idea of goodness generates "bad people" to define itself against. Whereas if I really see that what's in them is in me—that we're the same thing in different degrees of confusion—I don't have to try to be good. I just do the right thing, because the alternative isn't available anymore.

Some plain admissions the book owes you. Cruelty _can_ suppress misbehavior, at least the public kind, for a while, which is why it's so easy to believe it works; but check whether another part of you knows what it does to the total amount of darkness in the world.

Stopping evil with contempt is probably still better than quietly letting evil win. This is not a book about doing nothing. Any agenda, political or personal, can be carried out from connection or from contempt; people can feel which, and it changes everything downstream even when the words are identical.

And none of this is "I read it, I got it." There are levels, and then more levels. There are tools—the ones the contemplative traditions have been refining for millennia, and some newer ones—and the book will point at them without pretending to be them.

And one question I'll leave with you until then: why _would_ I want to stop corruption, when I feel so good judging it? Sit with that. The honest answer is that part of me doesn't. That's the part this book is about.

## 12. What this book is for

The mutual knowing in the first stranger's gaze was common knowledge that was never said out loud. That's what let it work. Say it aloud, in public, and the pretense fails. Not because anyone learns anything new, but because now everyone can see that everyone sees.

That's the bet this book is making. If it becomes ordinary, sayable knowledge that contempt is the thing—that this is what we've been calling evil—then contempt loses its cover. A thing that can be named can't do work that depended on being unnameable.

I should be honest about the limit. The word "contempt" already exists, and its existence hasn't dissolved anything. What's unnameable isn't the word. It's the act itself, in the moment it's happening, named by the person it's happening to, in a way the other person can't wave off. That takes a shared understanding of what the act is, and that's what I'm trying to give you. And I'll predict something else: this idea will be used as a weapon. Someone will accuse someone of "doing the thing," with contempt. When that happens, it will carry the same tell it always has. You'll know.

Nothing here asks you to soften what you see or pretend the harm isn't real. If anything you'll see more, and more clearly. The only thing being taken from you is the pleasure of certainty about what someone _is_. And I think, if you check, you'll find that it was never yours to begin with.

_When it is recognized, it is dissolved._
