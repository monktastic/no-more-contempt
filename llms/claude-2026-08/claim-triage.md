# Claim triage: how much to establish, and when

*The recurring problem: the instinct to establish every claim with full precision the moment it appears. That's right for a paper and wrong for a book. Here's the working rule.*

---

## Four grades, not one

Every claim in the book is doing one of four jobs. They cost wildly different amounts, and most claims are being over-served.

**Stated and owed.** You say the claim once, plainly. You don't defend it. You say out loud that you haven't defended it and where you will. Cost: one or two sentences. What it buys: when the claim comes back properly defended, the reader thinks *ah, that's what that was* — which feels like their own recognition rather than your argument.

**Demonstrated.** The reader checks it against their own memory or runs an experiment. Cost: a device (a scene, a question, a memory prompt) — usually half a page to two pages. This is the book's currency, and it's the grade the two strangers, the gaze experiment, and the limbo recall are all operating at.

**Argued.** You state an objection and answer it. Cost: a page or more, and it puts the objection into the heads of readers who didn't have it.

**Earned.** The payoff of prior work; feels like arrival rather than assertion. Cost: nothing at the point of use — it was paid earlier.

The over-precision instinct is the reflex to argue everything. A book can afford five or six argued claims. They should be the ones where a reader who didn't accept them would put the book down.

---

## The triage rule

Ask two questions in order.

**1. Would a reader accept this on loan if I admitted it was a loan?** If yes: state it, admit the debt, move on.

Most claims pass. Readers will lend you a great deal. What they won't forgive is borrowing without saying so. Saying so is what turns an unsupported claim from a weakness into a promise.

**2. What is the reader doing with it while it's unpaid?** If they're only holding it — waiting to see whether you make good — the loan is safe. If they're going to act on it, or use it to interpret their own life, then it has to be demonstrated before you ask for it, not after.

This isn't about whether you might be wrong. You should only be stating things you believe. It's about what the reader is being asked to carry, and for how long, before they can check it themselves.

Argue it only if a reader would actively refuse the loan — resist, not merely wonder. And put the argument where the resistance actually happens, which is usually chapters after the claim first appears.

---

## The flag, in three parts

When you state a claim you haven't earned, say three things. It can be one sentence.

1. **The claim, flat.** Don't hedge. Hedging reads as weakness; a flat claim plus an honest admission reads as confidence.
2. **What kind of thing it is.** Assertion, inference, or belief. The tier marking, done in passing.
3. **Where you'll pay for it.**

*"I'm stating that now rather than arguing for it, because it needs everything in the rest of the book to stand up. But hold it while you read, because a lot depends on it."*

That's all three, and it costs two sentences. Compare with the four pages it would take to actually defend the claim at that point in the book — pages during which the reader is being argued at about something they'd have granted for free.

---

## Three failure modes to watch for

**Handing the reader objections.** Answering an objection before the reader has it teaches them to have it. Every "one might object that…" puts a doubt in a reader who didn't have one. Objections have a clock. Answer them where a reader actually forms them, which is usually a chapter or three after the claim, not at it.

**Precision as a cost to the reader.** Every qualification is a clause someone has to hold in working memory. Precision should be spent where imprecision would produce a *wrong* belief that matters downstream — not where it would merely produce an incomplete one. Incomplete is fine. Incomplete plus a flag is better than complete and exhausting.

**Listing your debts too often.** "What I haven't shown you" is powerful at a seam and corrosive everywhere else. Every few pages it reads as anxiety. At the end of a movement it reads as integrity.

---

## The question that stops the whiplash

There are two questions about every claim, and the drafts kept asking one and answering the other.

*Does the reader need this to read the next stretch correctly?* That's function. It decides whether a claim appears early.

*What has the reader checked?* That's evidence. It decides how the claim is stated: demonstrated, stated-and-owed, argued, or held back.

The old interlude got function right and evidence wrong: ten claims the reader needed, each defended as though established. The cut got evidence right and function wrong: nothing unearned said early, the picture lost, and Chapter 1 under-credited into the bargain. Every reversal since has been one axis correcting the other.

The rule: **a claim goes early if the reader needs it. Its evidence grade decides the form, never the placement.** A high-need, low-evidence claim goes early, flat, with the debt named and no rival explanation attached. That isn't overclaiming. Overclaiming is defending it there.

Corollary for the interlude: three piles, not two. What you saw. What I make of it. What I'm asking you to hold. The middle pile is where well-supported inferences live, and the third is where the theses the reader needs go, undefended.

## The one-line version

**State most things once and admit the debt. Demonstrate what the argument stands on. Argue only what a reader would refuse to lend you.**
