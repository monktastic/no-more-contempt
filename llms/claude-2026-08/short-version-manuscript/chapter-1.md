# Chapter 1: Turning Away

Imagine looking into a stranger's eyes long enough that their face stops being something you're looking at and becomes a place somebody is looking out of.

Stay there until you feel what comes with it—not a conclusion, but a recognition:

> _This one is like me. I want them to be all right._

Call this being _turned toward_ them. With it comes a sense of their innocence. Not that they're incapable of causing harm—but the sense of something innocent *underneath* it.

Now erase that meeting. Run it again—but this time, they've just done something atrocious. Something deliberate, to someone innocent—maybe even to someone you love—and they are unrepentant.

Keep looking into the same eyes. Can you still find that same recognition?

It's much harder, isn't it? Where before there was someone whose wellbeing mattered, now there is only something rotten. Not merely something they did. Something they **are**.

And what is it that you're showing them through your gaze? Is it just anger? Just *I will stop you?* Or is there something darker in it? Something that says:

> *You are less than human, and you’ve forfeited the right to be treated as one.*

Something in you wants this verdict to *land*; to register deep inside. Can you feel that?

This overlooking of their humanity is what I'll call being _turned away_: you cannot see yourself in them. The everyday word for it is *othering*. And the need to make the verdict land is _contempt_: turning-away made into a message, with the sense that sending it is righteous.

And how do they react? Does it make remorse easier—or do they double down, feeling even more justified in their behavior?

The latter, nearly always.

Didn't some part of you know this would happen? And yet you did it anyway. Why?

---

Part of it is that you wanted justice—and making them feel the full weight of your judgement feels righteous; like the least you could do as a good person.

It feels like you've finally caught hold of a sliver of *what's wrong with the world*, and you're not going to let it escape so easily. See if you can locate that sense. And notice how good it feels when they dig in. Like relief. *There it is. Exactly what I'd expect from a rotten person.*

Now ask yourself: what's it like to be on the receiving end of that dark message you sent? Who *wouldn't* dig in? Wouldn't you? So how can you then turn around and use it as proof that they're beyond help?

Something fishy is going on here.

I'll go ahead and name it: one part of your mind is provoking them—sending them a dark message that you *know* will make them dig in—while another part is blocked from realizing what you're actually doing, so that you can hold their reaction against them in all sincerity.

Why? Because it feels like *revenge.* They've made their bed, and now they're going to sleep in it. They're the ones who chose the darkness—you're only making sure they can't wriggle out. Sure, it's a bit underhanded, but there's a difference: their darkness is *real* darkness, and yours is the good kind, only there to defeat the real kind. You can't be soft on evil. *That's how it wins!*

There's actually a deeper answer for why you do it, which we'll come to later. The question for now is *how* you provoke them.

To make this intuitive, imagine if you could somehow send them a message that said: 

> *I'm going to provoke you into embracing your darkness, to make sure you can't escape—and I'm going to get away with it, because I'm only "fighting evil."*

That would certainly do the trick, wouldn't it? But only if it were sent on a **private channel**; something that nobody could ever prove you sent—*least of all yourself.* Because if others can't prove you sent it, then you can't be held accountable. And if you show them that *you yourself* don't know, then they're **really** trapped: they know they can't appeal to your better nature, because you believe this *is* your better nature. They'll already know how you'll turn their accusations against them: *How **dare** you? You're just projecting!*

Don't we already know this is how it works? Of course we do.

But how do you send a message that even you, by definition, can't know you're sending? And even stranger: how can you show them *that* you don't know it, in a way that's sure to reach them?

---

First, notice that whatever they did wrong, they could only have done it by being *turned away* from their victims. Check if that's true—and if part of you already knew it intuitively, even before I told you. You also know that the *only* reason people turn away is to harm (or use) others without having to feel compunction. Check if some part of you already knew that, too.

Now, how could I, a stranger, know that you knew these things? The answer is that they are **common knowledge**—something we each know about each other, and that we know others know about us, etc.

By being turned away from your target, you already know how they're going to receive it: as **malice**. And they *know* you know it, even though you won't admit it. And you know that, too, etc. That's how you can have a communication *beneath* the words, using tone and gaze alone. What you're actually communicating is:

> *We both know I'm only doing the same thing you were—turning away to enable harm—but this time **you** are the victim, and nobody's going to feel sorry for you.*

Which means you know one more thing: how a fundamentally decent person—yourself—can act in *profoundly dark* ways. How you can keep a fellow being trapped in the place where harm originates, show them that you're doing it, how you'll get away with it, and how you'll sleep like a baby for it. 

I won't mince my words: it is **evil** (insofar as there is such a thing—more on that soon). We normally reserve that word for wrongdoing of extreme size, but even the tiniest morsel of it carries the flavor of the whole thing. There is no "good kind." It is the thing we all hate, and the source of all hatred, and expressing it (even for a "good reason") only spreads it. It is what you saw in their eyes, and what you are revealing to them now.

You know full well that they will recognize it for what it is, and you're *counting on* them knowing it, because otherwise it wouldn't work.

This is getting heavy, so let's pause before continuing. When I say "you know" this, I mean that *part* of you does. The reason you must hide it from yourself is that you *couldn't do it if you saw it clearly for what it was.* Check that. It's a big hint about your true nature. You're not a bad person. 

Okay, let's continue.

Notice what else it means. You know how a decent person (you) can wield evil while feeling eminently righteous, so *you don't actually know they're irredeemable*. As far as you know, this person is just like you—meaning that with the right help, they could actually reform. And *we know how to do this*—how to serve justice without making people feel that "this is all you are." Which means you're *actively cutting off their hope of redemption*, knowing there's a better way.

Before, you might have been able to tell yourself that sure, maybe they were "turned away" too or whatever, but that's just who they are; or that they chose to turn away due to some rotten essence that you lack. What you're seeing now is how that "choice" *doesn't require a rotten essence*. It just requires the right (wrong) circumstances.

I'm presenting this as a sequence of deepening realizations, but that's only because prose requires linear unfolding. In truth, this is all part of that same common knowledge. What makes it "evil"—and thus what allows it to work—is that you both know **all** of it. It is an **open secret** that the purpose of contempt is to keep people trapped in the dark and get away with it. Otherwise it wouldn't work.

So that's how the message reaches them. But remember, there's another half to the question. You need to show them the part of yourself that *doesn't know it's doing this.* Because if it read as open malice, it wouldn't be nearly as maddening (and thus wouldn't keep them trapped).

How do you show them that you don't know?

## Limbo

Let's set the enemy aside for a moment, and look at a different scene—one where nobody "had it coming."

Have you ever done something you knew was wrong at the time, but couldn't fully admit it to yourself until later? Maybe you took credit for someone else's work, said something cruel to someone who'd done nothing to you, or cheated on a test (or even a partner).

Recall what it was like to finally own up to it. The knowledge that it was wrong: did it feel like new information? Or like something you had always known, but somehow hadn't *let* yourself know?

We like to call such knowledge "unconscious"—as if we had no way of knowing—but that's not entirely accurate. It was *right there* to see if you had looked; fully available to consciousness. You were just *turned away* from it. The knowledge existed in a strange kind of **limbo**, in which you simultaneously knew and yet somehow didn't.

And it wasn't just sitting there idle, either: it affected your behavior. Any time someone came close to exposing it, or you started getting close to it yourself, you found a way to deflect. Did that happen passively?

Looking back, can you see how *some part of you knew exactly what you were doing, even as you did it?* The work of hiding it took place in limbo, too.

---

Now try to recall how you knew it was wrong in the first place.

Whatever it was, did it arrive before or after you'd reasoned through it? The part of you that knew: it couldn't say exactly how it knew. Meanwhile, another part of you was busy providing reasons. *Everyone does it. It's not a big deal. Just this once.*

Why did one side have to argue, and the other not? And which side did you listen to—the part that "just knew" or the part that could prove?

Also notice that limbo exists in degrees. It's not just black or white. The worse the behavior, the harder we must hide it from ourselves.

You cannot harm or use others while staring your conscience straight in the face, with no justifications at hand. Try it.

Finally, notice that when you are in limbo, part of you *knows* you are—even as you hide it from yourself. In other words, limbo always contains knowledge of itself.

---

Okay back to our scenario.

That dark message: you know you're sending it *and* you don't. It must be sent from limbo.

If you were fully unaware, you couldn’t aim it so precisely—making sure the verdict lands, anticipating their likely reaction, and preparing to use it against them. But if you were fully aware, you couldn’t sincerely mistake their reaction for evidence. You have to be present enough to plant the evidence, while absent enough not to recognize it as planted.

But your message wasn't just sent *from* limbo. Limbo, aimed at a person, **is** the darkness.

Think of what it's like to be on the receiving end of a dehumanizing gaze. Doesn't the other person feel somehow absent or "turned away" from themselves? *That's* what you're looking at—limbo, aimed at you.

Turning away from yourself; from your conscience; from your profound commonality with others—it's all a single move. It is what allows us to harm one another without compunction, or benefit ourselves at their expense. It is the evil I named above.

Check it against the cases you'd already call evil, because the word has to cover them or it's just a word. The murderer: he had to be turned away from his victim. The executive who signs off on the part he knows will fail. The official whose decision will cost lives but get him re-elected. It's one thing in all of them: real people, kept out of view, so that the harm doesn't register as harm. The heat is optional. Contempt is the hot form, the one that's aimed, and shows, and needs to. Indifference and negligence are the same move without the heat.

**That** is what you're revealing through your eyes, and you know they'll recognize it *because* it is universal. That's the common knowledge from before, and it exists at an implicit level, which is why you might never have verbalized it.

That is what permits the second conversation beneath the spoken one, and it is why gaze and tone are enough to carry it.

The thing you're turned away from (your conscience) is also the thing that warns you when you *are* turned away from it. That's why we so rarely recognize when we're in it. Seeing it clearly would destroy it.

Your target can see the state you're in even though you don't. Because you don't. What they're seeing is *that* you don't.

There's another way of looking at that message: you are trusting the part of you that can *prove* (that your behavior is good) over the part that *knows* (that it's wrong). You know *they* know you know it's wrong, and that they cannot prove it. And this pressures them to demote the part of themselves that knows—their conscience—in favor of the part that can prove. It feels like this:

> *If even the so-called "good people" will so easily embrace the darkness when they can get away with it, then why the fuck shouldn't I?*

Which is exactly the message that part of you wanted them to receive.

And before you file all of this under things we do to enemies: we also send it even to people we love. The *what's wrong with you?* that needs them to know they're broken. The sigh that lets them know what we really think of them in that moment, dressed up to make us seem like the reasonable ones. The look on their face that tells us it landed, which we'll pretend not to have seen. 

Think of a time you did this. For one second the same "something" flashed through your eyes, and you made sure they saw it, and then you went back to loving them as if nothing had happened.

---

Now: who, exactly, is sending that message? We already saw why full awareness would collapse it—but it's also just too Machiavellian for most of us to consciously design. We're not cartoon villains. In a certain important sense, it's not really *us* behind the wheel.

I've been calling it *the darkness*, and I'll keep the name. It's not a literal metaphysical entity, but in many ways behaves like one. It is the thing you revealed to them (or that revealed itself) in your message. It is what "takes control" of your mind when you are *turned away.*

That's a major thesis of this work: the darkness acts *through* each of us, and it can only do so by convincing us that it's not—that we're the ones in control, and what we're doing is okay or even righteous. It uses each person's unique psychology to find a way in, and the way it commandeers your mind and an "evil person's" differ only in the details. (Of course, when a mind has turned far enough away, turning it back may be a different kind of work, and it's fair to draw a conventional distinction. But it doesn't change what the person is made of.)

Look at how it got in this time. It didn't ask you to embrace darkness, because nobody falls for that. It offered you a *good kind* of darkness, and the good kind earned its name by what it accomplished: the verdict landed. Maybe it even scared them into pausing their misbehavior, or at least the part of it that could be caught. You knew the longer-term harm too, and that's the part that went into limbo. That's the trick, and it is how it always works. Never *be evil.* Always *mine is different.*

In other words, the darkness wins by masquerading *as* the light.

> *And no wonder, for even Satan masquerades as an angel of light. So it is not strange that his ministers also masquerade as ministers of righteousness.* —2 Corinthians 11:14–15

None of that lets us off. We are always **fully responsible** for the harm we cause while under its sway. We want to write it off as "unconscious," but that's not true. There's a paradox here that makes it difficult to hold.

Notice what happens when we call it *purely conscious.* In our enemies, it gives us reason to feel contempt—and we know where that leads. In ourselves, it produces shame. In both cases, that makes it harder to confront, which only gives it more power by keeping it in the dark.

But if we call it purely unconscious, we run into the opposite problem. There's no accountability in ourselves. And we're *nice* to the perpetrators, when what we should be is *kind*—fiercely kind, in a way that stops the harm without cutting off their potential to return to goodness.

Both views are incomplete, and our inability to hold this paradox is what enables it to continue. The same challenge confronts us when we're forced to choose between calling it "us" and "not-us." Both are true, in their own way.

Notice, too, that you never get both views of one instance. Your own turning away is always the kind you didn't see; theirs is always the kind they chose. Same act, opposite interpretations[^FAE].

[^FAE]: This is closely related to the Fundamental Attribution Error in psychology: we judge others by their actions and ourselves by our intentions.

And look what "it" was doing while in control. When you make your enemies dig in, does the harm end with them? No. It ripples out, with no clean boundary—and some part of you knew that too. 

Our contempt makes the **world as a whole** darker. Every time it's shown to someone and lands, it's easier for them to do, and for everyone they do it to, whatever unique form theirs might take. That's how the thing behind the atrocities is fed by a sigh.

Again, this cannot be something new I'm teaching you. You have always known that your enemies aren't identical with their darkness, and that treating them as if they were only shoves them deeper into its grip, even as you believe you're doing the opposite. You *had* to know this, for that dark message to function or even be coherent.

So why do we do it in the first place?

---

Imagine what it would be like to know, with complete certainty, that even the worst people aren't fundamentally different from you. That the way you've been treating them has been making the world a darker place—and that you knew it even as you were doing it, but convinced yourself you didn't.

It would mean that the darkness behind the atrocities you read about isn't disconnected from your own behavior. You are somehow a part of it—of "what's wrong with the world."

That knowledge would be so horrifying that you couldn't allow it into full conscious awareness. You would need to know that no, some people really are just fundamentally different. And that would drive you to behave in ways that "proved" it beyond any doubt, by turning them into what you need them to be.

*That* is the message we're actually sending to "evil people" through our cruelty:

> *You will be humanity's sacrifice. The dumping ground for our collective shadow. That way, we never have to face what is inside us. Good luck.*

This was all there to see if you had looked. That you were doing something underhanded. That it wasn't the "good kind" of darkness, because there's no such thing. That it would raise the net animosity in the world. And that this would give your own darkness more cover.

By turning away from your better nature, you turned away from theirs, and with it from everything your behavior would create. It's *all a single move.* The darkening of the world was implicit in it.

Again, if this is horrifying, notice why: because **you don’t actually want to be doing it.** The horror is evidence that the darkness is not your nature. And through the common knowledge we’ve just traced, some part of you already knows that it cannot be anyone else’s, either.
