# Chapter 2: Common Ground

That suggests something larger. If awareness were neutral between care and harm, why would harming someone require turning away from it? Why couldn’t cruelty proceed in full, undivided awareness? The simplest explanation is that care belongs to awareness itself. 

The familiar explanations (motivated reasoning, a self-image to protect, the avoidance of shame) don't compete with this. They're what the turning is built from, and they inherit its blind spot: none of them can see itself operating. Darkness is not an equal and opposite force. It is what becomes possible when awareness turns away from its own nature.

It also explains how such knowledge could be common—not merely shared, but something each of us can count on the other already knowing. If what's looking out of them has nothing to differ from what's looking out of you, then to know this in yourself is already to know it in them, and to know that they know it in you, etc. This is the only way the trap could function. Otherwise, there would be no violation to recognize, no hypocrisy to expose, no taunt to make land. **Darkness can communicate only by borrowing the light as common ground.**

So here is the counterintuitive claim at the center of this book. Contempt is coherent only if all of the following is already common knowledge between you: that you're the same kind of thing; that turning away from that is the only way to harm; that the person you're turning from can read it, because what reads it in them is the same faculty you've turned away from in yourself; and that they can still turn back, because a verdict can only sting someone who could have.

In brief, **the fact that we wield contempt means we already know it's a miniature version of the thing we call evil, and that its target is redeemable—and that these are common knowledge.**

This is also why this book was originally titled *Contempt Is Evil*. Not as a judgment. Nor is it about size or outcomes: cutting in line costs thirty seconds, and the heat it raises is out of all proportion to that, because what you're reacting to is the exception, not the cost. Evil, at the level of the person, is the turning away; contempt is the turning away made into a message. And for the message to sting, they had to read it as the real thing, not as harshness, which a person turned toward you can deliver in larger doses and which lands as a reminder. So every time your contempt worked, both of you already knew what it was.

This is why awakening another person does not ultimately mean teaching them something new. It means helping them stop turning away from what they were already using—even in their darkness—to understand you. Words can point, but the deepest reminder travels through the same parallel channel: by remaining turned toward them, you embody openly what both of you already know. That's the look you started with, held when it's hardest to hold.

This gives the Buddhist account of ignorance an interpersonal form. Darkness has no independent substance; it is awareness failing to recognize its own nature. What I think I can add is an anatomy of how that ignorance passes between people—how it communicates through gaze and tone, recruits itself in another person, organizes groups, and leaves everyone convinced that they were fighting it.

This is not a call to be nice. Harm still has to be stopped, sometimes fiercely. The question is whether we can oppose the darkness without identifying the person with it—because that identification is how the darkness passes between us. And the only way to do that is by remaining unflinchingly *turned toward* them.
