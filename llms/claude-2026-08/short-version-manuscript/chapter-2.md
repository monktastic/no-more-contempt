# Chapter 2: Common Ground

That suggests something larger. If awareness were neutral between care and harm, why would harming someone require turning away from it? Why couldn’t cruelty proceed in full, undivided awareness? The simplest explanation is that care belongs to awareness itself. 

The familiar explanations (motivated reasoning, a self-image to protect, the avoidance of shame) don't compete with this. They're what the turning is built from, and they inherit its blind spot: none of them can see itself operating. Darkness is not an equal and opposite force. It is what becomes possible when awareness turns away from its own nature.

It also explains how such knowledge could be common—not merely shared, but something each of us can count on the other already knowing. The trap demonstrates that it is. If that knowledge were truly absent from either person, the message could neither be sent nor understood: no violation to recognize, no hypocrisy to expose, no taunt to make land. **Darkness can communicate only by borrowing the light as common ground.**

That is what limbo preserves. The knowledge remains present enough to organize the interaction while being kept out of full awareness. The trap’s very success therefore reveals what it is trying to conceal: an unbroken, mutually recognized knowledge of the light.

This is why awakening another person does not ultimately mean teaching them something new. It means helping them stop turning away from what they were already using—even in their darkness—to understand you. Words can point, but the deepest reminder travels through the same parallel channel: by remaining turned toward them, you embody openly what both of you already know. That's the look you started with, held when it's hardest to hold.

This gives the Buddhist account of ignorance an interpersonal form. Darkness has no independent substance; it is awareness failing to recognize its own nature. What I think I can add is an anatomy of how that ignorance passes between people—how it communicates through gaze and tone, recruits itself in another person, organizes groups, and leaves everyone convinced that they were fighting it.

This is not a call to be nice. Harm still has to be stopped, sometimes fiercely. The question is whether we can oppose the darkness without identifying the person with it—because that identification is how the darkness passes between us.
