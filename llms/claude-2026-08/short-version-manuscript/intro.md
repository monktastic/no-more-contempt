## Introduction

The nature of the mind is compassion. Evil is nothing but a mind turned away from itself.

A mind turned toward itself knows these truths innately. So when we're *not* seeing them clearly—such as when we perceive our enemies as *less* or *other* than ourselves—we are committing **evil**.

This is not an abstract idea. I want to show you its profound, real-world implications; how you already know them; and how *believing that you don't* enables "evil" to keep operating through your blind spots.

This is not an attack on you. I only know it because I'm still caught in it myself. This is merely an attempt to put a new lens on something ancient, so that we have one more tool by which to see it clearly. With that in mind, feel free to read what I say as a kind of poetry.

The darkness in each of our minds doesn't only operate *through* us; it collaborates *across* us. The corrupt structures we see around us are merely its outward manifestations. Attempting to destroy them without fixing the error in ourselves can only recreate them, because it is rooted in the same fundamental mistake: that they are essentially *other*.

This is also the origin of the archetype of the "evil global conspiracy," as well as the Devil. The collaboration of darkness across our minds is, by its very nature, hidden from the part of us that can see it—and yet we can never stop sensing it, which is why we retain the suspicion that there's something there, operating behind the scenes. There is. So awakening to one's true nature can be seen, in a certain sense, as finally stepping outside the conspiracy—the only vantage point from which one can actually take it down, rather than blindly participate in it.

Our attempts to define *goodness* are similarly folly. No matter how carefully we try to define it, the "dark" aspect of our mind finds clever ways to subvert it: to obey the letter of the law while violating its spirit. We recognize this error in other people, but never in ourselves. That folly is not accidental: getting us to believe our thoughts (and definitions)—as though they could be a substitute for staying turned toward our true nature—is precisely what the darkness *is*.

As civilization moves toward ever-greater abstraction, we feel a corresponding pull to relegate our true nature—authentic goodness—to the realm of superstition. Thus the compulsion toward pure abstraction, too, can be seen as an operation of the darkness.

These are all very lofty ideas. Now let me pull them into the realm of our everyday experience.
