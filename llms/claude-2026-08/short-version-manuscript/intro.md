We've heard these ideas before (and maybe even have some personal familiarity with them), but what relevance do they have to our actual lives?

A mind turned toward itself knows these truths innately. So when we're *not* seeing them clearly—such as when we perceive our enemies as *less* or *other* than ourselves—our mind is turned away from itself, and, in however small a way, it means we are being "evil" in some sense.

I know that's a bold word to use, and it's not meant as a judgement or attack. The book tries to earn it carefully. And it is something I know from the inside, because I'm still caught in it myself. And it isn't an abstract idea. It has profound, real-world consequences, and my hope is to help you notice whether you've been acting on a knowledge of them all along. What's new here is the lens: an attempt to see something ancient more clearly, so that we have one more tool for it.

The main engine of this book is one familiar thing: *moral contempt*, the way we treat our enemies as though they were *inherently defective.* Traced carefully, I'll argue, it turns out to be a uniquely insidious expression of evil: how the darkness spreads between us while masquerading as *righteousness*.

Now for the strange part, which this book exists to show. Contempt for our enemies is only coherent if both sender and receiver already know the things this introduction opened with: that the nature of mind is compassion, that darkness is nothing but being *turned away*, and—strangest of all—that these are both *common knowledge*[^ck]. Contempt claims the opposite, but it can only operate by denying exactly what it presupposes.

[^ck]: For my analytical readers: common knowledge in the game-theoretic sense, approximately, and of an implicit kind. My claim about the nature of awareness is what would make it exact. Appendix 1 has the details.

One consequence will matter to readers who trust reasoning above all, as I tend to: our attempts to define goodness keep failing, and not by accident. However carefully we pin it down, something in us finds a way to obey the letter while violating the spirit, and we catch that in others far more easily than in ourselves. I'll argue that you can't calculate your way to goodness, and say what's left when you can't.

By the end I'll be claiming something stranger still: that this darkness doesn't only work through each of us but, in effect, across us, and that the old stories about the Devil, and about hidden conspiracies, were reaching for it. You don't need to believe that to read on.

I realize these are all very lofty ideas. Let me now pull them into the realm of our everyday experience.
