# Chapter 4: The Way Through

Our "innate goodness" is the only part of us that's oriented toward the light; sensing its way toward it. Our reasoning mind can be in service of it, but it cannot orient by itself. Yet it *convinces* us it can. That's why we so easily fall for justifications: the part that knows cannot prove, while the part that proves does not know.

What this means is that all the darkness must do to "win" is to convince us to follow the part that proves. To try and pin down what goodness is; to formalize it. We can then obey the letter of the law while ignoring its spirit, and never suspect what's happening. That's what an ideology is, and I don't mean a bad one. Every ideology began as care, written down so it could be carried. But anything written down can be obeyed while disconnected from the source of care, and you can't tell, because the ideology feels exactly like the care. The problem was never following it blindly; nobody thinks they do. It's that it can be followed at all without looking. The test is simple: does it tell you what to do to the person in front of you before you've looked at them? What the darkness is doing is making that deeper part of us feel increasingly naive and superfluous. A vestige of our irrational past.

Fighting someone with contempt and fighting them with integrity can both "work" if you only take into account first-order effects: stopping them or shutting them up. A deeper part of you knows that in one case you've left the world a better place, and in the other, a worse one. But that knowing cannot be admitted into evidence.

The hardest part to convey is how this scales. We have projects, agendas, etc. that are motivated by the self-serving part of us, and they create apparent good but hollow out the caring in everything they touch. The harm is invisible to our rational minds, but a deeper part always knows. One test for any grand plan for doing good: does it need you to feel contempt for what's in front of you now, for the sake of some good that comes later? If so, it isn't coming from the part that cares, and it won't do what its proof says it will.

Or, if the parasite could speak, it would be saying:

> *Look how easily I can take over even the "good people." Genuine goodness is a myth—a name that "good people" use to get away with what they need to.*

The darkness doesn't win by head-on confrontation with the light. That's a fight it cannot win. It wins by *usurping* it; convincing us that it *is* it. We never stop wanting to orient to it; we just progressively forget what "it" is. You can only choose the counterfeit by forgetting what the real one feels like, the way you can only choose junk food once you've forgotten real food.

This is closely connected to the meaning crisis. We have nothing to orient us by, because we've convinced ourselves that we are free to define what is "good." And it's what I think is eating the world. We handed the question of what's good to the part of us that can only prove, and it answered.

So you can't think your way out, and that leaves the obvious question. If you can't trust your reasoning, and you can't trust your seeing once the verdict is in, what's left?

---

Only the thing that was demoted. And there's one clue you can use today. When you're acting from contempt, notice how *sure* you are that it's good. The real thing doesn't feel like that. It doesn't need to be sure.

Here is how to tell them apart in yourself, because they arrive together and the second one dresses like the first. Start with the hatred of the harm. It's the part that can't stand to watch people get hurt and wants it stopped. Nothing here asks you to feel that less; it's the most trustworthy thing in you. Now watch what happens the moment the harm gets a face. The heat changes. It stops being about the harm and starts being about the person. You want them to *know* what they are, and something in you would be disappointed if they got better. That second heat feels like the first one, only more serious. It isn't the same thing at all. It's the parasite, speaking in your conscience's voice.

So the check: if the harm simply stopped tomorrow, with nobody punished and nobody exposed, would you feel relief, or robbed? That question is most of the practice. Ask it of whoever you're carrying. The first several times you may find nothing. Then one day you'll catch it.

A second check, for the causes you hold. Keep the analysis, the genuine care, the valid critique, and give up only the permission to feel contempt for the people on the other side. Would you still want it? Notice the hesitation. That's the finding: the contempt came first, and the cause supplied the justification.

And a third, for the times you're caught. Think of how it feels when you've skirted your own conscience and somebody tries to shame you for it. You know they don't have your best interests at heart; you know they're using your all-too-human failure to throw you under the bus, and there's no way you'd let them get away with it. We think it's somehow different when we do it. That's the privilege we grant ourselves: the privilege to treat others with contempt for falling victim to the same thing.

One more, which I ask myself: why *would* I want to stop corruption, when I feel so good judging it? Sit with that. The honest answer is that part of me doesn't. And there's a price I hadn't counted: more self-serving behavior for me, in exchange for a darker world. When I could see the trade, I didn't want it.

---

Nice is worse than useless against evil. The only way to actually fight it is to remain *turned toward* the perpetrator while fighting the behavior. Refusing to see the darkness *as* them.

There are two kinds of judgment. One sees that a behavior is harmful and moves to stop it. The other adds a verdict about the person. We know the difference; at a deeper level it's obvious. And we keep the two blurred on purpose, so that when we're caught doing the second, we can retreat into the first: *wait, are you saying I shouldn't correct bad behavior?* The objection sounds reasonable, and it is the mechanism itself, leaving by the door it kept open for exactly this moment. Once that door is closed, what's left is correction with no self-deception in it. It can be fierce. It aims at the sickness in a person, never at the person, so it carries no malice.

Fierce is where the darkness finds it easiest to get in. From outside, fierce and dark look alike, and it counts on our not wanting to tell them apart. But we can tell, from the sending end: that little something extra, added to the fierceness on its way out, that you knew they'd feel.

Now the part I most want to get right, because the parasite frame can fail in the opposite direction. You don't have to believe that "bad people" are under the control of a parasite, or that they don't want what they're doing. You only have to notice what happens when you believe the alternative: that they are equivalent to their darkness, or that they want it. You can't help but hand control to your own. That is the sense in which the frame is useful, and it's the same with "rescuing them from it." Neither is a fact about them you're being asked to accept. Each is a stance whose alternative you have now watched recruit you.

But merely *believing* they're possessed fails too, in the other direction. You'll be nice. Believing someone has light when you can't see it is its own kind of not-looking, and someone deep in will use it against you. Overlooking the darkness and faking the light both fail. The only thing that works is to actually see it: the turning-away as turning-away, and what's underneath it, for real. And here is what makes this a practice rather than a belief. How much of that you can see in another person tracks, exactly, how free you are of your own.

Check it. Think of someone you once despised and later came to see differently, without their having changed. Maybe you'd since been through something like what they were up against. Maybe you'd caught yourself doing what they did. What changed wasn't them. It was how much of your own turning away was in the seeing. And the reverse, which you can check today: on a day when you're tired and self-justifying, everyone looks worse. The innocence you can see in others is a measure of your own freedom, not of their goodness. That's why seeing all the way into somebody far gone may be the work of a lifetime, and I don't have it, and why the near end is available now: catch yourself wanting them to be what you've decided they are, and notice, as the verdict settles, that something in you was after it.

You cannot see the parasite in someone else while you're hiding from it in yourself. Not won't; cannot. What lets you recognize it in another person is that you know it from the inside, the way you knew the stranger's state because you'd been in it an hour earlier. So the skill doesn't get built by studying other people. It gets built by whatever lets you stop turning away from your own, and the freer you get, the better you see it in others, and the less you fall for the trap. Which is also its own trap, and the one I fall into most: contempt for the people who still have contempt.

---

You've been on the receiving end of both. Think of two times it happened. In each, you've done something you shouldn't have, cut a line, taken credit, made the cruel remark, and somebody catches you.

The first person catches you and lets you know what you are. Not what you did; what you are. Nothing they say could be quoted against them, since the words are about the behavior. But you can feel it, and you dig in, and you leave a little worse: a little more certain that this is the kind of world where people look out for themselves.

The second person catches you and is, if anything, harsher. Says more, in front of more people, leaves you less room to wriggle. And it lands as something else entirely: as a reminder that *you're better than this.* You don't dig in. You leave a little more yourself. It weakened the grip of your parasite, by refusing to identify you with it, so that you could look at it.

Same words, nearly. Opposite outcomes. And both of them walk away confident that what they did worked, because it did: you stopped. Nothing in any record would show the difference. Only one of them knows better. Somewhere in the first, the flash you felt was felt by him too, on its way out, and he needed you to feel it, and he'll never look at that. That's the channel the darkness rides into the world on, and the light rides the same one: nothing said, nothing recorded, and the world a little darker or a little lighter afterward, in a way that outlasts the encounter and that neither of them will be blamed or thanked for.

The person may have had different politics, religion, creed; and yet you still felt that something was deeply aligned. What is that thing we're all aligned to, but that is hard to pin down?

It's the opposite of the darkness. It is the light. We already know how to do this, because we *are* it.

You use that recognition all the time, and you've never once had to define what it recognizes. Think of the news story that restored your faith in humanity: a stranger who dove in after somebody else's child, a town that took in people it had no reason to take in. You felt gratitude, and none of it benefited you. You didn't calculate what it was worth. Or the person who gives, quietly, to something that will never come back to them, and something in you thanks them for it. What were they aimed at? Not your good, and not theirs. You can't say what it is, and you recognized it instantly, the way you recognized it in the person who caught you and treated you with dignity, across every difference of race and religion and creed. That's what I've been calling the light: the thing that, when someone is aimed at it, you know it, and when they're aimed away from it, you know that too, and you can't define either. We all know the distinction. We just don't trust that we know it.

Compare that with how we usually judge the people we hand power to. We ask whether their policies are good, and "good" quietly means good for us and ours, worked out by the part that proves. There's another check available, and it's the one you just used on the stranger: are they aimed at the light, or away from it? That check tells you only about the compassion, not the wisdom; a person aimed at the light can still be wrong about everything and make a mess. But a person aimed away from it isn't even trying to make things better, however good the proof looks, and you can tell which is which the same way you could tell with the second person. You already do it. You just haven't been told that it counts.

One more thing about the light. Think of the last time you defended someone you love by tearing down the person who wronged them. Your sister's ex. The teacher who was unfair to your kid. The loyalty was real and warm, but riding on it was contempt, the need for the other person to be worthless. It may feel like being turned toward one person and away from the other, but that's not true. When you see yourself in any other, you necessarily see it in all—or else what you're actually seeing is *how to benefit you and yours.* It is the darkness. And it scales. Every cause you've ever fought for had people on the other side of it, and the test is the same: could you fight for yours while they stayed in view? If the fight needed them out of view, the cause was borrowing love's equipment too, whichever side it was on, and that's what fighting for justice by hating the bad guys is.

It's also why the book opened with one person and not with everything. Turning toward has no target. It's the faculty with nothing in the way, and a face is where you notice it first. What the traditions call awakening is the same state with nothing held out at all: not a bigger act of attention, but the covering removed, and the covering was one thing.

---

What is yours is how fast you catch it afterward: days, then hours, then sometimes in the moment. That is trainable, and it is the only part of this that improves with effort.

The only way to end it is by turning people back toward, and the only way to do that is by turning toward yourself.

Every act of integrity, every choice to care when nobody is watching, leaks out into the world, just as your flash of darkness did. Nothing is confined to your skull—and part of you already knows it. Somewhere, somebody you'll never meet is about to give up on the part of them that knows. The pressure on them is the kind you felt from the stranger, and they're a breath from deciding that the part which knew, and couldn't prove it, isn't worth listening to. Whether they hold is made, in a way I can't trace and won't pretend to, of what the rest of us have done. That's the sense in which every last thing you do matters, witnessed or not.

This is what the traditions were for, at their best. Not a set of rules, and not a story about who is damned. A method for getting free of every last trace of self-deception, so that you could help somebody else get free of theirs. That's what the second person did in miniature. And it's the thing that goes wrong fastest. Almost the moment the method is stated it becomes *I'm free and you're not*, or worse, *poor you, still in the grip*, which is contempt with a soft face. The tell is the one you already know. Look at whether some part of you needs them to stay lost. The second stranger didn't, and that's the whole difference between a saint and a scold.

This is the real battle between Good and evil; between Light and darkness—and it is fought in your every thought, word, and deed.
