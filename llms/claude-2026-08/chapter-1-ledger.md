# Chapter 1: the knowledge ledger

*Stateless. What each section of Chapter 1 establishes, who knows it, and what it leaves open. The chapter is a detective story about one glance, and it fails whenever a sentence forgets whose knowledge it's reporting. Run the knowledge check (CLAUDE.md) against this table before and after any edit to the chapter.*

## The three frames

Every sentence about the first stranger is in one of three frames, and a sentence that drifts between them is the regression this file exists to catch.

- **Outside.** What the reader saw and can verify: his tone, his gaze, the funnel, the intensifying. Marked by "you could sense," "notice," "look at."
- **Inside.** What it was like for him, as far as the reader can reconstruct it: what he believed, what felt obvious to him. Marked by "to him," "from where he stood," "he 'knows.'"
- **Held.** What he knew and didn't let himself know. This is the only frame in which "he knew" and "he didn't know" are both true, and the chapter's whole argument is that it exists. Marked by "in limbo," "some part of him."

The two collapses: writing a held fact as inside ("he didn't know") makes him an accident; writing it as outside ("he knew") makes him a villain. Chapter 1a names these as the book's two failure modes. The Cause names them as "mustache-twirling villain" and "nothing to hold him responsible for."

## The regression rules

1. **Never write "whatever it was" about something the reader has been told.** If The Cause has said what he did, later sections may only be uncertain about something else (what his conscience registered, why he needs it, how he does it). Name the uncertainty exactly.
2. **Every "he knew" and "he didn't know" carries its frame.** "To him," "from where he stood," "some part of him," "you could see." An unmarked "he knew" is a villain sentence; an unmarked "he didn't know" is an accident sentence.
3. **One section, one open question.** Each section ends by opening the question the next section answers, and no section reopens a question already closed. The two big ones are *why* (closed by The Why) and *how* (closed by The How). If a later section seems to answer why or how again, it's an echo; cut it or make it say something new.
4. **The sum-known sentence at the hinge.** At the end of the first Tell section the chapter says what's known and names both open questions. That sentence is the reader's map. Don't add a second one elsewhere.
5. **"We all know this" is a scope claim.** It appears three times (chapter-1.md, The Cause; the Taunt; the close). It's allowed as the seed of Chapter 5's "contempt only works because everybody already knows it," but each instance is a claim about everyone and the Fable item on the phenomenology seam applies.
6. **Common knowledge versus learned.** *Common knowledge:* turning away from one's true nature is required for harm, and harm is what turning away is for; everyone knows this about everyone; the reader verifies it in the gaze experiment and acts on it in the two-strangers scene. *Learned:* recognizing the turned-away state on a face or in a message, and the social form called contempt; a child feels something eerie and can't read it; the sigh is the learned form from the sending end. The machine needs both: the learned reading supplies the signal, the common knowledge supplies its meaning (he has turned away in order to do something to you, and that is what licenses harm without bottom). The regression is putting the meaning inside the gaze (making the look innate), or calling the common knowledge an overclaim (making the fact learned). Both have happened in this session.

## The chain

The abductive structure of the chapter, from what the reader checked to the recursion. Any rebuild of a section is checked against this, not against memory.

*Checked by the reader:* the message "this is all you are" arrived without words; it licenses harm with no bottom and every road funnels; it felt aimed (tracking, closing exits, intensifying under exposure); he seemed sincere; you dug in, and the harsher second stranger didn't produce that; nothing either of you could hold up as proof.

1. **Limbo.** Aimed rules out accident. Sincere rules out knowing, since planted evidence is worthless to the planter. Adapting under exposure rules out blindness. The only state that fits all three is limbo. (The Tell.)
2. **Something registers as wrong.** Limbo exists only to do what conscience would stop, from the reader's own case. So he needed it for the same reason. (The Tell's hinge; the gap in v6.5 was here.)
3. **What he hides is your sameness.** From his side nothing is wrong. So the wrong thing is hidden: his conscience registers a life like his; he knows what overriding it is because he's doing it; so he can't rule out that you're the same; so he may be harming a redeemable person; and the cost of admitting it is his superiority. What he's in limbo about is your limbo. (The Why.)
4. **The precise combination.** He needs proof only you can supply. He can't be openly cruel: that implicates him, not you. So only tone, gaze, manner, the tone of a message: whatever can't be audited. And what must come through is two things at once, aimed and unappealable, because unintentional gets pity and appealable loses the sting. Each feature is forced by something the reader felt. (The How's three reasons; v6.5's Constraints.)
5. **The hiding is the revealing.** He can't know he's sending it. He doesn't have to: the turned-away state is perceptible as absence, so being in it is the signal. (The How.)
6. **The recursion.** For this to work you must read the signal, learned from both ends, and know what it means, the common knowledge. He counts on both without avowing either. The sigh shows you count on the same two when you send. So the transaction runs on knowledge both have and neither can say, and it requires you to be in limbo too: you recognize it and dig in anyway. (What He Could Count On.)
7. **Loop, Agent, Taunt.** Each round adds conscience pressure and demands more proof, to the end; the thing that hides from him by showing itself to you is not him in the usual sense; the message under the words, and the lesson it teaches.
