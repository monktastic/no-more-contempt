# Appendix 1: What Contempt Presupposes

*The three appendices were written with Claude's help.*

*For the reader who wants the mechanism taken apart. Nothing in the book rests on this; the book rests on what you found when you looked. Section 1 builds the trap up from one sentence, ends on what it turns out to presuppose, and then says what I think is really going on. Section 2 sorts all of it into what you checked, what's derived, and what's objection, for whoever wants that.*

---

## 1. The trap

The particular limbo called contempt is hiding something (X) from yourself while revealing it to them. And what is X? The fact that you're hiding X from yourself while revealing it to them. It is self-referential, with no base case. So how does it converge on anything meaningful?

Because hiding-while-revealing is the universal signature of being up to no good while pretending (in fact, believing) it's righteous. You depend on this fact being common knowledge,[^ck] because look at what happens when it is.

A look is enough to cause harm, because both of you already know what a turned-away face aimed at someone is for. Here is what that one look delivers to them, in the order the pieces land:

- **The turn.** You've turned away from them, and they read it.
- **The intent.** They know what an aimed turn is for. You mean harm.
- **The wanting.** You want them to recognize the intent. If you didn't, this would be a leak, not a message.
- **The injury.** Their recognizing it is already the harm; no separate deed has to follow. But the recognition is also meant to produce a reaction, and the reaction is going to become your proof.
- **The disavowal.** You'll deny the intention if they name it, and you'll believe the denial.
- **The proof.** So their protest becomes evidence of the verdict that provoked it, and they know that in advance.

The injury is that they know you mean harm, and that you mean them to know it, and that you're not leaving any evidence of this intent behind, which is the harm you mean. The act's purpose is its own reception; take away either half and there's nothing left of the other.

Notice how this differs from every other harm. In ordinary harm, the message and the injury are two things. You turn away, that lets you do the deed, and the deed does the damage. **In contempt there is no separate deed.** The look is the deed, and there is nothing else.

And the fact that there's nothing else—no harm to point to—is itself part of what's communicated, making it part of the harm, without there being anything to point to, which itself is communicated, and so on. The last two pieces, your denial and their protest turning into your evidence, aren't added on. They follow from there being nothing to point to, and they know that in advance, which is part of what lands.

Notice how it differs from pure malice. All malice hides one thing from the one doing it: the sameness of the target. This is true even in the sadist. But malice as such needs neither of two things contempt needs. It need not be received (the target can be asleep while the deed is done), and where it does demand reception (a threat, a sadist's display) it needn't disavow itself as righteous; the honest villain says *yes, I mean you harm, and no, you're not my kind.* Contempt needs both. The target must receive the harmful intention, and the sender must sincerely deny that it's harmful. The trap is made by that conjunction and by nothing less. It's why malice is frightening and contempt is maddening.

This is what makes contempt uniquely powerful at spreading evil: it causes harm, but the harm is invisible and cannot be attributed to the aggressor.

### The punchline

We tell ourselves that "bad people" are a different kind of thing: that they have no better nature to turn from, or that something in them we don't have makes them turn from it. One way or another, we locate an essential difference in them. Contempt relies on there being none. The look is aimed at the better nature the verdict says they lack. And it works by pushing them away from it: how far they turn depends on what's done to them, the way it does for you. You counted on their being what you are, from five sides:

- They read the move the way you'd have read it.
- There's a standard the two of you share, since what they felt was hypocrisy, and hypocrisy needs a shared standard and a departure from it.
- The standard is care, and that turning away is the departure from it: what you're like when nothing is in the way, and what happens when something is.
- The care in them is still there, obscured and not gone, since that's what the look is aimed at. If darkness were all they were, there would be nothing left for your contempt to corrupt.
- They can still turn back, since the whole point of the message was to push them further away, and you know from your own case that the same road runs back.

Each of those is the opposite of what the verdict says. The verdict says *this is all you are*: no sameness, no shared standard, nothing left to reach, nothing to turn back. And the act can't work unless all of that is false, and it's common knowledge between you that it's false: each of you knows it, knows the other knows it, as far up as anyone cares to go, without either of you going there.[^ck] That part needs no look. Nobody was told it; everyone has been on both ends. What the look adds is a second piece of common knowledge: that you're turned away from them, now, on purpose. You know the standard you're breaking, you mean them to see that you know, and you watch them see it, and each of you sees the other seeing. Both have to stay unsaid. Saying them wouldn't tell either of you anything new. It would put them on the record, and the verdict couldn't survive being heard. Contempt can tell someone *this is all you are* only by addressing the capacity in them that the verdict denies exists. Its explicit message denies equality. Its successful operation presupposes it.

And look at what those five amount to, stated without the labels. 

- What's looking out of them is no different in nature from what's looking out of you. 
- The mind's nature is care. 
- The confusion covering it is not the same thing as it, and doesn't touch it. 
- The covering is a turning away, not a substance. 
- Recognition is available, and is the way back. 

Anyone who practices will have heard all five before, as the view. Here they arrive as the conditions your own contempt was counting on, in the one act that denies them loudest. You didn't have to believe them. You had to have been using them, and you were.

One more thing while the word is in front of you. Evil, in this book, names the turning: your own caring held out of view so that theirs can be. Contempt is that turning made into a message. Two things the word usually carries, and both are wrong, and you already know they're wrong. The first is size. We reserve "evil" for the extreme, but watch what cutting in line does to you. The cost is thirty seconds. The heat is out of all proportion to thirty seconds, because what you're reacting to isn't the cost. It's the exception: somebody put you out of view to get ahead, and let you see it. You can find the same thing from inside: even a small flash of contempt is shameful to admit, out of all proportion to its size. The smallest morsel carries the whole. The second is outcomes. We think "bad" is about damage done, but the friend who forgot your birthday did more damage by any accounting than the friend who made one small cutting remark, and the remark is the one you're still thinking about a week later. Desecrating an enemy's corpse does no damage at all, and everyone knows it's wrong (Appendix 3 comes back to this). Two acts identical from outside can have opposite effects. What you're tracking, in every one of those, is the turning, and you were tracking it before this book named it.

### What I think is really happening

*From here on I'm telling you what I believe. The argument stops at this door; nothing practical depends on walking through it.*

Here's my read. The care isn't something awareness has. It's what awareness is. If that's right, then evil is just awareness turned away from its own nature, and the only way to do it to someone else is to forget it in yourself first. Limbo, turning away, the parasite: three names for that one move, depending on where you're standing.

It also explains why the knowledge is common. Nothing tells what's looking out of you apart from what's looking out of them. Not that there's one awareness. Only that there's nothing to tell them apart, which is all the mutual recognition needs. To know this in yourself is already to know it in them, and to know they know it in you, etc.

So nothing in the encounter belongs to a separate thing called the darkness. Everything doing anything, the sending, the hiding, the reading, the answering, is the light: one faculty, in two bodies, turned against itself in one of them. The message that denies the sameness is made of the sameness, and nothing else could receive it. Even the concealment runs on it. You don't hide the intention and then let it slip; what you show them is that you're hiding it.

And that's what waking up is, on this reading. Nothing gets added. What the two of you were already counting on, silently, becomes something one of you can say. Contempt is the act that keeps it from being said while both of you go on knowing it.

Here's one way to check whether I'm right, and it runs opposite to what you'd expect. If contempt were an accurate perception of badness, then the clearer someone sees, the more contempt they'd feel toward the worst people. If instead the verdict is your own turning away showing itself, then clarity should keep the perception of the harm and dissolve the verdict. Which do we see? The people we call evil hold the most contempt, not the least. And the people we'd call wise can look at the worst person, see everything you see, and no verdict forms. Not because they're soft. Because nothing in them needs it. 

That's not proof, but the two accounts predict opposite things, and only one of them fits. It also tells you what *now I finally see what they really are* is: the darkness is real, and the *really are* is your own turning away, reading itself as insight, and you can only see it that way once you've lost touch with the thing that would have recognized them. And the further gone someone is, the more of their own darkness they're afraid of, so the more of it they have to see in other people. That's why the most lost need the most enemies.

Where this stops. The argument gets you this far: the care is present, it's obscured rather than gone, it can be turned back, and it's the baseline that turning away departs from. It does not get you that care is what awareness *is*. But notice how little room that leaves. "Missing in some" is out, because even the psychopath appears turned away. Try to picture one doing what they do with the kind eyes you held at the start of Chapter 1, the ones with the recognition in them. You can't, and nobody has reported the case. If the care were simply missing in someone, harm from that person would need no turning away, and would look like nothing in particular. It never does. "One drive among others" is out too: a drive gets outweighed while you're still looking, and this one can't be; you have to stop looking first, and it takes work to keep from looking. What's left to a naturalist is a universal human faculty that can't be outweighed and happens to have an evolutionary history. That differs from my reading only about origin, and about whether it belongs to awareness as such or only to minds like ours, which is a question no human experiment touches. What decides between those isn't the argument. It's what you find under your own hiding when the hiding stops.[^godel] Appendix 3 lays out the correspondences, the evidence for my reading, and the test that would count against it.

## 2. For the lawyers among you

*Everything above, sorted into what you observed, what's derived from it, and what's objection. Nobody has to read this.*

**What you checked.** O1: you couldn't give a stranger the gaze while looking at them; harm requires holding the person out of view. The converse holds with a purpose attached.[^converse] O2: the message tracked its target's exits and got stronger when pointed at (*you're just projecting*), and the flash behind it had to be aimed, land, and go unrecorded, none of it calculated: holding someone out of view takes work. O3: on the receiving end, what lands is hypocrisy, not threat; the sender appeals to your capacity to recognize the violation while treating you as the kind of thing that has none, and from the sending end you count on exactly that. O4: hold a stranger's eyes and the wanting that they be all right arrives with the looking: seeing carries care. O5: in the limbo recall the knowledge wasn't new, and when the person who caught you without a verdict made it safe to look, the care was there: turning away obscures the care and doesn't remove it. Each is a claim about experience, open to a reader who looked and found otherwise.

**What's derived, and from what.** The punchline's five are one fact from five sides: there's no essential difference between you and the target. The observations ground the sides separately. The first two of the punchline's five follow from O3 alone: shared standing and a surviving capacity, since the act's effect depends on a capacity the verdict says they don't have. The third, that the standard is care and turning away the departure, needs O4 and O5: hypocrisy shows a shared standard, and O4 says what it is. The fourth and fifth, present, obscured, movable, follow from O5 and from the act's needing them to be pushable. Nothing is assumed. And the identification the book's original title made, that contempt is evil: by O1 the harm and the turning are one event; by O4 what's turned from is your own caring; by O5 what turns away obscures and adds nothing. So evil, at the level of the person, names the turning, and contempt is the turning made into a message. It isn't a judgment of anyone.

**Necessary and sufficient.** Two conditions make contempt: it's common knowledge between the two people that there's no essential difference between them (call it C), and the sender is hiding that from himself (call it H). Every feature of the trap needs one or both. The verdict wounds, and licenses, only because it comes from the target's own kind (C). It reads as hypocrisy because the target knows the sender knows the standard applies to him too (C, two levels). It reads as intended rather than as a slip because, at any level where the knowing stopped, *maybe he doesn't realize I see it* would open, and a slip can be shrugged off; only common knowledge closes every exit (C, all levels). The sender's denial is sincere, so the target's protest can serve as proof, and nothing can be said aloud without the verdict collapsing (H). Take H away and keep C, and you get the second stranger: fierce, no verdict, and the target doesn't dig in. Take C away and keep H, and you get malice nobody receives; you don't send messages to a stump. Take both away and you get indifference. And given both, nothing else is needed: the look reads as hypocrisy, as intended, and as unanswerable, and the target's defiance becomes the sender's evidence. The one further requirement is the channel, that turning away shows in a face, and that is what the encounter's own common knowledge is built from (the footnote to section 1).

**The two conditions, from the sender's side.** C is what the intention aims with: a part of the sender tracks the target's reactions and reads them through what both know, which is why the look reads as intended; it is. H is what the target reads: *I know what I'm doing to you* is the intention, *I genuinely believe it's righteous* is the unawareness, and the two together are the sender's limbo, delivered. H does one more thing on the sender's side. Because he can't see his part in the target's reaction, the reaction shows up to him as the target's nature: the bad essence he perceives is his own act, unseen as his.

**Two things it gets confused with.**

The **reflection.** Around the injury there's a tower of mutual recognition: they recognize that you meant them to recognize it, you know they recognize it, they know you know. Those levels add nothing to the injury; each reflects the same thing, which is why the mirrors feel bottomless and why the bottom is never new. Only the first few levels are ever in play. The rest are available, which is all common knowledge asks; nobody has to climb them. Why the ground under them is shared at all is a further question, taken up under "What I think is really happening" and marked there as belief. Nor does the tower restart the self-reference of section 1. The self-reference is in the content (what's hidden-and-revealed is the hiding-and-revealing), and on its own it has no base case. The tower is infinite only in levels, and every level is about the same fixed content: the convention that hiding-while-revealing means harm believed righteous, which comes before the encounter. That convention is what the loop bottoms out on.

The **loop in time.** If they protest, you take the protest as evidence of the verdict that provoked it, the renewed verdict sends the message again, and the hall of mirrors goes on for years. That's not a depth of recognition. It's a feedback loop.

**What is not claimed.** Not that turning away is sufficient for harm; it also produces numbness and paralysis with nobody harmed. Not the cold form; the argument is about contempt, the aimed form, and the general rule rests on O1. Not universality from this page; O1 to O5 are stated for everyone and open to counterexample, and the deduction needs only your own instance. Not that every target can be reached in practice; in principle the faculty is there to be turned, and for some it may not happen in a lifetime.

*For the reader who wants the fixed point written down:* let G(P) mean *make the target know, without recourse, that you intend P.* Then the injury H satisfies H = G(H), a fixed point in the ordinary sense, with nothing inside it but its ground, the sameness held out of view and the fact of holding it. You can write the reflection as a tower, M for the turn and F(P) for *hide P from yourself while showing it to them*, and it doesn't collapse as propositions, since "I'm hiding that I'm hiding it" is a different sentence from "I'm hiding it." What collapses is the acting: there is one act, turning away from the sameness, and every level is that act under a further description, which is why nobody has to climb it and why it fits in one look.

## Where else to look

**On common knowledge.** Lewis, _Convention_ (1969); Aumann (1976), for the definition. Chwe, _Rational Ritual_ (2001), for the public event that usually produces it. The modal-logic literature on unawareness, for knowledge present in a state without being available for use. The footnote to section 1 is the contrast; the last paragraph of section 2 develops the analogue under its own premises.

**On what it is.** Bateson et al. (1956) on the double bind: a demand, a contradicting demand at another level, and no way to comment on the contradiction. Pinker, Nowak & Lee (2008), "The logic of indirect speech," on content conveyed _so as not to_ become common knowledge. The trap is an innuendo whose subject is itself, with one difference: the sender makes the uptake visible, so it does become common knowledge between the two, and stays unacknowledged. Goffman on face-work, for the auditable surface the act hides behind.

**On limbo.** Fingarette, _Self-Deception_ (1969): self-deception as declining to _spell out_ what you're doing. Sartre's bad faith; Mele as the deflationary rival. Kunda (1990) on motivated reasoning: the search stops at the wanted conclusion.

**On the leak being functional.** Trivers; von Hippel & Trivers (2011); Kurzban (2010). Adjacent, with one inversion: here the leak is the payload.

**On the two strangers, measured.** Tangney & Dearing, _Shame and Guilt_ (2002): "I did a bad thing" repairs, "I am bad" hides and attacks. Braithwaite, _Crime, Shame and Reintegration_ (1989): stigmatizing versus reintegrative shaming, with outcome data. Gilligan, _Violence_ (1996): shame as the universal precursor to violence.

**On turning away as a perceptual event.** Harris & Fiske (2006): social-perception regions go quiet for dehumanized groups. Gray & Wegner on moral typecasting: seen as agent, unseen as sufferer. Bandura on moral disengagement, which catalogs limbo's toolkit.

**On the arrangement.** Girard on the scapegoat. Baumeister, _Evil_ (1997), for the exterior view of the license, the terminus, and the myth of pure evil. Browning, _Ordinary Men_ (1992).

**On the shadow.** Jung.

**On privation.** Augustine, _Enchiridion_.

[^converse]: The other direction says that when you hold a person out of view, it's in order to do something their being in view would stop, so the holding-out and the permission are one event seen from two sides. That needs one distinction. Walking through an airport, you don't take in the hundred people around you as people. They're in the background, and that isn't turning away: if one of them dropped a bag and started crying, they'd come forward into view at once, and you'd feel the pull. Turning away is keeping someone out of view when they'd otherwise come forward, because their reality would place a claim on you. People also turn from each other to escape grief, or fear, or a claim they can't meet, and that isn't this page's subject.

[^ck]: Game theorists call something common knowledge when everyone knows it, everyone knows that everyone knows it, and so on without end. Nobody climbs that tower. Lewis's version asks only that each person have reason to believe at every level, and a shared basis to reason from. Usually that basis is a public event: a trusted party says the thing aloud where everyone can see everyone hear it. The child in *The Emperor's New Clothes* adds no information; everyone already knew. He makes it common knowledge. The term has two uses on this page, and they're different. The first needs no encounter: that there's no essential difference between you, no better nature missing in them and nothing in them you don't have that turns them from it. It's common knowledge the way a convention is: nobody was told it, and everyone has been on both ends. The rules the page leans on, that an aimed turn is for harm and that hiding-while-revealing is the signature of harm dressed as righteousness, are that one fact seen from outside. The second is made in the encounter: that you're turned away from them now, on purpose. The encounter supplies its own public event, a silent one: the aimed look, its familiar meaning, the look landing, and each of you seeing the other see it land. It can be silent because turning away shows. Unsaid is not the same as not common knowledge. What's missing, for both, is acknowledgment. Nothing is on the record, so nothing can be quoted to anyone else, or to yourself. Neither use reaches the reading under "What I think is really happening."

[^godel]: A note on Gödel, since the comparison will occur to some readers. It's an analogy, not an argument, and the analogy is to the second theorem. A formal system strong enough to matter can't certify its own consistency using only its own resources; a stronger system can assess it from outside, and then the question moves to the stronger one. The lesson contempt teaches, demote the part that knows and be only the part that proves, asks the reasoning part to vouch for itself in the same way, and the reasoning that justifies an act can't show, by producing more justification, that it hasn't been recruited. That's the book's claim that you can't calculate your way to goodness, in Gödel's shape. What the theorem doesn't supply is that the outside faculty is sound: it gives truth conditional on consistency, established from outside, and the analogue, that the part that knows is right if it isn't itself turned away, is a problem the book carries rather than a result it has.
