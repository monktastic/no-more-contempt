# Appendix 1: What Contempt Presupposes

*The three appendices were written with Claude's help.*

*For the reader who wants the mechanism taken apart. I didn't arrive at any of this analytically. It came as direct insight, in moments like the ones the book describes, and I've been trying to find an analytical description of it ever since. This appendix runs the opposite way from the book: I start from a few assumptions you don't have to hold, show the one shape they assemble, and set it beside what we observe. Not a proof; the inference that the simplest explanation of what you found is the one these assumptions give. Nothing in the book rests on it.*

*Section 1 is the construction and the comparison, ending with what I believe (and saw) past the point the argument reaches. Section 2 sorts it into what you checked, what's derived, and what's objection.*

---

## 1. The trap

Start here: my better nature is what sees myself in others. In particular, it sees theirs. Take that as an assumption; the book is where you check it. If it holds, a great deal follows at once:

- I know my enemy has one. 
- I know that to harm him I have to turn away from it, since what sees myself in him won't let the harm go while it's looking. And I know there's no other way: the turn is for harm, and harm needs the turn. So seeing him as a different kind of thing isn't a perception. It's the turn itself.
- I know he can be pushed away from his, in the same way I can. 
- I know he'll perceive my *turning away* as evil, because that's how I see his.
- I know he can see all of this, and that I can see him seeing it, and so on, as far up as either of us cares to go—because what's being seen is itself a faculty of seeing, and it sees back.[^ck]

When I'm turned away, I know it, but it's obscured from me. I call this "limbo." Normally I want to hide that state from my enemy. Contempt is the one place where I want to show it, because it turns him away too, which I will read as evil, making him look like something fundamentally different from me. In other words, the turned-away state propagates itself, and it will turn out that nothing else can propagate it.

Here is what the look automatically delivers to him, in the order the pieces land:

- **The turn.** I've turned away from him, and he reads it.
- **The intent.** He knows what an aimed turn is for. I mean harm.
- **The wanting.** I want him to recognize the intent. If I didn't, this would be a leak, not a message.
- **The injury.** His recognizing it is already an injury: someone who can see him has put him out of view on purpose and let him watch. But it is also meant to produce a reaction, and the reaction is going to become my proof.
- **The disavowal.** I'll deny the intention if he names it, and I'll believe the denial, in the limbo way: knowing, and not letting myself know.
- **The proof.** So his protest becomes evidence of the verdict that provoked it, and he knows that in advance.
- **The license.** He's watched someone posing as righteous cheat on what both of us serve, and get away with it. That licenses him to do the same: *why the hell shouldn't I?*

The injury is that he knows I mean harm, and that I mean him to know it, and that I'm leaving him nothing that would settle it, which is the harm I mean. The act's purpose is its own reception and its own concealment: to be read by him and denied by me. Take away either half and there's nothing left of the other.

In ordinary harm, the message and the injury are two things. I turn away, that lets me do the deed, and the deed does the damage. **In contempt there is no separate deed.** The look is the deed, and there is nothing else.

The list has no end, but it has a small source. Everything on it, and anything that could be added, follows from one sentence read by two people who both know what a turned-away look is for: *I know what I'm doing to you; I won't admit it, even to myself; and you can see all of this.* Whatever we can work out from that is in the look, and so is the fact that we can work it out. So no list of what the look carries can close: write down an item and you've made the next one, since both of us can now know that as well. That isn't a gap in the account. An account of the look can't be a list. It's the sentence, plus the fact that what the sentence generates never ends. That's the hall of mirrors, described from outside.

All of that endless meaning rides on one act: I'm hiding the turn from myself while showing it to him. But to hide it from myself I have to hide that I'm hiding it, and that shows too, and so on up. Every level is the one act, the turn, under one more description. That's a fixed point: the recursion never contains anything but the turn.

One implication falls straight out of the fixed point, and it's the one the trap depends on: there's nothing to quote. A secret whose only content is the hiding-and-showing of it leaves no evidence except the showing, and the showing is evidence of a particular kind: gaze, tone, timing, recognized by both of us and conclusive to neither. He has everything he needs to know and nothing that settles the dispute. And the look needs no words, because hiding-while-revealing is the universal signature of being up to no good while believing it's righteous. That's what lets one look deliver the whole list.

All of it stands on the assumption I started with. Take it away: a being with nothing to turn from could see that I'm a fraud, but he wouldn't feel it as a betrayal, and the betrayal is what the look needs. Hold on to that. The verdict says he's a different kind of thing. The look only works on someone who isn't. I'll come back to what that costs the verdict, once the pieces are on the table.

Notice how it differs from pure malice. All malice hides one thing from the one doing it: the sameness of the target. This is true even in the sadist. But malice as such needs neither of two things contempt needs. It need not be received (the target can be asleep while the deed is done), and where it does demand reception (a threat, a sadist's display) it needn't disavow itself as righteous; the honest villain says *yes, I mean you harm, and no, you're not my kind.* Contempt needs both. The target must receive the harmful intention, and the sender must deny that it's harmful and believe the denial, in the limbo way. The trap is made by that conjunction and by nothing less. It's why malice is frightening and contempt is maddening.

### What the need was looking for

Part of me needs to be a different kind of thing from the people I despise. Why I need that, I'll come to. Nothing true can satisfy it, since there is no such difference. So if it's going to be satisfied, something false will have to be made to look true, and contempt is the instrument built for the job. Look at how well it fits.

It manufactures the evidence. The look pushes him further from his better nature, and how far he turns depends on what's done to him, the way it does for me. His hardening is the difference I needed to see, produced on demand. And I can't see my part in producing it. The fixed point leaves nothing to quote, for him and for me alike, so his reaction arrives looking like his nature. The bad essence I perceive is my own act, unseen as mine.

It seals every exit. Deny it and that's the kind of person he is; protest and that's proof; stay calm and he doesn't care. Whatever he does feeds the verdict, and he knows it, which is part of what he's being shown. Nothing is on the record, so nothing can be quoted to anyone, including to myself.

And it reproduces. The injury is invisible and can't be attributed to me. The reaction it provokes isn't only evidence: it's him turning away in turn, licensed by the betrayal, and carrying it to whoever he meets next. Reception is the injury; corruption is the harm the injury licenses, and it's how the thing spreads. It's also the only way it spreads. Pushing him from his better nature is harm, so it needs my turn; and the license needs my hypocrisy, which is my turn seen from his side. Turned toward, I can be as harsh as I like; it may hurt, but it hands him no license. The license travels only through the turn. Do this enough times, with enough people, and the world fills with the kind of person the need requires. Then the need looks like perception. I'm not making anyone bad; look at them.

That's the whole arrangement: a need that can't be met by anything true, met by arranging for something false to look true, with the arranging kept out of my own view by the same act that does it.

### The punchline

I said I'd come back to what the look costs the verdict. The verdict says *this is all you are*: no better nature, nothing shared, nothing left to reach, nothing to turn back. Now count what the look needed. He had to read the move as I would have. What he felt had to be hypocrisy, which needs a standard we share and my departure from it. The standard had to be care and the departure the turn, or the hypocrisy would have no content. The care in him had to be there, obscured and not gone, or the look would have nothing to land on and nothing to corrupt. And he had to be able to turn back, or there would be no point in pushing him further. The first two come from the encounter alone; the rest lean on what you checked in your own case, and Section 2 says which check carries which.

Every one of those is the opposite of the verdict, and every one was common knowledge between us before the encounter began. The encounter taught neither of us anything. What it did was put me on one side of that knowledge and him on the other, with both of us knowing which, and neither able to say so. Saying it wouldn't tell either of us anything new. It would put it on the record, and the verdict can't survive being heard. Contempt can tell someone *this is all you are* only by addressing the capacity in him that the verdict denies exists. Its explicit message denies equality. Its successful operation presupposes it.

Stated without the labels, the five conditions are these. What's looking out of him is no different in nature from what's looking out of me. Care is the baseline, what I'm like when nothing is in the way. The confusion covering it is not the same thing as it, and doesn't touch it. The covering is a turning away, not a substance. Recognition is available, and is the way back. Anyone who practices will have heard all five before, as the view. Here they arrive as the conditions my own contempt was counting on, in the one act that denies them loudest. I didn't have to believe them. I had to have been using them, and I was. So were you.

### Why it had to be this way

Take two things you checked. Harm needs the turn: with the person fully in view, it won't go. And the turn is only ever needed for harm: nobody hides from himself what he did out of decency, and remorse never runs backward. Put those together and what you turn from is the baseline, and the turning is a departure from it, not a second baseline. That's all "absence" means here. Not that nothing is happening; that the thing happening has no ground of its own.

Add the opening assumption, and a look that is aimed and meant to be read. Then knowing harm between two such beings has exactly one shape, and it's the one just built. Here it is as a chain, each link resting on the one before:

1. The hiding shows, because the faculty I hide from is the one he reads with.
2. The showing is the harm, because there's no other deed.
3. The harm is deniable, because its only content is the hiding.
4. The denial is sincere, because the turn hides it from me too.
5. His protest is my proof, because the denial is sincere, I read him through the verdict, and he knows both.
6. It maddens rather than frightens, because he knows it in advance.
7. It injures, because the standard is shared; it offers him the license, because he's watched the breach; and when he takes the license it spreads, and only from the turned-away state, because the breach is the turn.

The first four links are the act. The fifth and sixth are what the act does to the one it lands on. The seventh is what he can do with it, and the chain forces the offer, not the taking.

Not one of those is a separate fact. Each is the turn, seen from a different position: mine, his, an onlooker's, the next day's. That's why there's no slack anywhere, and the lack of slack is evidence of a particular kind. It's what you'd expect if the thing had no nature of its own to vary: an absence can act only through the presence it's the absence of, so it borrows the eyes and the reasons, it has to hide from what would stop it, and its hiding shows to whoever shares what it's hiding from. Every move is fixed by the structure of that presence. A thing with a nature of its own would have to be held to this shape by something further: whatever makes it hide, whatever makes it need the target's better nature, whatever keeps it from working with the eyes open. An absence needs nothing further. The shape is what it is. That's the sense in which the precision in the chain is what privation looks like from inside. That is what "an absence that behaves like a presence" means, and why it isn't a metaphor.

Set the rival beside it. Say evil is a nature instead: a thing some people have, or a drive everyone carries that sometimes wins. Then harm could sometimes be done with the eyes open. Remorse would sometimes run backward: a drive that merely lost would leave regret at losing, now and then mourning, and someone, somewhere, would feel the sickening sense of having gone against himself over an act of decency. The look could injure without the target's better nature. Harshness without a verdict would be answered as harshness. The clearest-seeing would hold the most contempt. None of those happens, in any case I've found. Each is something the nature account has to arrange separately not to happen, and something the absence account can't produce in the first place. That's what the simplest explanation means: not the shortest, but the one where one fact predicts the rest. And somewhere, on the rival account, there would be a conscience that runs the other way. I haven't found one, and I'd want to hear of it.

Evil, in this book, names the turning: your own caring held out of view so that theirs can be. Contempt is that turning made into a message. Two things the word usually carries, and both are wrong, and you already know they're wrong. The first is size. We reserve "evil" for the extreme, but watch what cutting in line does to you. The cost is thirty seconds. The heat is out of all proportion to thirty seconds, because what you're reacting to isn't the cost. It's the exception: somebody put you out of view to get ahead, and let you see it. You can find the same thing from inside: even a small flash of contempt is shameful to admit, out of all proportion to its size. The smallest morsel carries the whole, because there's nothing in it but the turn, and the turn has no size. The second is outcomes. We think "bad" is about damage done, but the friend who forgot your birthday did more damage by most accountings than the friend who made one small cutting remark, and the remark is the one you're still thinking about a week later. Desecrating an enemy's corpse does the dead man no damage, and everyone knows it's wrong (Appendix 3 comes back to this). Two acts identical from outside can have opposite effects. What you're tracking, in every one of those, is the turning, and you were tracking it before this book named it.

One more thing the shape forces. Limbo isn't one state among several the trap could use. It's the only one. Fully unaware, nothing aims, and he reads a slip. Fully aware, I'm looking at him, and the harm won't go; or it's honest malice, which frightens and doesn't madden. So the knowledge has to be present and operating and refused, all at once. That tells you something about the faculty: it can't be switched off, only turned from, which is why the one move available against it is not looking. And since hiding the turn means hiding the hiding, the not-looking is never partial. The whole stance turns, and the room stays lit. Nobody is surprised by their own wrong when they finally see it. The knowledge was there the whole time, which is what "it wasn't new" was reporting.

Where this stops. The argument gets you this far: the care is present, it's obscured rather than gone, it can be turned back, and it's the baseline that turning away departs from. It does not get you that care is what awareness *is*. But notice how little room that leaves. "Missing in some" is the one I can't rule out, and here is why I doubt it. The con man's warmth is the most reported case there is, and it's performed; what I've never found reported is the recognition. Try to picture one doing what they do while seeing the person in front of them, the way the second stranger saw you. I can't, and I'd want to hear of the case. If the care were simply missing in someone, harm from that person would need no turning away, and would look like nothing in particular. I've never seen it look like nothing in particular. "One drive among others" is out too: a drive gets outweighed while you're still looking, and this one can't be; you have to stop looking first, and it takes work to keep from looking. What's left to a naturalist is a universal human faculty that can't be outweighed and happens to have an evolutionary history. That differs from my reading only about origin, and about whether it belongs to awareness as such or only to minds like ours, which is a question no human experiment touches. What decides between those isn't the argument. It's what you find under your own hiding when the hiding stops.[^godel] Appendix 3 lays out the correspondences, the evidence for my reading, and the test that would count against it.

### What I think is really happening

*From here on I'm telling you what I believe, and the one test I know of for it. Nothing practical depends on walking through this door.*

Here's my read. The care isn't something awareness has. It's what awareness is. If that's right, then evil is just awareness turned away from its own nature, and the only way to do it to someone else is to forget it in yourself first. Limbo, turning away, the absence that behaves like a presence: three names for that one move, depending on where you're standing.

It also explains why the knowledge is common. Nothing tells what's looking out of you apart from what's looking out of them. Not that there's one awareness. Only that there's nothing to tell them apart, which is all the mutual recognition needs. To know this in yourself is already to know it in them, and to know they know it in you, etc.

So nothing in the encounter belongs to a separate thing called the darkness. Everything doing anything, the sending, the hiding, the reading, the answering, is the light: one faculty, in two bodies, turned against itself in one of them. The message that denies the sameness is made of the sameness, and nothing else could receive it. Even the concealment runs on it. You don't hide the intention and then let it slip; what you show them is that you're hiding it.

And that's what waking up is, on this reading. Nothing gets added. What the two of you were already counting on, silently, becomes something one of you can say. Contempt is the act that keeps it from being said while both of you go on knowing it.

Here is the rest of what I believe, and I hold it the way the traditions hold their stories. Most of it can be found in *A Course in Miracles* as Kenneth Wapnick reads it, and in the Dzogchen Aspiration of Samantabhadra, where beings arise from a first non-recognition and no one is at fault. I got there by a different road; Appendix 3 says where the roads meet. There was a turning away from my own nature, with nothing behind it: a lapse, not a crime, and not a real departure either, since there's nowhere else to be. What came after was the fear that it was real. Taken as fact, the lapse meant I'd lost my goodness, or never had it, and that was unbearable. So I began looking for goodness the one place a frightened mind can find it: relatively, at the expense of others. If he's the bad one, I'm not. I've been on the lam from my own light ever since, trying to get home by the one road that can't get there, keeping others from theirs. Every look of contempt is a step down that road, and every step makes the separation more real in the world I experience. The only way back is forgiveness, his and mine, which turn out to be one act, since there was never anything to forgive but the turning.

None of this unfolds in time. The appearance of separate people at all, me here and him there, is what the first turning looks like from inside. I know how that sentence sounds. It's the one no check in this book reaches, and I put it here because it's what I found, and because everything above fits it.

Here's one way to check one part of this, the part that says the verdict is not a perception, and it runs opposite to what you'd expect. If contempt were an accurate perception of badness, then the clearer someone sees, the more contempt they'd feel toward the worst people. If instead the verdict is your own turning away showing itself, then clarity should keep the perception of the harm and dissolve the verdict. Which do we see? Take the people others bring their worst to, because they see what's there. They can look at the worst person, see everything you see, and no verdict forms. Not because they're soft. Because nothing in them needs it. 

That's a test, not a proof, and it tests only that part. The two accounts predict opposite things, and what I've seen fits the second. It also tells you what *now I finally see what they really are* is: the darkness is real, and the *really are* is your own turning away, reading itself as insight, and you can only see it that way once you've lost touch with the thing that would have recognized them. And the further gone someone is, the more of their own darkness they're afraid of, so the more of it they have to see in other people. That's why the most lost need the most enemies.

## 2. For the lawyers among you

*Everything above, sorted into what you observed, what's derived from it, and what's objection. Nobody has to read this.*

**What you checked.** O1: you couldn't give a stranger the gaze while looking at them; harm requires holding the person out of view. The converse holds with a purpose attached.[^converse] O2: the message tracked its target's exits and got stronger when pointed at (*you're just projecting*), and the flash behind it had to be aimed, land, and go unrecorded, none of it calculated: holding someone out of view takes work. O3: on the receiving end, what lands is hypocrisy, not threat; the sender appeals to your capacity to recognize the violation while treating you as the kind of thing that has none, and from the sending end you count on exactly that. O4: hold a stranger's eyes, now, until the face becomes a place somebody is looking out of; the wanting that they be all right arrives with the looking: seeing carries care. O5: in the limbo recall the knowledge wasn't new, and when the person who caught you without a verdict made it safe to look, the care was there: turning away obscures the care and doesn't remove it. Each is a claim about experience, open to a reader who looked and found otherwise. They aren't all of one kind: O1, O2 and O5 are recalled, O4 is run on the spot, and the second stranger in O5 is imagined by anyone who hasn't met one. The recalled ones carry the most weight, and the derivation leans on them.

**What's derived, and from what.** The punchline's five are one fact from five sides: there's no essential difference between you and the target. The observations ground the sides separately. The first two of the punchline's five follow from O3 alone: shared standing and a surviving capacity, since the act's effect depends on a capacity the verdict says they don't have. The third, that the standard is care and turning away the departure, needs O4 and O5: hypocrisy shows a shared standard, and O4 says what it is. The fourth and fifth, present, obscured, movable, follow from O5 and from the act's needing them to be pushable. Nothing is assumed beyond the opening assumption and these five. And the identification the book makes, that contempt is evil: by O1 the harm and the turning are one event; by O4 what's turned from is your own caring; by O5 what turns away obscures and adds nothing. So evil, at the level of the person, names the turning, and contempt is the turning made into a message. It isn't a judgment of anyone.

**Necessary and sufficient.** Given a look that is aimed, meant to be read, and carries the verdict *you're a lesser kind of thing*, two conditions make it contempt rather than an insult: it's common knowledge between the two people that there's no essential difference between them (call it C), and the sender is hiding that from himself (call it H). Every feature of the trap needs one or both. The verdict wounds, and licenses, only because it comes from the target's own kind (C). It reads as hypocrisy because the target knows the sender knows the standard applies to him too (C, two levels). It reads as intended rather than as a slip because the target knows the sender meant him to see it, and the sender knows he knows; those first few levels close the exits, and the higher ones are available to anyone who climbs (C, as far as needed). The sender's denial is sincere, so the target's protest can serve as proof, and nothing can be said aloud without the verdict collapsing (H). Take H away and keep C, and you get the second stranger: fierce, no verdict, and the target doesn't dig in. Take C away and keep H, and what you send can frighten or degrade but can't read as hypocrisy: malice, not contempt. Take both away and you get indifference. And given both, nothing else is needed: the look reads as hypocrisy, as intended, and as unanswerable, and the target's defiance becomes the sender's evidence. The aimed look is what the conditions are conditions on; the channel it travels by, that turning away shows in a face, is what the encounter's own common knowledge is built from (the footnote to section 1).

**The two conditions, from the sender's side.** C is what the intention aims with: a part of the sender tracks the target's reactions and reads them through what both know, which is why the look reads as intended; it is. H is what the target reads: *I know what I'm doing to you* is the intention, *I genuinely believe it's righteous* is the unawareness, and the two together are the sender's limbo, delivered. H does one more thing on the sender's side. Because he can't see his part in the target's reaction, the reaction shows up to him as the target's nature: the bad essence he perceives is his own act, unseen as his.

**Two things it gets confused with.**

The **reflection.** Around the injury there's a tower of mutual recognition: they recognize that you meant them to recognize it, you know they recognize it, they know you know. Those levels add nothing to the injury; each reflects the same thing, which is why the mirrors feel bottomless and why the bottom is never new. Only the first few levels are ever in play. The rest are available, which is all common knowledge asks; nobody has to climb them. Why the ground under them is shared at all is a further question, taken up under "What I think is really happening" and marked there as belief. Nor does the tower restart the self-reference of section 1. The self-reference is in the content (what's hidden-and-revealed is the hiding-and-revealing), and its base case is the turn itself. The tower is infinite only in levels, and every level is about that same content, read through a convention that comes before the encounter: hiding-while-revealing means harm believed righteous.

The **loop in time.** If they protest, you take the protest as evidence of the verdict that provoked it, the renewed verdict sends the message again, and the hall of mirrors goes on for years. That's not a depth of recognition. It's a feedback loop.

**What is not claimed.** Not that turning away is sufficient for harm; it also produces numbness and paralysis with nobody harmed. Not the cold form; the argument is about contempt, the aimed form, and the general rule rests on O1. Not universality from this page; O1 to O5 are stated for everyone and open to counterexample, and the deduction needs only your own instance. Not that every target can be reached in practice; in principle the faculty is there to be turned, and for some it may not happen in a lifetime.

## Where else to look

**On common knowledge.** Lewis, _Convention_ (1969); Aumann (1976), for the definition. Chwe, _Rational Ritual_ (2001), for the public event that usually produces it. Barwise, "Three Views of Common Knowledge" (1988), for common knowledge itself as a fixed point, and for the shared-situation view that the footnote's silent public event resembles. Fagin & Halpern (1988) on implicit versus explicit knowledge, for knowing something without having it available for use; limbo holds that gap open on purpose. The footnote to section 1 is the contrast.

**On what it is.** Bateson et al. (1956) on the double bind: a demand, a contradicting demand at another level, and no way to comment on the contradiction. Pinker, Nowak & Lee (2008), "The logic of indirect speech," on content conveyed _so as not to_ become common knowledge. The trap is an innuendo whose subject is itself, with one difference: the sender makes the uptake visible, so it does become common knowledge between the two, and stays unacknowledged. Goffman on face-work, for the auditable surface the act hides behind.

**On limbo.** Butler, "Upon Self-Deceit" (1726). Fingarette, _Self-Deception_ (1969): self-deception as declining to _spell out_ what you're doing. Sartre's bad faith; Mele as the deflationary rival. Kunda (1990) on motivated reasoning: the search stops at the wanted conclusion.

**On the leak being functional.** Trivers; von Hippel & Trivers (2011); Kurzban (2010). Adjacent, with one inversion: here the leak is the payload.

**On contempt, measured.** Gottman, for contempt as the strongest single predictor of divorce in his couples data, and for its description: superiority and disgust, delivered without words.

**On the just gaze.** Murdoch, _The Sovereignty of Good_ (1970): the mother-in-law who re-sees her daughter-in-law.

**On the two strangers, measured.** Tangney & Dearing, _Shame and Guilt_ (2002): "I did a bad thing" repairs, "I am bad" hides and attacks. Braithwaite, _Crime, Shame and Reintegration_ (1989): stigmatizing versus reintegrative shaming, with outcome data. Gilligan, _Violence_ (1996): shame as the universal precursor to violence.

**On turning away as a perceptual event.** Harris & Fiske (2006): social-perception regions go quiet for dehumanized groups. Gray & Wegner on moral typecasting: seen as agent, unseen as sufferer. Bandura on moral disengagement, which catalogs limbo's toolkit.

**On the arrangement.** Girard on the scapegoat. Baumeister, _Evil_ (1997), for the exterior view of the license, the terminus, and the myth of pure evil. Browning, _Ordinary Men_ (1992).

**On the shadow.** Jung.

**On privation.** Augustine, _Enchiridion_.

**On the story under the last section.** Wapnick, _A Vast Illusion_ (1990) and _The Message of A Course in Miracles_ (1997), for the reading of the Course in which the ego is the belief in separation and its one strategy is to keep the guilt out there. Appendix 3 has the correspondences.

[^converse]: The other direction says that when you hold a person out of view, it's in order to do something their being in view would stop, so the holding-out and the permission are one event seen from two sides. That needs one distinction. Walking through an airport, you don't take in the hundred people around you as people. They're in the background, and that isn't turning away: if one of them dropped a bag and started crying, they'd come forward into view at once, and you'd feel the pull. Turning away is keeping someone out of view when they'd otherwise come forward, because their reality would place a claim on you. People also turn from each other to escape grief, or fear, or a claim they can't meet, and that isn't this page's subject.

[^ck]: Game theorists call something common knowledge when everyone knows it, everyone knows that everyone knows it, and so on without end. Nobody climbs that tower. Lewis's version asks only that each person have reason to believe at every level, and a shared basis to reason from. Usually that basis is a public event: a trusted party says the thing aloud where everyone can see everyone hear it. The child in *The Emperor's New Clothes* adds no information; everyone already knew. He makes it common knowledge. The term has two uses on this page, and they're different. The first needs no encounter: that there's no essential difference between you, no better nature missing in them and nothing in them you don't have that turns them from it. It's common knowledge the way a convention is: nobody was told it, and everyone has been on both ends. The rules the page leans on, that an aimed turn is for harm and that hiding-while-revealing is the signature of harm dressed as righteousness, are that one fact seen from outside. The second is made in the encounter: that you're turned away from them now, on purpose. The encounter supplies its own public event, a silent one: the aimed look, its familiar meaning, the look landing, and each of you seeing the other see it land. It can be silent because turning away shows. Unsaid is not the same as not common knowledge. What's missing, for both, is acknowledgment. Nothing is on the record, so nothing can be quoted to anyone else, or to yourself. Neither use reaches the reading under "What I think is really happening."

[^godel]: A note on Gödel, since the comparison will occur to some readers. It's an analogy, not an argument, and the analogy is to the second theorem. A formal system strong enough to matter can't certify its own consistency using only its own resources; a stronger system can assess it from outside, and then the question moves to the stronger one. The lesson contempt teaches, demote the part that knows and be only the part that proves, asks the reasoning part to vouch for itself in the same way, and the reasoning that justifies an act can't show, by producing more justification, that it hasn't been recruited. That's the book's claim that you can't calculate your way to goodness, in Gödel's shape. What the theorem doesn't supply is that the outside faculty is sound. The analogue, that the part that knows is right if it isn't itself turned away, is a problem the book carries rather than a result it has.
