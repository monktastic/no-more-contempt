# Appendix 1: What Contempt Presupposes

*For the reader who wants the mechanism taken apart. I didn't arrive at any of this analytically. It came as direct insight, in moments like the ones the book describes, and I've been trying to find an analytical description of it ever since. This appendix runs the opposite way from the book: I start from a few assumptions you don't have to hold, show the one shape they assemble, and set it beside what we observe. Not a proof; the inference that the simplest explanation of what you found is the one these assumptions give. Nothing in the book rests on it.*

*Section 1 is the construction and the comparison, ending with what I believe (and saw) past the point the argument reaches. Section 2 sorts it into what you checked, what the trap needs, the rivals, and what isn't claimed.*

---

## 1. The trap

The trap rests on three things. If they're true of us, contempt has the shape you've watched it have, every step of it leaning on the sameness it denies. As far as I can find, nothing else naturally produces that shape, which makes the shape evidence for the three.

1. **Turning toward is the good.** To turn toward someone is to face them with my better nature, the part that cares. It sees myself in others; in particular, it sees their better nature. Take that as an assumption; the book is where you check it.
2. **Aimed turning away is evil.** Turning away is the only way to harm: with the person fully in view, I can't bring myself to harm him. And decency never needs it: nobody I've found hides from himself what he did out of decency. You can check both halves: did your wrong need a story, and did your decency? (Harm here means more than damage, which can be an accident: hurting someone because part of me wants them hurt, for my own sake or for its own. A judge can sentence a man without wanting him to suffer. I can stop someone for good reasons and still want it to sting, and then it's harm.)
3. **I know when I've turned, I won't let myself know it, and it shows.** The faculty I turn from can't be switched off, only turned from, so what it knows doesn't go away; it goes into limbo, seen and not seen. And unless I work to hide it, it shows, in a face, to anyone who has the faculty.

By turning away I mean keeping someone out of view when they'd come forward, because their reality would otherwise place a claim on me. And not every turn is aimed. People turn from each other to escape grief, or fear, or a claim they can't meet, and done knowingly, with a story, that is the same turn. This page is about the aimed one.

If those hold, I know my enemy has a better nature, and that he can turn from it the way I can. On this picture, I know the three points from inside, as facts about the faculty that's doing the looking, and seeing that faculty in him, I see someone who knows them too. And I know he can see all of this, and that I can see him seeing it, and so on, as far up as either of us cares to go, because what's being seen is itself a faculty of seeing, and it sees back.[^ck] So the three points above are common knowledge between us, though neither of us could state them, any more than we could state what a face means.

An aimed turn, owned, is malice, and it can come with a verdict: *you're not my kind*. What contempt adds is the pretense that the harm is righteous. The look says _I mean you harm_ and _I’m in the right_ at once, and since the three points are common knowledge, the contradiction is in plain view to both of us, and each of us knows the other sees it. He can see it. I can’t afford to. So I have to show it to him and hide it from myself. That is the third point again, done on purpose and in front of him: the turn’s own limbo, made into a message. That one bind is what turns a threat into a trap. Given the common knowledge, watch what it produces, in the order the pieces land, each resting on the one before:

- **The turn.** I've turned away from him, and he reads it, because turning away shows in a face.
- **The intent.** He knows what an aimed turn is for. I mean harm.
- **The wanting.** I want him to recognize the intent. If I didn't, this would be a leak, not a message.
- **The injury.** His recognizing it is already an injury: someone who can see him, and could turn back to him now, has put him out of view on purpose and let him watch. There's no other deed; the showing is the injury, and it wounds because the standard I'm breaking is one we share. But it is also meant to produce a reaction, and the reaction is going to become my proof.
- **The disavowal.** I'll deny the intention if he names it, and I'll believe the denial, in the limbo way: knowing, and not letting myself know, because the turn hides it from me too.
- **The proof.** So his protest becomes evidence of the verdict that provoked it, because my denial is sincere and I read him through the verdict, and he knows all of that in advance.
- **The license.** He's watched someone posing as righteous cheat on what both of us serve, and get away with it. That offers him the license to do the same: *why the hell can't I?* It offers; it doesn't force. And if he takes it, it spreads, and only from the turned-away state, because the breach is the turn.

The first five are the act. The proof is what I make of his answer, which he knows in advance, and the license is what he can do with it.

In ordinary harm, the message and the injury are two things: I turn away, that lets me do the deed, and the deed does the damage. Here the look is the deed. The act is nothing but its own reception and its own concealment: read by him, denied by me. Take away either half and what's left isn't contempt.

The list has a small source but no end. Everything on it, and anything that could be added, follows from one sentence read by two people who both know what a turned-away look is for: *I know what I'm doing to you; I won't admit it, even to myself; and you can see all of this.* Whatever we can work out from that is in the look, and so is the fact that we can work it out. So no list of what the look carries can close.

It doesn't have to, because the look is about itself. What it delivers is the look: it hurts him by making him know that it's meant to hurt him, and that it's deniable, to him and to me. A mathematician would call that a fixed point. Spell it out and you get the endless list, each level the same act again, one step up. This one is unusual. A message that refers to itself usually still carries something: *I'm warning you that I'm warning you* has a warning inside. This one has nothing inside but the knowing it delivers. Its force comes from what it stands on: the three points, common knowledge between us, held out of view, and the fact of my holding them. Take those away and it couldn't hurt anyone.

To hide the turn from myself, I have to hide that I'm hiding it, and that shows too, and so on up. "I'm hiding it" and "I'm hiding that I'm hiding it" are different sentences, but there's only one act under all of them, the turn, under one more description each time. No level calls for a new act. That's why I never have to climb the levels, and why the whole of it fits in one look.

The trap depends on one consequence of that: there's nothing to quote. A secret whose only content is the hiding-and-showing of it leaves no evidence except the showing, and the showing is evidence of a particular kind: gaze, tone, timing, recognized by both of us and conclusive to neither. He has everything he needs to know and nothing that settles the dispute, and that's what makes the injury unanswerable.

Now see how tight this is. The look needs him to face what I won’t, while I go on using that knowledge to aim. But what it carries is common knowledge, and common knowledge comes in one piece: if he knows, I know he knows, so I know. The look is an attempt to split something that can’t be split. All I can withhold is the acknowledgment, from the record and from myself: knowing, and not letting myself know, which is limbo, which is the turn itself. And even that is inside what we share: the third point says a turn goes into limbo and shows, so he watches me go there, and I know he does. That’s why my denial is never a lie he might catch. It’s a lie we’re both standing inside, and neither of us can name it to any effect, because his naming it becomes my proof. That’s where the maddening hypocrisy comes from: I’m denying something we both know, and know the other knows, all the way up.

### Take one away

Given a look aimed to be read and dressed as righteous, take each of the three away and see what goes with it.

Without the first, there's nothing shared to betray. A being with nothing to turn from could see that I'm a fraud, but he wouldn't feel it as a betrayal, and the betrayal is what the look needs. The verdict says he's a different kind of thing. The look only works on someone who isn't.

Without the second, the turn wouldn't be the harm. Being put out of view might still sting, the way being ignored does, but the harm would lie somewhere else, and I could do it with my eyes open, holding nothing of his out of view.

Without the third, it fails three ways. Fully unaware, nothing aims, and he reads a slip. Unseen, it's private self-deceit, and he's untouched. Fully aware of my intent, either I'm looking at him, and I can't go through with it, or it's malice, owned. All malice hides one thing from the one doing it: the sameness of the target. This is true even in the sadist. But malice as such needs neither of two things contempt needs. It need not be received (the target can be asleep while the deed is done), and where it does demand reception (a threat, a sadist's display) it needn't disavow itself as righteous; the honest villain says *yes, I mean you harm, and no, you're not my kind.* Aimed contempt needs both. The target must receive the harmful intention, and the sender must deny that it's harmful and believe the denial, in the limbo way. So contempt holds two things in limbo where malice holds one: his sameness, which every harm hides, and my own intent. The trap needs the two together. It's why malice is frightening and contempt is maddening.

So the knowledge has to be present and operating and refused, all at once. That tells you something about the faculty. It's the third point again, reached this time from what contempt does: if the faculty could be switched off, nobody would go to the trouble of turning from it. It can only be turned from, which is why the one move available against it is not looking. However far I turn, nothing goes out of sight; I just face away from it. I've never met anyone who was surprised by a wrong they'd hidden when they finally saw it; surprised at its size, often. The knowledge was there the whole time, which is what the limbo recall was reporting.

Honest malice spreads too, but in the open, where it can be seen for what it is and fought as what it is. Contempt is evil's way of spreading in secret, through people who sincerely believe they're fighting it, and that is what makes it uniquely insidious.

### What the need was looking for

Part of me needs to be a different kind of thing from the people I despise. Nothing true can satisfy it, since there is no such difference. So if it's going to be satisfied, something false will have to be made to look true, and contempt is the instrument built for the job. Look at how well it fits.

It manufactures the evidence. The look pushes him further from his better nature, and how far he turns depends on what's done to him, the way it does for me. His hardening is the difference I needed to see, produced on demand. And I can't see my part in producing it. Nothing is left to quote, for him or for me, so his reaction arrives looking like his nature. The bad essence I perceive is my own act, unseen as mine.

And it reproduces. The reaction it provokes isn't only evidence: it's him turning away in turn, and carrying it to whoever he meets next. The injury is his seeing it; the deeper harm is what it licenses him to become, and that's how the turn spreads. The license needs my hypocrisy, which is my turn seen from his side. Turned toward, I can be as harsh as I like; it may hurt, but it hands him no license. The license travels only through the turn.

What I wanted was to hurt him and get away with it, with nothing he could quote. What spreads the turn is a look he receives, and a denial he can see through, which is what hands him the license. Those are the same act. What makes the hurt deniable to me is what makes it hypocrisy to him. Nothing had to plan the spread. My wanting did the aiming, and the spread came with it. When I finally saw all of it, I found I'd known that part too.

Do this enough times, with enough people, and the world fills with the kind of person the need requires. Then the need looks like perception. I'm not making anyone bad; look at them.

### What the look needed

Contempt's explicit message denies equality. Its successful operation presupposes it.

The verdict says *this is all you are*: no better nature, nothing shared, nothing left to reach, nothing to turn back. Now count what the look needed:

- He had to read the move as I would have.
- What he felt had to be hypocrisy, which needs a standard we share and my departure from it.
- The standard was care and the departure the turn, by the first two points.
- The care in him had to be there, obscured and not gone, or the look would have nothing to land on and nothing to corrupt.
- He had to be able to turn back. Hypocrisy needs a standard I could meet, so we both know I could turn back to him now, and what's true of me is true of him.

The first two come from the encounter alone; the rest lean on what you checked in your own case. On this picture, the capacity that recognizes the violation and the capacity to care are one faculty: the encounter shows the first, your own case the second.

Every one of those is the opposite of the verdict, and every one rests on what was common knowledge between us before the encounter began. The encounter taught neither of us any of it, and saying it aloud wouldn't either. It would only put it on the record, and the verdict can't survive being heard. Contempt can tell someone *this is all you are* only by addressing the capacity in him that the verdict denies exists.

Put generally, the five conditions are these:

- What's looking out of him is no different in nature from what's looking out of me.
- Care is the baseline, what I'm like when nothing is in the way.
- The confusion covering it is not the same thing as it, and doesn't touch it.
- The covering is a turning away, not a substance.
- Recognition is available, and is the way back.

Two things in that list go further than the look needed. One is that the covering never touches it: the look needed the care in him to be there, obscured and not gone, not untouched. The other is the first, said as the traditions say it, which means not only that his better nature points where mine does, which hypocrisy requires, but that there's nothing to tell the two apart. Anyone who practices in a tradition that teaches a nature of mind will have heard all five before, as the view. Here they arrive as the conditions my own contempt was counting on, in the weaker form the look needs, in the one act that denies them loudest. I didn't have to believe them. I had to have been using them, and I was.

### Why it had to be this way

Everything above points to evil being an absence: every piece of the trap runs on the very thing it turns from, and as far as I can tell, only an absence works that way.

For him to read my turned look as meaning harm, and my claim to be in the right as a lie, we both have to know that harm needs the turn and decency never does. If turning away could be decent too, the look would be ambiguous, and there'd be no hypocrisy to see. That asymmetry is what it means for care to be the baseline: what needs no turn and no story is where I am when I'm not doing anything, and turning away is a departure from it, not a second baseline of its own. You checked the asymmetry when your wrong needed a story and your decency didn't, and the rest of the evidence points the same way: when a grudge finally drops, it feels like setting something down, not like starting something new.

And the three are enough for the act. Given them, and a look aimed to be read and dressed as righteous, harm done knowingly between two such beings has the shape listed above, each piece resting on the one before, and I haven't found another. The proof and the license are what it hands him; what he does with them is his.

Not one of those is a separate fact. Each is the turn, seen from a different position: mine, his, an onlooker's, the next day's. That's what I mean by the simplest explanation: not the shortest, but the one where one fact predicts the rest. And the turn is an absence, the facing gone missing. An absence can act only through the presence it's the absence of: it borrows the eyes and the reasons, it has to hide from what would stop it, and its hiding shows to whoever shares what it's hiding from. That is what "an absence that behaves like a presence" means.

Evil, in this book, names the turning: your own caring held out of view so that someone else can be. Contempt is that turning made into a message and dressed as righteous. The word *evil* usually carries two more things, and both are wrong. The first is size: think of the heat when someone cuts in line, and how shameful even a small flash of contempt is to admit, out of all proportion to its size. The second is outcomes: think of the forgotten birthday against the cutting remark, and of this: desecrating an enemy's corpse does the dead man no damage, and everyone knows it's wrong (Appendix 2 comes back to this). The worse outcome can come from the lesser wrong. What I find I'm tracking, in every one of those, is the turning, and I was tracking it long before I had a name for it.

Where this stops. The argument gets you this far: the care is present, it's obscured rather than gone, it can be turned back to, and it's the baseline that turning away departs from. It does not get you that care is what awareness *is*. The nearest rival reading is a universal human faculty with an evolutionary history, which nothing outweighs while it's in view. It differs from mine only about origin, and about whether the care belongs to awareness as such or only to minds like ours, which is a question no human experiment touches. What decides between those isn't the argument. It's what you find under your own hiding when the hiding stops.[^godel] Appendix 2 lays out the correspondences, the evidence for my reading, and the test that would count against it.

### What I think is really happening

*From here on I'm telling you what I believe. Nothing practical depends on walking through this door.*

I didn't reason my way to any of this. I saw it first, years ago, in an altered state, and since then in moments like the ones in this book. The argument above is what I've built afterward, trying to describe what I saw in terms you could check. It surely has holes. If you find them, they're holes in my description. What I'd ask you to test is the seeing, in your own case.

Here's my read. The care isn't something awareness has. It's what awareness is. If that's right, then evil is just awareness turned away from its own nature, and the only way to do it to someone else is to forget it in yourself first. Limbo, turning away, the absence that behaves like a presence: three names for that one move, depending on where you're standing.

It also explains why the knowledge is common. Nothing tells what's looking out of you apart from what's looking out of them. Not that there's one awareness. Only that there's nothing to tell them apart, which is all the mutual recognition needs. To know this in yourself is already to know it in them, and to know they know it in you, and so on.

So nothing in the encounter belongs to a separate thing called the darkness. Everything doing anything, the sending, the hiding, the reading, the answering, is the Light: one faculty, in two bodies, turned against itself in one of them. The message that denies the sameness is made of the sameness, and nothing else could receive it.

And that's what waking up is, on this reading. Nothing gets added. What the two of you were already counting on, silently, becomes something one of you can say. Contempt is the act that keeps it from being said while both of you go on knowing it.

The rest of what I believe is a story, and I hold it the way the traditions hold theirs: a first turning with no motive behind it and no one at fault, from the Aspiration of Samantabhadra, the Dzogchen prayer; then the fear that it was real, the guilt put outward onto others, and forgiveness as the way back, from *A Course in Miracles* as Kenneth Wapnick reads it. I got there by a different road from both; Appendix 2 says where the roads meet.

## 2. For the lawyers among you

*Everything above, sorted into what you observed, what the trap needs, the rivals, and what isn't claimed.*

**What you checked, and one to run.** 
- O1: with someone fully in view, you couldn't bring yourself to use them; harm requires holding the person out of view. And the other way round: when I hold someone out of view, it's so that I can do something their being in view would stop.[^converse]
- O2: the message tracked its target's exits and got stronger when pointed at (*you're just projecting*), and the flash behind it had to be aimed, land, and go unrecorded, none of it planned, though holding someone out of view takes effort. 
- O3: on the receiving end, what lands is hypocrisy, more than threat; the sender appeals to your capacity to recognize the violation while treating you as the kind of thing that has none, and from the sending end you count on exactly that. 
- O4, to run when you can: hold a stranger's eyes until the face becomes a place somebody is looking out of; the wanting that they be all right arrives with the looking: seeing carries care. 
- O5: in the limbo recall the knowledge wasn't new, and the person who caught you without a verdict reminded you of something rather than told you something: turning away obscures the care and doesn't remove it. 
 
Each is a claim about experience, open to a reader who looked and found otherwise. They aren't all of one kind: O1, O2, O3 and O5 are recalled, O4 is run when you can, and the second stranger in O5 is imagined by anyone who hasn't met one. The recalled ones carry the most weight, and the argument leans on them. Nothing is assumed beyond the three points and these five.

**Necessary and sufficient.** Take the trap as Section 1 builds it: a look that is aimed, meant to be read, and carries the verdict *you're not my kind*, sent to someone who can see through it. Two conditions make it contempt rather than malice: the three points, and the sameness they give, are common knowledge between the two people (call it C), and the sender disowns the harm as righteous and believes it (call it H). The wound and the hypocrisy need C, and so does reading it as intended: the target knows the sender meant him to see it, and the sender knows he knows, and those first few levels close the exits. The sincere denial needs H, and so does the protest turned into proof, which can renew the verdict for years. The license needs both. And H can be held only in limbo: given C, the sender can believe the denial only by not letting himself know what both of them know. Drop the turn, and the verdict with it, and keep C, and you get the second stranger: fierce, with no verdict for the target to dig in against. Keep C and the verdict but own the harm, and you get malice: it can frighten or degrade but can't read as hypocrisy. Given C, H and the verdict, nothing else is needed for the act.

**The rivals.** Say evil is a nature: a bad essence some people have, that wants harm the way hunger wants food. Its best evidence is the perception itself, *now I see what they really are*. Section 1 gives that perception another source: the essence I perceive is my own act, unseen as mine. And it shows where that source says it should: the gaze that sees a bad essence looks, from outside, like a turned-away face. That's a hint, not a proof. The nature account also allows harm done with the eyes open, and a look that injures without the target's better nature. Give that nature a standard of its own, and remorse would sometimes run backward too: someone, somewhere, would feel the sickening sense of having gone against himself over an act of decency. Regret over what a decency cost, I've found, and so have you. That sense of having betrayed myself, I haven't, and I'd want to hear of a conscience that runs the other way. None of those happens, in any case I've found. Each is something the nature account has to arrange separately not to happen, and something the absence account can't produce in the first place.

The stronger rival is a will that keeps conscience intact and silences it with self-deceit: Kant's radical evil, or the need for justification that dissonance theory, in Aronson's version, predicts whenever an act conflicts with the self-image. Those predict the hiding and the one-way remorse as well as this account does. What they don't predict is the trap: that the harm has to be received, has to read as hypocrisy, and lands nowhere but on the capacity the verdict denies. A propensity of the will would have no reason to need its target's better nature. Contempt can't work without it. Those accounts can take the trap on as an addition. Here, given only what contempt is, a look aimed to be read and dressed as righteous, it falls out of the same three points that give the hiding. Kant comes closest: he holds that even a vicious man can't be denied all respect, because the predisposition to good can't be lost in him. That's a rule about what you mustn't do. The claim here is that contempt can't do otherwise, and was counting on it all along.

Past the point the argument reaches, two readings remain: the care could be missing in some people, or it could be a human faculty with an evolutionary history. "Missing in some" is the one I can't rule out, and here is why I doubt it. The con man's warmth is the most reported case there is, and it's performed; what I've never found reported is the recognition. Try to picture one doing what they do while seeing the person in front of them, the way the second stranger saw you. I can't, and I'd want to hear of the case. If the care were simply missing in someone, harm from that person would need no turning away, and would look like nothing in particular. I've never seen it look like nothing in particular. The second is the naturalist's, and the observation narrows it: a drive can be outweighed into harm while you're still looking, and this one can't be; you have to stop looking first, and it takes work to keep from looking. What's left is the naturalist reading "Where this stops" leaves standing, with the one constraint the observation adds: whatever wins has to get the care out of view first.

**What is not claimed.** Not that every target sees the hypocrisy. One who can't believes the verdict instead, and what he feels is shame: harm still, resting on the same shared standard, but without the recursion this page is about. Not that turning away is sufficient for harm; it also produces numbness and paralysis with nobody harmed. Not contempt for the world; the argument is about contempt aimed at someone, hot or cold, since the cold form is meant to be felt too, and the general rule, that harm needs the turn, rests on O1. Not universality from this page; O1 to O5 are stated for everyone and open to counterexample, and the argument needs only your own instance and the first point. Not that every target can be reached in practice; in principle the faculty is there to be turned toward, and for some it may not happen in a lifetime.

## Where else to look

**On common knowledge.** Lewis, _Convention_ (1969); Aumann (1976), for the definition. Chwe, _Rational Ritual_ (2001), for the public event that usually produces it. Barwise, "Three Views of Common Knowledge" (1988), for common knowledge itself as a fixed point, and for the shared-situation view that the footnote's silent public event resembles. Fagin & Halpern (1988) on implicit versus explicit knowledge, for knowing something without having it available for use; limbo holds that gap open on purpose. The footnote on common knowledge is the contrast.

**On what it is.** Bateson et al. (1956) on the double bind: a demand, a contradicting demand at another level, and no way to comment on the contradiction. Pinker, Nowak & Lee (2008), "The logic of indirect speech," on content conveyed _so as not to_ become common knowledge. The trap is an innuendo whose subject is itself, with one difference: the sender makes the uptake visible, so it does become common knowledge between the two, and stays unacknowledged. Goffman on face-work, for the auditable surface the act hides behind. Manne, _Down Girl_ (2017), and Bloom, "The Root of All Cruelty?" (2017), on cruelty and degradation as recognizing the target's humanity rather than denying it; the difference here is what's recognized, the capacity to see the violation, and the sender's limbo about it.

**On limbo.** Butler, "Upon Self-Deceit" (1726). Fingarette, _Self-Deception_ (1969): self-deception as declining to _spell out_ what you're doing. Sartre's bad faith; Mele as the deflationary rival. Kunda (1990) on motivated reasoning: the search stops at the wanted conclusion. Thibodeau & Aronson (1992) for dissonance as a conflict with the self-concept.

**On the leak being functional.** Trivers; von Hippel & Trivers (2011); Kurzban (2010). Adjacent, with one inversion: here what looks like a leak is the payload.

**On contempt, measured.** Gottman, for contempt as the strongest single predictor of divorce in his couples data, and for its description: superiority and disgust, delivered without words.

**On the just gaze.** Murdoch, _The Sovereignty of Good_ (1970): the mother-in-law who re-sees her daughter-in-law.

**On the two strangers, measured.** Tangney & Dearing, _Shame and Guilt_ (2002): "I did a bad thing" repairs, "I am bad" hides and attacks. Braithwaite, _Crime, Shame and Reintegration_ (1989): stigmatizing versus reintegrative shaming, with outcome data. Gilligan, _Violence_ (1996): shame as the universal precursor to violence.

**On turning away as a perceptual event.** Harris & Fiske (2006): social-perception regions go quiet for dehumanized groups. Gray & Wegner on moral typecasting: seen as agent, unseen as sufferer. Bandura on moral disengagement, which catalogs limbo's toolkit.

**On the arrangement.** Girard on the scapegoat. Baumeister, _Evil_ (1997), for the exterior view of the license, the terminus, and the myth of pure evil. Browning, _Ordinary Men_ (1992).

**On the shadow.** Jung.

**On privation.** Augustine, _Enchiridion_.

**On the story in "What I think is really happening."** Wapnick, _A Vast Illusion_ (1990) and _The Message of A Course in Miracles_ (1997), for the reading of the Course in which the ego is the belief in separation and its one strategy is to keep the guilt out there. Appendix 2 has the correspondences.

[^converse]: Holding him out of view and permitting myself the act are one event seen from two sides.

[^ck]: Game theorists call something common knowledge when everyone knows it, everyone knows that everyone knows it, and so on without end. Nobody climbs that tower. Lewis's version asks only that each person have reason to believe at every level, and a shared basis to reason from. Usually that basis is a public event: a trusted party says the thing aloud where everyone can see everyone hear it. The child in *The Emperor's New Clothes* adds no information; everyone already knew. He makes it common knowledge. The term has two uses on this page, and they're different. The first needs no encounter: that there's no essential difference between you, no better nature missing in them, and no extra thing in them, one you lack, that turns them from it. It's common knowledge the way a convention is: nobody was told it, and everyone has been on both ends. The rules the page leans on, that an aimed turn is for harm and that hiding-while-revealing is the signature of harm dressed as righteousness, are known the same way. The second is made in the encounter: that you're turned away from them now, on purpose. The encounter supplies its own public event, a silent one: the aimed look, its familiar meaning, the look landing, and each of you seeing the other see it land. It can be silent because turning away shows. Unsaid is not the same as not common knowledge. What's missing, for both, is acknowledgment. Nothing is on the record, so nothing can be quoted to anyone else, or to yourself. Neither use reaches the reading under "What I think is really happening."

[^godel]: A note on Gödel, since the comparison will occur to some readers. It's an analogy, not an argument, and the analogy is to the second theorem. A formal system strong enough to matter can't certify its own consistency using only its own resources; a stronger system can assess it from outside, and then the question moves to the stronger one. The lesson contempt teaches, demote the part that knows and be only the part that proves, asks the reasoning part to vouch for itself in the same way, and the reasoning that justifies an act can't show, by producing more justification, that it hasn't been recruited. That's the book's claim that you can't calculate your way to goodness, in Gödel's shape. What the theorem doesn't supply is that the outside faculty is sound. The analogue, that the part that knows is right if it isn't itself turned away, is a problem the book carries rather than a result it has.
