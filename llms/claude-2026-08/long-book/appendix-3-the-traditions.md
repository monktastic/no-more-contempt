# Appendix 3: What the Traditions Named

*Appendix 2 was about the thinkers who saw the mechanism. This is about the traditions that named the thing. Nothing in the book depends on it. I'm including it because some of my early readers practice in these traditions, because the names they chose turn out to carry the book's central paradox, and because I want to be clear about which parts of this book are borrowed and which are mine.*

## Dzogchen and Mahamudra

Anyone who has done Dzogchen or Mahamudra practice will have recognized the architecture several sections ago. I want to name the correspondences, since it costs nothing and since pretending to have invented them would be its own small act of hiding.

What I've been calling the turning-away is *marigpa*, non-recognition: not a thing and not an act, just the nature of mind failing to recognize itself. What I've called the parasite having no substance of its own is the standard position that the afflictions, anger, craving, and the rest, arise as the mind's own display and have nothing of their own. What I've called "it isn't what you are" is the teaching that the obscurations are *adventitious*: they cover the nature without ever touching it, the way clouds cover the sky. And what I've called "you can't decide to look, but you can train how fast you catch it" is what the tradition calls non-fabrication and familiarization: the recognition can't be manufactured by effort, and the path is recognizing again and again, not repairing anything.

Two of these are more than parallels.

The first is that this tradition already answers the question I've been circling: how does a turned mind turn itself? It doesn't. Recognition isn't produced by thinking; it's introduced, by someone who is in it, in a way that isn't an argument. That's called pointing-out instruction. It has the same shape as what the second person who caught you did, and I don't mean the two are the same thing; one is a specialized transmission and the other was an ordinary moment between two people. He didn't put anything into you and he didn't win a debate. He made recognition possible and it happened by itself. If that move seemed too slight to be the whole answer, there's a twelve-hundred-year-old body of technique built on the claim that it's the only thing that works.

The second is the Aspiration Prayer of Samantabhadra, which answers the question I said I couldn't answer: why the first turning away happens at all. One ground; from it, appearances arise; Samantabhadra recognizes them as his own display and is free in that instant; beings don't recognize, and wander. Same ground, same arising. And the not-recognizing is not a rebellion or a sin or a choice. It's described as a momentary blankness, with no motive given—and the sense of self and other arises *after* it, not before. There was no one there yet to want anything. That is the answer to the regress, the one where every explanation of the first turning away seems to need an evil chooser behind it: the first turning-away doesn't need a cause, because it was never an act.

This sits differently from the fear account elsewhere in this book, where the turning begins with the fear of having left home. I hold both, in this order: the blank comes first, and the fear is what the blank feels like once there's someone there to feel it. Projection—making the other one guilty—is the first thing that someone does. On that reading the fear isn't the cause of the first not-seeing; it's the first content of the self that not-seeing produced. If that's wrong, the mistake is mine and not the traditions'.

## The wider Mahayana, and the ego

Before those, the practices, because the tradition doesn't only describe this; it treats it. Lojong is built for the person you can't stand: drive all blames into one; be grateful to everyone, the enemy as the rarest condition for practice; and tonglen, taking in their darkness on the in-breath and sending them what you'd want for yourself on the out-breath, which works on the self-cherishing the verdict comes from. Shantideva's patience chapter says the enemy is more precious than a friend for exactly this reason.

Three more, and these are the ones Appendix 1 ends up resting on, so I'll say them plainly. The first: an affliction, looked at directly, turns out to be made of the very nature it was hiding. Mahamudra says it as "the nature of thought is dharmakaya"; Dzogchen says delusion is the energy of awareness, not recognized as such, and that samsara and nirvana have one ground. Appendix 1 gets to the same place from the street: the message that denies our sameness is made of that sameness and can be read by nothing else. The second: the Uttaratantra's teaching that the nature is present and untouched even in the most obscured being. That's what Appendix 1 means when it says the care is still there, covered rather than gone, and able to turn back, and it's what the hostage at the window in Chapter 3 needs to be true. The third is Shantideva's, in his chapter on patience: being angry at the person is like being angry at the stick instead of the hand that swings it; the affliction is the enemy, not the being it's using. That's the book's "go after the absence and leave the person standing," and it's why the thing can't be fought as a thing: contempt for the contemptuous is the same move, made by the one fighting. The Dhammapada says the whole of it in a line: hatred is never ended by hatred.

One more correspondence, and I haven't finished working it out, so hold it loosely. What I've called the parasite is not "ego" in the everyday sense, a big personality or a bad attitude. It's closer to what the Mahayana calls self-cherishing: the mind that counts itself as the one exception, which Shantideva names as the source of every harm and which the mind-training texts tell you to treat as the single enemy, while denying in the same breath that it is a thing. That is the book's paradox exactly, a nothing that has to be fought like a something. The exception is the ego. The parasite is the ego defending the exception, and self-righteous contempt is the defense at full volume, which is why the word has "self" in it. Every tradition that looked at this noticed the same thing about where it hides: in the last place you'd ever look, behind your own eyes, convincing you it isn't there. The Christians gave that trick to Satan. The Buddhists gave it to the self. I think they were describing one thing.

The clearest modern statement of it I know doesn't come from a monastery. Angelo DiLullo, a contemporary teacher in the nondual line: 

> The ego doesnʼt tell you what it thinks. It doesnʼt tell you what it wants you to think. In fact, it doesnʼt even tell you what you think. Ego does the thinking for you. Take a moment to let that sink in. The ego convinces you that its view is your view. It does this by speaking as if you are the one speaking. It structures thoughts in such a way that it sounds and feels like they are your voice, your thoughts. Because itʼs happening inside your mind, you immediately assume that the egoʼs voice and its view are your voice and your view.

That is the parasite exactly as this book met it. It speaks in your voice, so its view arrives as your view, and you never notice a second speaker because there was only ever one voice in the room. DiLullo points to a scene in the film *Revolver* for the same idea: the enemy behind everything, hiding in the last place you'd ever look, behind your own eyes. Put that beside Eckhart below and the two traditions are saying one sentence.

## Christianity: privation, and the devil's one trick

The Christian tradition names the same thing from the other side. Augustine's evil is a privation, an absence with no substance of its own, and the *Enchiridion* is the earliest statement I know of that same paradox: nothing to point at, and everything to fight. Eckhart is sharper: 

> *The devil is none other than the will turned away from God. When the will turns from the One, it becomes its own devil.*

That is the turning-away, named as Satan seven centuries ago, and the absence is kept intact: there's no creature in it, only a direction. And the trick every tradition gives the devil, convincing you he doesn't exist, is in this book the parasite's whole method, hiding behind your own eyes in the last place you'd look. The line I opened the book with, from the Gospel of Philip, says what to do about it and says it's enough. I should be honest about one difference. Augustine keeps the will and the guilt where I keep innocence and the catching of yourself. The privation is shared; the account of responsibility isn't.

## A Course in Miracles

*A Course in Miracles*, in Kenneth Wapnick's reading, is the nearest thing to the fear account in this book: the ego is nothing but the belief in separation, and its one strategy is to keep the guilt out there. I got there by a different road. That's where the roads meet.

## What's mine, and a caution

Where I go beyond what all of these say, and you should hold it as mine: none of them claim that non-recognition coordinates across minds. That's the whole middle of this book, and it isn't in them.

One more thing is mine, though the conclusion isn't. No single tradition here states the whole of it. Between them they hold the pieces: a nature that is there before anything is acquired; ignorance as not-recognizing rather than corruption; recognition as the way back; and, in the Mahayana line, care as part of that nature. Each got to its piece by practice or revelation. This book gets there by a different road: taking a bad act apart. You can't harm somebody while looking at them, so the harm needs a hiding, and nobody builds machinery that elaborate to keep something from a part of themselves that doesn't care. The size of the hiding measures the care. The road starts from your worst moment rather than your best; Mencius's child at the well is the nearest thing I know, and it runs on the good moment. What I add is the psychology, two places to look that nobody had pointed at as places, limbo and contempt, and the fact that you can run it on yourself in an afternoon.

I should be careful about how much that convergence proves. The evidence is independent: the gaze experiment and the anatomy of contempt borrow nothing from any tradition, though the words I reach for at the end, care and recognition and turning toward, are theirs. The method isn't independent: both rest on disciplined looking from the inside, so they could share a blind spot. And my road doesn't reach all the way to the destination on its own. It shows that something is there that has to be hidden for harm to happen. It doesn't, by itself, show that the something is care rather than, say, a fear of being caught, or a flinch we evolved. So here is what favors care, and what would count against it.

What favors it. First, conscience fires when nobody is harmed and nobody is watching. You cheated on a test and knew it was wrong with no victim in front of you; soldiers asked whether it's wrong to desecrate an enemy's corpse say yes at once and can't tell you who's hurt. A flinch built to keep us from harming each other has no reason to fire there. Something that recognizes the other person's standing does. Second, it isn't cultural. You recognized the second stranger across every difference you could name, and what he was standing up for wasn't his. Third, the traditions converge on the same shape from different roads, which is weak evidence by itself and stronger when the shape has this many parts. And fourth, the hardest case doesn't point the other way. In psychopaths, where the care looks absent, what's been found is that the response is weak when spontaneous and present when asked for. That shows they can take another's perspective; it doesn't show anything in them minds. So it can't prove the care is there and merely covered. What it rules out is the crudest version of "missing entirely."

What would count against it. Someone doing harm to a person while fully seeing them. That's the sadist test, and it's the same test as Appendix 1's, because if care is part of what awareness is, it can't coexist with full seeing, while if it's one drive among others, it could be outweighed with nothing turned away. "Fully seeing" has to mean something you can check without knowing whether harm followed, or the test is empty, so here are three marks for it: their objection would land on you, and you'd have to answer it rather than manage it; their hurt would register as bad, the way yours does; and what's in them could change what you do next. A person who passes all three and wants the suffering anyway is the case. It's a test you run on what you've seen, not one a lab can run, and I'm calling it that. I've never met the case. If you have, it counts.

And one caution, mostly for me. The easiest way to misread non-fabrication is as *nothing to do*. That reading is available in every one of these correspondences, and it produces the other way of failing the book's paradox, treating the thing as harmless because it's an absence, which makes you complicit, in a form that feels like attainment. Whatever else this book is, it isn't permission.
