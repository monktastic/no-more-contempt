# Chapter 4: The Darkness

Before we recognize evil as a turning away, we can't help but feel contempt for "evil people." But even after we recognize it for what it is, the question remains: why do they turn away in the first place?

If we again ascribe a *bad essence*—that some people are just the kind of people who *would*, whereas we're the kind who wouldn't—then we end up in contempt again, just pushed one layer down.

Our mind keeps reaching for a reason the contempt would be right, and a bad essence is the reason it keeps finding. Here is why that reason can't hold. Evil is the *result* of turning away, not its cause. And the turning can't have been chosen, in the sense we usually mean. Look at what that would require. If harm can only be done from the turned-away state, then choosing to turn away would be a wrong done while still looking, which is the one thing we just saw can't happen. Try it: hold someone's eyes and decide, while holding them, to stop seeing them. See if you can find the moment. I can't. The view goes, then the reasons arrive, and then it feels like wanting.

There is one choice, and it's real. While you can still feel both pulls—the recognition, and the want the recognition would veto—you're still looking, and you can keep looking or stop. That's the choice. After it, there's no chooser in the middle, only reasons, and they feel like your own judgment. And what you chose wasn't described to you as *stop seeing him*. It came labeled *I've had a long day*, or *he had it coming*. The want was innocent; the label was borrowed. That's why, looking back, it feels like you chose it knowing better, and you did, and that isn't shame's shape, *I am it*. It's remorse's, *I did it*.

---

Look at what it did in the first two chapters. It aimed the verdict, anticipated their reaction, and prepared to use it. It hid from you while showing itself to them, and it got stronger, not weaker, when you came close to catching it. It pursued a goal you would never choose, a darker world, and while it had you it felt entirely like you. What do we call a thing that lives off a host, hides from it, works against what the host wants, and defends itself when exposed? 

A parasite. 

I'll call it that from here, because the word fits the behavior, and for no other reason. If the word makes you picture a creature, drop the picture and keep the behavior: a groove in attention so old that its shape looks like intent. A habit that lives by going unseen will push back when seen. That's all a habit is, and it doesn't need a tenant. 

This picture has real drawbacks, and you should know them. A name for an affliction easily turns into a character, and then the character has to be fought, and fighting something in your own mind is mostly a way of not looking at it. It also hands people a new thing to call each other: "that's your parasite talking" is contempt in a fresh coat. And it can be used as an excuse. I keep it anyway, for a reason Chapter 5 spells out: the two alternatives, that the darkness simply *is* the person, or that it's nothing much, both do worse. If a different picture works better for you, use yours.

In the Mahayana's terms it isn't "ego" in the everyday sense, a big personality. It's closer to self-cherishing: the mind that counts itself as the one exception. The exception is the ego. The parasite is the ego defending it, and contempt is the defense at full volume.

When you send that message to the "bad person," his gets stronger and so does yours, each a little better hidden from its host. Watch any two sides go to war: both grow, and every side comes out more certain the problem lives in the other.

This is not the claim that the two sides are the same, or equally right, or equally dangerous. They usually aren't, and you may well be on the side that's right. The claim is only about what goes out through the eyes. That part is the same on both sides, and it's the one part of your fight that feeds what you're fighting.

Which also says what you're feeling when you feel contempt. Not the sign that you've spotted evil, though you have. A tug, to be recruited into the thing you're looking at, and it uses your accurate perception of it as the hook.

---

I cannot give an account of why the first turn happens, but once it does, it becomes a self-perpetuating loop.

When we forget our true nature, something tragic happens: fearing loss of our *absolute goodness* (which we could never actually lose), we go out seeking it in the *relative*. That is, at the expense of others. At this point, some form of contempt is nearly inevitable.

Say it the other way around, because this is what was under the answer at the end of Chapter 2. You only ever turn away from someone because you suspect, below words, that care might *not* be what you are. If you knew it was, you'd have nothing to prove and nobody to prove it against. The suspicion is what makes goodness feel like something you have to earn, and you can only be good *relative* to someone worse. So as long as you don't feel at home, you can't afford to let anyone else get there. You're *forced* to keep them out, because if they arrived you'd lose the only proof you had. That's what the dark message was for.

We are all trying to find our way home, not realizing that this cannot be done at another's expense (by keeping them from it). The pattern is kept alive by the subtle fear that we don't deserve home, because we secretly fear what we've done, and thus what we must *be*. And what we've done, mostly, is keep others out. That closes the loop. The suspicion makes us turn away; turning away is the one thing that would confirm the suspicion, if we looked; so we don't look, and we go on needing bad people.

I've been writing as if contempt is only for the "bad people." It isn't, and most of mine isn't spent on them. It goes to people I've decided are stupid, or crazy, or weak, or slow: the coworker who doesn't get it the third time, the relative who believes something ridiculous. They've harmed nobody. The verdict is the same one, *this is all you are*, and the cover is better. With a wrongdoer I tell myself I'm fighting evil. Here I tell myself I'm only being accurate, and I may be: they may really be wrong, or slow. But being right about what someone can't do doesn't call for the eye-roll, or the sigh, or the need to be above them. Something in me is sensing who it's okay to look down on.

Here's a check. Think of someone who can't do a thing through no fault of theirs: a toddler who can't add, a grandparent losing their words. Is the contempt there? For me it isn't. It arrives once I've decided they *should* know better; that they could, and won't. So even this contempt seems to carry a moral charge underneath. I've found them guilty of being what they are.

And it leaks the same way. A person treated as stupid stops asking questions. A person treated as crazy stops trying to make themselves understood, and I take that as proof. (For a real-world example of this: I once caught myself enjoying snide comments directed at flat-Earthers. Then, later, I found contempt for people who make snide comments directed at flat-Earthers.)

Underneath the turning away is that suspicion. And underneath the suspicion is fear: fear of what you might be. Pride is the fear's public face, and shame is its last defense, the thing that says *you are it* the moment you glimpse your own darkness and sends you back into hiding. (The traditions put something with no motive before the fear; Appendix 3 says what.)

That's what makes it possible to stay turned toward someone at their worst: under the contempt there is someone afraid, and you know that state.

This is why the only solution is to recognize (and actualize) our innate goodness. Everything else just leads to more harm, even as we're flawlessly convinced we're *helping*. Trying to simply be a "good person" often recreates the idea of "bad people," ensuring the dynamic continues.

So all evil is innocent, in one exact sense. Not excusable, and not harmless. Innocent in that nobody stops seeing while they can still see, and everything they do afterward is done from where they've ended up. And everyone is fully responsible, because responsibility never lived in a lucid instant of choosing evil. It lives in how fast you catch the blindness, in what you repair when sight returns, and in what you've done to give the blindness less room next time.

---

None of that stops being true at the worst end. So bring the worst people back to mind, the ones from the start of Chapter 1, because the general case is easy to nod at and theirs is not.

Watch what your verdict does to them once it lands. They've just watched someone who counts as a good person turn away from them and call it righteousness. If their parasite could speak, this is what it would say to them:

> *Look how easily I can take over even the "good people." Genuine goodness is a myth—a name that "good people" use to cover what they really want.*

So their conscience looks a little more naive to them, and next time the excuses come a little faster. Nobody notices one step. The murderer, the abuser, the man in the news: each is this one step, taken thousands of times. You think your contempt is punishing what they became. It's one more push in the same direction.

(You may be wondering about the people who seem to be born this way, or who get there for no reason anyone can find. There are answers on offer, from genetics to karma, and none of them is needed here. However they got there, what you're looking at is the same turned-away state, and it reads the same from outside. It doesn't change the state they're in *now*, or how to treat them.)

Here is what's strange about being at the far end. The part of us that would notice we've drifted is the part we've drifted from, so the further gone we are, the less of it we can hear. That much is obvious. What isn't obvious is that the knowing doesn't shrink, and you can check this in your own case. Think of the smallest wrong you've had to admit to yourself, and the biggest. Which took longer? And was that because you knew the big one *less*? For me it was the reverse. I knew it more, and had to hide it correspondingly harder. Chapter 1 said limbo comes in degrees, and this is what the degrees are made of. It can look like the opposite, because people who do harm every day say they feel it less over time. But getting used to turning away isn't the same as turning away less. Think of the worker on a factory-farm line. The hiding has become so practiced that it no longer takes effort, which is what makes it complete. Now run that out to the far end. The most lost person is carrying *more* of that knowledge than you are, not less, and holding it down harder, with more of themselves committed to the holding. They know the most and the least at once.[^far]

Ask what you're looking at when you look at such a person and feel that particular horror. It isn't a hole where a person should be, whatever the verdict says. What produces that horror is something under pressure, pressing to get out: a conscience under crushing load, showing through. And the parasite holding it down is using your contempt to help. Every time your verdict arrives, it presses on the one part of them that could end this. You think you're looking at a monster showing you what it is. You're looking at a hostage, held up to a window. If you look at such a person and find only a monster, I can't argue you out of that from here. I can only say what I found.

And the hostage isn't struggling. From inside, the thing holding him doesn't present itself as a captor. It presents itself as his judgment, his seriousness about what's wrong with the world. The captor has become his best friend; that's Stockholm syndrome, and it's why the people who appear to want their darkness are the ones most fully taken. Being unkind to him is allying with his captor, because the urge to do it comes from yours.

That's why "they need the most love" isn't sentiment, and it doesn't mean anything soft. They're the ones it's most tempting to shove in harder, and the ones least able to get out, even though they're also the ones who see it most. What they need is what the second person gave you: someone fierce who won't add the verdict.

And the darkest idea this thing has ever produced is the one that sounds most like a solution: if we could round up the bad people and be rid of them, the rest of us would live in peace. The turning away it takes to hold that thought guarantees the darkness stays behind, in full force, changed only in shape. I can't find a hot atrocity that didn't begin there. The cold ones begin with nobody in view at all.

## A note for people who were hurt

If the person you're carrying is somebody who hurt you, or your child, or your people: your rage is right. Stopping them is right, and if stopping them takes force, the force is right. The fierceness comes from a good place, and nothing here asks you to feel less of it. But notice whether a second thing is riding along with it. Something that says: *I've been hurt, so I've earned the right to let a little of my own darkness out.* If it's there, look at it, and ask whether you want to recreate the thing that hurt you. Because that's what it makes. Accountability is what you owe them, all of it, with force if it takes force. The verdict is the one thing you don't owe them, and it's the one thing their parasite needs from you.

Look at who is relieved when you hold it. Not the person. The parasite in them, which needed you to see them that way, because your contempt is the evidence it runs on: *see, this world is a war, and the only thing that works is power.* Now you're doing what you did in your own case in Chapter 2, and it doesn't matter that you're right about what they did.

I want to say this part carefully, because if you were hurt, you have every reason to hear it as one more person telling you how to feel. You have earned things. Your rage, your refusal, your distance, and whatever it takes to make sure it never happens again: all of that is yours. Staying turned toward someone never means staying near them. If you're still living with the person who hurt you, or still within their reach, distance comes first. It is possible to leave, cut contact, and never see them again with no verdict in it at all. And nothing here demands that you turn toward them at all. That may come one day, or never, and there is no shame in being unable. 

The only thing to notice is this, and it's only an offer. That license, the one that says you've also earned a little darkness of your own: look at where it comes from. It doesn't come from the hurt. It comes from the same darkness that hurt you. That darkness wants you to take the license, because nothing proves its case better than a good person who was wronged and turned dark. Look at what it gets if you do: the person with the least reason to serve it, serving it. And the pull to do so is strongest here, because here you can see the darkness more clearly than anywhere else, and it uses that clear sight as the hook. You, of all people, deserve to know that.

And what actually frightens it is the other thing: fierce, unyielding refusal that carries no verdict. That isn't a nice idea from a book. It's among the most effective forces in the history of people facing power they couldn't match, and it works for the reason the second person worked on you: it gives the thing nothing to eat. It is also much harder than contempt, which is why contempt gets sold to you as the strong option.

And the most common contempt of all is the one you aim at yourself: *you are what you did.* It feels like accountability. It produces what contempt always produces, which is hiding. The way you hold your own darkness is the way you'll end up holding theirs, and it's true of you first.

---

When we gather with allies to foster contempt for our shared enemy, the same thing happens. Our parasites are in control. When we speak it into a crowd or on social media, our parasite is seeking others to resonate with.

Now imagine a locker room. Ten men, gathered around. One makes an off-color joke, testing the water. If nobody opposes it, a "shadow space" opens up, where all their parasites come out and play. Each man is a little bolder than they might have otherwise been. The moment the "wrong person" enters the room, the space shuts down. Nobody retains a record of what just happened—the parasites empowering themselves.

If someone had opposed it with contempt ("you're all evil!"), that too would have reinforced everyone's (including the person's who opposed it).

And most contempt isn't performed at all. It's consumed. Bring up a scene of somebody getting exactly what they had coming, delivered with relish. It feels good, and it wants more: the *fuck yeah* from the interlude. That's the parasite, watching. It's why this wrong, of all wrongs, spreads: it leaves no record, it arrives dressed as duty, it's rewarded in public, and it's pleasant to watch. Comment sections, the shared post, the outrage we pass around: each one negligible, and together they set how dark everyone assumes the world is. The background hum of the Devil, if you like the name.

Most of what we now call evil was once respectable. Plenty of it still gets a pass: greed gets praised as ambition, and indifference as realism. But contempt is the one we still praise as *goodness itself*, as what a good person is supposed to feel toward bad people. And that's why the others are so hard to stamp out: we fight every one of them with it.

Turning toward / away isn't binary, and most of it has no enemy in view at all. It's the water: the ordinary day, the small self-serving move with nobody in front of you and nothing you'd call a wrong. Every act of unkindness, every skirting of conscience, every self-serving behavior lives somewhere along the spectrum, and encourages more turning away in the world. Each such action carries it, the way mine did through nothing but a look.

This is what the draw in the interlude was measuring. Not whether you've done anything terrible, but how much contempt you're still capable of, toward anyone, for any reason. To the degree it's there, the road to the worst is open, and what's left is time and conditions. The murderer and the abuser didn't start there. They started where you are, and nothing about you guarantees you'd have turned out differently. That isn't said to frighten you. The road is open in both directions, and the way back is the same looking.

Hiding takes effort; it isn't our natural state. But the better someone gets at it, the less it looks like darkness. The smartest hide it behind agendas that look shiny from outside and are rotten inside, eating away at the world in ways that are hard to detect until it's too late.

Look behind any of the tragedies (toward people, animals, nature itself) and you will find turning away. Animals being abused in factory farms. Prison inmates subjected to dehumanizing conditions. Rainforests being cut down for profit. Each requires the same *turning away*. This is the executive from Chapter 2 again: nobody in view, and a wall that our contempt for him only thickens.

And it goes further than living things. We think of contempt as something we feel toward people. But simply by perceiving reality as a lifeless collection of stuff over which we hold dominion, we've already turned away from it, and every system of extraction and destruction on Earth rests on that. Here's a check that needs no metaphysics. Somebody cuts down a tree that has stood for a thousand years, for fun. Somebody takes a hammer to a stone arch that took ten thousand years to form, for the pleasure of watching it fall. Notice what you feel, and whether it arrives before any story about who was deprived. The tree has no conscience and the arch has no inside. Whatever you're registering, see whether it lands in the place where the violation of a person lands. Contempt for the world is the same act as contempt for a person, albeit in a subtler form.

So the question isn't whether you're in this. It's what to do from inside it.
