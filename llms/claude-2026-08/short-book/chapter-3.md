# Chapter 3: Common Ground

Chapter 2 held a hint: you couldn't have done it if you'd seen it clearly. That suggests something larger. Go back to the stranger's eyes for a moment. Imagine seeing yourself in them, the way you did at the start, and going ahead with harming or using them anyway, while still seeing them that way. Now imagine even the worst person doing their worst with what you'd recognize as genuine kindness in their eyes. See if either picture will hold. If not, then they had to turn away too.

If awareness were neutral between care and harm, why would harming someone require turning away from it? Why couldn’t cruelty proceed in full, undivided awareness? The simplest explanation is that care belongs to awareness itself. That care is what this book has been calling the light, and it's all the word will mean.

Something else points the same way. Turning away takes upkeep, at least until it's practiced enough to run by itself. A grudge has to be fed: the reasons rehearsed, the case kept open, their face kept out of view. Being turned toward someone takes nothing like that. And when a grudge finally drops, it doesn't feel like starting a new effort. It feels like setting something down. If turning away were the mind at rest, I'd expect it the other way around.

Familiar explanations for wrongdoing (motivated reasoning, a self-image to protect, the avoidance of shame) don't compete with this. Each presupposes that turning away: motivated reasoning is what the part that proves does once the view has already gone. And each inherits the blind spot. From inside, none of them can be seen operating. So darkness is not an equal and opposite force. It is simply what remains when awareness turns away from its own nature.

It also explains how such knowledge could be common—not merely shared, but something each of us can count on the other already knowing. If what's looking out of us has nothing to differ by, then to know this in yourself is already to know it in them, and to know that they know it in you, etc. Without that, the knowledge is only approximately common: close enough for the trap to work. With it, it's exact. Either way, it's required for the trap to function. Otherwise, there would be no violation to recognize, no hypocrisy to expose, no taunt to make land. **Darkness can communicate only by borrowing the light as common ground.**

You can check that from the receiving end, and it's subtle, so go slowly. Recall the last time someone's contempt really got under your skin, and feel for *where* it was trying to reach. Not your pride. An insult to your pride stings differently, and you can shrug it off. This reaches further in, for the *you* that knows you count: the same you that you would have to turn away from in order to harm someone, and the same you that a person who loves you is speaking to when they tell you something hard. Contempt and love are addressed to the same place. One says *I know you're in there.* The other says *there's nobody in there*, and they both say it to the somebody who is. That's what borrowing the light means. The message has only one place it can be delivered, which is the good in you, and some part of the sender must know it. Now turn it around and check that the same is true when you're the sender.

That's the claim at the center of this book, and the first two chapters already had you using it. Contempt works only if both of you already know that you're the same kind of thing, that turning away is the only way to harm, that they can read it in you, and that they could still turn back, since a verdict can only sting someone who could have. In brief, **wielding contempt proves we already know it's evil in miniature, and that its target is redeemable.**

It's why this book was originally titled *Contempt Is Evil*. Not as a judgement, and not about the magnitude of wrongdoing: evil, at the level of the person, is the turning away, and contempt is the turning away made into a message, which spreads it. And for the message to sting, they had to read it as the real thing, not as harshness. A person turned toward you can be far harsher, and it lands as a reminder of what you are, beneath all the confusion.

This is why awakening another person does not ultimately mean teaching them something new. It means helping them stop turning away from what they were already using—even in their darkness—to understand you. Words can point, but the deepest reminder travels through the same parallel channel: by remaining turned toward them, you embody openly what both of you already know. That's the look you started with, held when it's hardest to hold.

This gives the Buddhist account of ignorance an interpersonal form. Darkness has no independent substance; it is awareness failing to recognize its own nature. What I think I can add is an anatomy of how that ignorance passes between people—how it communicates through gaze and tone, recruits itself in another person, organizes groups, and leaves everyone convinced that they were fighting it.

And here's what makes it so hard: although the darkness is merely an absence, it *behaves* like an eerie presence. It aims, anticipates, deflects, and defends itself when exposed. That's no mystery: a turning away survives only while it goes unseen, so whatever comes close to exposing it has to be pushed off, and that takes aim. And it presents itself *as* the person, yet it cannot *be* them.

And it *benefits* from you confusing it with the person; believing that it *is* them, or that they want it. That is why I'm giving it a name of its own. Reifying it is a mistake, but treating it as a mere absence undersells the urgency and ferocity needed to defeat it. You are seeing something authentic. It just isn't what your own darkness tells you it is.

This is not a call to be nice. Harm still has to be stopped, sometimes fiercely. The question is whether we can oppose the darkness without identifying the person with it—because that identification is how the darkness passes between us. And the only way to do that is by remaining unflinchingly *turned toward* them.

## What I'm claiming

Here are the claims, stated flat. I believe all of them, and the full book tries much harder to show them. I'm not asking you to take them from me. For each one there's something you can check, and I'd like you to try to sense whether it's real.

**Contempt presupposes what it denies.** That's the claim above, and the one the rest hang from: to work at all, contempt has to count on the goodness and the redeemability it says aren't there.

**There is one kind of evil, and this is it.** Turned away, othering, evil: these are names for one thing, seen from three sides. Every decision made while the people it touches are held out of view belongs to it, and I haven't found a second kind. Check it against the cases you'd already use the word for. By *evil* I don't mean harm. Harm can be an accident. I mean the not-seeing that licenses it.

**It never stays confined to your skull.** It goes out through your eyes and your tone whether you meant to send it or not, and it lands, making it easier for the next person to do.

**Goodness is one thing too.** Being turned toward, seeing yourself in others, love, goodness: all one. And since what you turn toward in yourself is what looks out of everyone, turning toward yourself and turning toward everyone are the same act. It's only the whole thing when it includes everyone. Not because partial love is fake; it's the seed. But love for one person at the expense of another, love that needs someone else to be worthless, isn't love. It's the darkness borrowing love's equipment.

**Value systems change. What they were tracking doesn't.** People point out that morality has differed across centuries and cultures, and conclude that it's made up. The rules differed. What the rules were reaching for is this, and you can recognize it across every difference of creed; the interlude asked you to check that in your own memory: the second person who caught you.

**You can't calculate your way to goodness.** This follows from the last one. If the rules were only ever reaching for the seeing, then no rule, and no reasoning from rules, can stand in for it. Any framework for "good" can be followed with the people it touches held out of view, and the calculating is done by the part of us that can prove anything, from wherever we already are.

**There is only one guide you can trust.** This is the other half of the last claim. If goodness can't be calculated, what's left is the part of you that knows: the seeing itself, with the person fully in view. Rules, reasons, and frameworks can serve it. None of them can replace it, and every one of them can be followed without it. It isn't the feeling of being right; certainty is a mark of the other thing. It's what's there when someone is fully in front of you, and you're not sure of anything except that they matter.

People raise Hitler as the counterexample: *he thought he was doing good.* He's the perfect example of the opposite. *Thought.* What he followed wasn't the part that knows. It was a complete framework for what "good" meant, held with total certainty, with the people it touched entirely out of view. That is the part that proves, at full size. The part that knows can't operate with people out of view; keeping them in view is what it *is*.

**Compassion is not wisdom.** The one guide tells you that they matter. It doesn't tell you what to do. A person fully turned toward others can still be wrong about everything else, and make a mess. Knowledge, skill, and judgement are separate things, and unevenly distributed. What the guide gives you is the one stance that doesn't feed the darkness, and the one stance from which wisdom has a chance. That's also where the reasoning mind belongs: in service of the guide, it's how care gets anything done.

**In an important sense, nobody's there.** A person doing evil isn't present; that's what turning away is. So anger at the state they're in can be productive, and fierce. But the judgement that *someone in there is choosing this* is aimed at a person who, for that moment, isn't home. It's incoherent. And more than that, it's the same evil, since it takes turning away from them to make it. Chapter 4 says why there's no chooser to find. None of that lets anyone off, them or you; Chapter 4 also says where responsibility actually lives, and it's a heavier standard than the usual one.

**No amount of turning away ever produces a turning back.** That's the big lie: that enough contempt, aimed well enough, will make someone better. It can frighten someone into stopping, and now and then into thinking again. But when they do turn back, it's through the very awareness the contempt was attacking, not through the contempt. King said it: darkness cannot drive out darkness. The only thing that has ever turned anyone back is someone who stayed turned toward them while refusing what they did. That is kindness, and it has nothing to do with being nice. It can be the harshest thing in the room.
