# Chapter 1: Limbo

I want to show you how my conviction that I'm nothing like the "bad people" compels me to act just like them, pushing them deeper into the very condition that enables their wrongdoing; how part of me knows I'm doing this, even as I do it; and how I hide this fact from myself, enabling me to keep doing it. Maybe you'll recognize something of yourself in it. Or maybe not.

I'm going to make some claims about your mind and what you know. I realize this can sound presumptuous. Hold them tentatively, until you see the picture they assemble. Then you can decide for yourself whether they hold. Here's the central one: contempt is the claim that you're a different kind of thing than me, but for it to land, you must be the same kind of thing, and part of you must know it.

Seeing this depends on a state of mind that's easier to find in a memory than to describe, so I'll start by asking you for one.

---
## Limbo

Have you ever done something you knew was wrong at the time, but couldn't fully admit it to yourself until later? Maybe you took credit for someone else's work, said something cruel to someone who'd done nothing to you, or cheated on a test (or even a partner).

Recall what it was like to finally own up to it. The knowledge that it was wrong: did it feel like new information? Or like something you had always known, but somehow hadn't *let* yourself know?

Here's the first claim, and everything else rests on it: that knowledge was never unconscious. It was right there to see—fully available to consciousness—but you were *turned away* from it. It lived in a strange kind of limbo, in which you simultaneously knew and yet somehow didn't. If that doesn't match anything in your memory, the rest of this won't make sense, and I'd rather you know that now.

Was that knowledge just sitting there idle? Any time someone came close to exposing it, or you started getting close to it yourself, did you find a way to deflect? If you did, did that happen passively?

Looking back, did some part of you know you were doing that, even as you did it?

If seeing that makes you want to call yourself names, hold off. That's not owning up; it's another way to hide.

Now try to recall how you knew it was wrong in the first place. Did that knowledge arrive before or after you'd reasoned it through? The part that knew might not have been able to say how it knew. Meanwhile, another part was busy providing reasons. *Everyone does it. It's not a big deal. Just this once.* 

Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?

---

As best I can tell, most of us don't want to harm each other, and usually won't without a reason. But the reasons themselves aren't what allow the harm. All they do is distract us from the fact that we've turned away from the part of ourselves that wouldn't allow it.

Here's a quick check. Picture someone who's done nothing to you, like the last cashier you spoke to, and see their face clearly. Imagine giving them a small kindness first: a real smile, a thank-you. Then a small cruelty: a sneer, a cutting remark. Did either one need a reason before it would go?

I know this is true of myself, and I suspect it's true of others. When I can really see that they're ultimately just like me, harming them is difficult. The part of me that sees that, and that I must turn away from to cause harm: call it my *better nature*.

Notice an asymmetry: I've felt remorse for harm I've done, but never for having been decent. Regret that it cost me, sure—anger at being taken advantage of, or feeling foolish. But never remorse: that sickening sense of having gone against myself. Have you? 

Can you imagine anyone feeling it in reverse? A bad person who does a secret act of kindness, and then lies awake at night wracked with genuine remorse for having gone against his nature?

I can't. We might regret kindness because it cost us, but it's not the same as remorse. That suggests our moral compass isn't just arbitrary; it only points in one direction.

But it can be painful to confront the fact that I might be just like others—including even the worst people. It means that under the right circumstances, I'm capable of the worst acts humans have committed. So some part of me needs to believe I'm made of "better stuff." That there's *some* kind of fundamental difference between us.

I'm usually not aware of this need. Watch how it affects my behavior anyway.

---
## The Encounter

I'll narrate what happens in my mind when I confront a "bad person." My target has just been cruel to someone who didn't deserve it. The threat is over, and now I'm confronting him. If it sounds like I'm contradicting myself in places, it's because two parts of my mind are in tension.

I've decided he's a *bad person*—not just someone who does bad things, but who *is* bad. My eyes are communicating something like: *I'm a better kind of thing than you.* And I want him to feel the full weight of that judgement, almost as a kind of punishment. 

Part of me senses something dark about my message, though I can't quite place it. If I were on the receiving end, I would recognize it instantly. The message *this is all you are* carries a subtle but primal threat: *... and therefore your wellbeing has ceased to matter.*

I would defy a message like that, and violently.

But if I acknowledged that, it would undermine the verdict I feel strangely attached to: he's **bad**, dammit. Irredeemable. So I push that knowledge out of mind. I'm only holding him to account, and if he reacts poorly, well, it only proves my point.

And it's not like I'm actually threatening him. I'm just hinting at what will happen if he doesn't stop. When you look at it that way, it's really more like an *invitation*—to give up his darkness and return to the realm of those who matter.

I sense something underhanded in my behavior, but here's the thing: he's actually bad, so what does it matter? He would have reacted poorly no matter what I did. Who cares if I give him a little nudge to help him reveal what he truly is? It's a net benefit to the world.

And he can't prove I'm doing this, can he? No. And crucially, *neither can I*. So I know it's not on purpose.

But wouldn't you know it, there he goes, accusing me of it anyway. He thinks *I'm* the bad one. *Ha!* Classic projection.

He's not accusing me with his words, but with his eyes:

> *Don't pretend you're doing something noble here. We both know what you're really up to.*

*Shit.* He senses it. But I absolutely cannot afford to admit it. I don't even want to believe I have that kind of darkness in me—the kind that would knowingly forsake a fellow being just to prove I'm nothing like him.

Surely that's not what I'm doing. I'm just being fierce with evil, right? Right. 

So I double down on my attack. *This really **is** what you are—and I'm going to make damn sure everyone knows it.*

Yet for some reason, this only makes him *more* defiant...

---

That's the hot version. Contempt often runs cold. I just write the person off: a quiet filing-away, a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them. It's the same verdict, delivered by withdrawal, and they feel it just the same. If the hot version didn't sound like you, read what follows with the cold one in mind; all of it applies.

---
## The Corruption

I'll just go ahead and name what's happening. I see *genuine darkness* in him, and my fierce desire to stop him comes from a good place. But my verdict that it's *what he is* does not. One part of me knows it will provoke him, but another part is kept in the dark, so that I can absolve myself of blame. [TODO: This is why it sneaks into places like this, where it can hide better.]

He can see both halves: that I'm provoking him, and that I can't admit it. He feels it as a maddening combination of messages—*I know exactly what I'm doing* and *I'll never own up to it*—and that's enough to make him dig in. Neither half alone would be enough to get me both his reaction and my innocence. If it felt accidental, he might forgive or pity me instead. If it looked self-aware, then his accusation would have somewhere to land, making *me* the bad guy.

In brief: I am in limbo about provoking him, and showing him that I'm in limbo is itself the provocation. Normally, having my enemy see my self-deception would be horrible. In this case, it's the whole point.

The signal is the pretense that there is no signal. It's carried entirely by my tone, my gaze, my manner—all of which are plausibly deniable, *even to myself*. We both know that if he tries to name it, it will just be used against him.

If you've been on his end, you know how it closes around you. Deny it, and you're the kind of person who does. Get angry, and that's proof too. Stay calm, and you don't even care. Apologize, and it's too convenient. The stories keep changing; the conclusion never does.

He sees that he cannot appeal to my better nature, because I'm convinced this *is* my better nature.

---

I'm overriding the part of myself that knows (I'm causing harm) in favor of the one that can prove (I'm doing good). And it teaches him to make the same mistake: to demote his inner knowing, because what good is it in a world as rotten as this? It is like a corruption passing from my mind to his.

I've been in his shoes before. To me, it feels something like this:

> *If this is the kind of world I live in—where people can engage in dark behavior and get away with it—then why the hell shouldn't I?*

Of course, I never tell myself I'm going to ignore my better nature. My justifications simply feel more convincing, more *righteous* even—as if they *are* my better nature. The two get just a little harder to tell apart. Maybe you've felt that before.

That's what's happening in my enemy. I sense his hardening, and feel a brief flicker of satisfaction, vindication, even relief at being proven right about him—just before I relabel it disgust or dismay.

> *Ugh, I knew it. Other people are the problem.*

Any doubt I had about him is now erased. Which means I don't have to worry about being like the *bad people.* And that's a much bigger deal than I might notice in the moment. It means I don't have to introspect on my own motives and behaviors.

---

Here are two checks you can try on yourself. Forget about the "right" or "decent" answers.

Is there something primally, viscerally satisfying about seeing evil violently punished?

Bring to mind your worst enemy. Say you had two buttons in front of you. One delivers them the vengeance they so richly deserve. The other gives them a pill that instantly changes their heart. Imagine no one will ever know you were given this choice, let alone what you chose. Is some part of you itching to mash that first button? I'll be honest: there is in me.

Something in me craves punishment, quite independently of any good it might do; over and above the justice it might serve. But who cares? That's just part of being human. It's not like that hidden urge actually *does* anything.

Right?

That lie is exactly what lets it act, in ways I almost never recognize.

---

Notice how precise the trap is. I must be present enough to plant the evidence—track his reactions, counter them, steer around obstacles, etc.—while being absent enough to sincerely believe I didn't. And I must show him both halves while not knowing that I am.

He sees it as intentional because it is—just like your deflection was, in the opening. But although I am intentionally steering him somewhere, I didn't design the setup. I can't have masterminded a plan in which two aspects of my mind coordinate so precisely to get me what I need. 

Here's how I conceive of what's happening.

There's a part of me that helps me get away with self-serving behavior, and that needs to know I'm nothing like the "bad people" who do exactly that. It is the same part that craves punishing them. It acts as if it has goals of its own, and it pursues them *intentionally.*

Don't mistake the grammar for the claim: I'm not saying there's a separate thing living in me. I'm saying that, while I'm turned away, my own mind behaves as if there were. None of this lets me off the hook. It's my mind, and I answer for what it does.

This part reveals itself to my enemies through my eyes while condemning the same thing in them. They recognize what I'm doing, but have no recourse. And that is *how* it punishes them: by keeping them trapped in the corrupted state that enables harm. It's as though it's telling them: 

> *Yes, we ultimately cause harm through the same mechanism—but I'm going to get away with mine, by making you rot for yours.*

I desperately want to call this behavior *unconscious* (if I can admit I do it at all), because otherwise what would it make me? This kind of evil is something only *bad people* do. In me it's always an accident; something I had "no idea" I was doing. In them, it's done with "full awareness." I can literally see it in their eyes! That's why I deserve understanding and they deserve contempt.

When I'm sure someone was fully aware of what they were doing, I've made them a villain. When I'm sure they had no idea, I've made them a machine. Either way, they're a different kind of thing from me. What I find hard to hold is what I found in my own memory: that they knew, and didn't let themselves know. And sometimes I'm simply wrong: what they did wasn't harm at all. Sometimes I only view it that way because it serves me somehow.

I don't do it because I'm a bad person. I do it because I believe there *are* "bad people"—people who are fundamentally defective. And I only do that because, at a level I cannot let myself see, I fear I might be one of them.

Every enemy this part helps me make gives that fear somewhere to point other than at me.

---

There is a way out.

Notice how I've called it both turning away from "myself" and from my "better nature." Go back to the memory at the start: the knowledge you hadn't *let yourself* know, so that you could do what you did. It was *yourself* you were keeping it from. Why should turning away from your better nature require turning away from yourself?

Now the reverse: is there anything you hide from yourself that your better nature wouldn't object to?

Sure, sometimes I hide good things from the part of me that would call them weakness. But I am able to look those things squarely in the face later. I never feel *remorse* for them. They don't feel hidden from *myself* in the same way.

And when you finally owned up, did it feel like losing something of yourself, or like getting something back?

Here's what I think: our better nature isn't one part among others. It's what we most fundamentally are. It's still our choice, in a sense, whether to stay turned toward it. But if I attribute bad choices to a "bad chooser"—whether consciously or not—I've just recreated the problem one level deeper. I feel shame in myself and contempt for others, and the cycle continues.

They say integrity is what you do when no one is watching. Whenever I've broken mine, it was by telling myself that no one was. But someone always is: me. Not the part of me busy with reasons; the part I'd turned away from, so I wouldn't feel it watching. And I knew it was there the whole time.

As best I can tell, the only solution is to stop turning away. Everything else is self-deception.

---
## Integrity

So what does it look like when someone fights evil without self-deception?

Think of a time when someone corrected you with integrity, without the slightest trace of contempt. Not more gently. Just that their anger was directed at what you did, and not what you are.

They might have been *more* fierce than I just was with my enemy. But did it make you dig in? Vow revenge?

For me, it has a very different effect. Even if they have different politics, religion, creed: something about their behavior feels deeply aligned. It feels like a reminder—*you're better than this*. This robs me of self-righteous indignation, forcing me to confront myself. It's the exact opposite of what I did to my enemy above, where I gave him a hook to use against me.

It's true that contempt can sometimes stop people's misbehavior (at least, their public misbehavior, and temporarily). But it's not what does the stopping. It's a little darkness I inject into otherwise-good behavior, poisoning it. Remove it, and the fierceness stays; only the poison goes.

Can you remember a time when you corrected someone in a nasty way, but then later, with more years of wisdom, took an entirely different approach to the same problem? One that worked better?

Fighting evil is the place where my own darkness is most likely to come out, because it has the best cover: I get to pass off my contempt as righteous anger.

To anyone watching from a distance, the two might look the same. But between the two people facing each other, they aren't confused. The person on the receiving end knows the difference in their marrow—and so do I. This, I've found, is what allows evil to spread while staying "off the record."

---
## Common knowledge

But this all raises a curious question: how could the part of me that was provoking him be so sure of his reaction?

A wordless, seemingly-contentless signal only works if both parties already know what it means. And it only reads *as* a signal (and not a slip) if each knows that the other knows that the other knows, etc.

And what is it that we both know in this way? That we are the **same kind of thing**: beings who have a better nature; who must turn away from it to cause harm; and who *only* turn away from it to cause harm. In the opening, were you surprised that I knew you needed limbo to cause harm? If not, why not?

It's why a single look, which on its face says only *you're nothing*, can show him something like this (and why I can count on it to land):

> *I am turned away from my better nature. I know you know what this means, because it's the same trick that enables your own wrongdoing. I want you to know that I know this about you, so that you can feel the maddening hypocrisy of what I'm doing to you.*

Hypocrisy only makes sense between two people who answer to the same thing.

Part of me doesn't want to believe we answer to the same thing. It would shatter my whole worldview. Because if we answer to the same thing, then it has binding authority. And if it has authority, then I was never free to override it in the first place.

The story I tell myself is that I am the one in charge, sitting between my better nature and my self-interest, deciding which one gets to win today. I treat my conscience like an advisor offering an opinion, which I can weigh against other practical concerns and set aside if the price is right.

But it's not an advisor. To treat it like one is already to turn away—and to teach others to do the same, which I will then judge them for.

The moment I pretend that listening to it is optional—that I have the right to look at what is right and say not today—I've already committed to the harm. I'm just looking for reasons to make it feel like a considered choice instead of what it actually is: a betrayal of the only thing in me that knows better. Listening to it doesn't hand me answers, though. I can stay turned toward someone and still not know what to do.

If my enemy and I answer to the same thing, then I don't get to look down on him from above. I'm just another person who knew what was right, decided it was negotiable, and then looked for someone worse than me to prove I wasn't so bad after all.

---

Now notice where the signal lands. Recall the last time someone's contempt really got under your skin, and feel for *where* it was trying to reach. Not your pride: an insult to your pride is easier to shrug off. This reaches further in, for the *you* that knows you count: the same you that you'd have to turn away from to harm someone, and the same you the corrector with integrity was speaking to.

Think of the last time you sent that look yourself. Did you know they'd read it? Did you mean them to know you meant it, while giving yourself an "out"? Did you count on their seeing that too, so they couldn't call it a slip, or name it without sounding crazy?

Love and contempt are addressed to the same place. One says *I know you're in there.* The other says *there's nobody in there*, and they both say it to the somebody who is.

I was speaking directly to the part of him that I claimed didn't exist—his better nature. And, in my desperate need to prove myself right (and righteous), I was attempting to bury it under another layer of confusion. He sensed that, which is where his accusation came from. And I felt the truth in it, which is why I couldn't stand to face it.

What I'm hiding isn't that I *could* do what the "bad people" do, it's that I'm *already doing it*, and using them as a place to hide the fact that I am.

It's hard to describe the excruciating pain I experienced the first time I saw this—that I had been smothering the "innocent" part of my enemies, and that I had always known it but carefully hidden it from myself. What I was displaying through my eyes was the very essence of cruelty—the thing I hate most in this world.

Maybe this isn't how it works in others. But it's what I found in myself.

---

How is it possible that we all know this about one another? It's because my better nature *is* what sees yours. What looks out of me is the same kind of thing I'm looking at in you, so each of us already sees that the other sees, and so on. Nothing has to be calculated.

That's why, although it's normally presumptuous to assume things about your mind, this one feels like safe ground. I don't feel like I'm *guessing* that your better nature is the deepest part of you, or just asserting it to be nice. It's more like something I see.

When I'm unable to see it, it isn't due to something I lack. It's because of something I've *added*. Some belief about what you are, often as a result of something I believe about myself, whether or not I realize it. Underneath it, usually, is fear, pain, or pride: something I'm not ready to face in myself.

Here's something you can check: in moments where you're feeling fully whole—not a trace of either shame or pride: is it easier to forgive others? To see the part of them that doesn't want to cause harm, and to help correct their behavior in a way that actually works?

From what I can tell, even the worst person must turn away from a deep part of themselves to cause harm. Their better nature can be buried so deep it seems gone, but I don't think it ever works in reverse. Here's a brief check that might help elicit that recognition.

Have you ever been on the receiving end of a dehumanizing gaze? Can you feel the sense in which the other person feels absent or somehow "turned away" from himself? Why would he have to turn away, if what he is, is essentially bad? What is he turned away *from*? I'm not saying you're as bad as him, but is it possible that you look a little bit like this when treating others with contempt?

When I still feel a fundamental difference between me and my worst enemy, I ask which part of me needs it to be there. It's always the one that wants to excuse my contempt.

We'd disagree on the details of what's good on almost any subject you could name. But whatever "good" turns out to mean, as best I can tell there's only one thing in us that's trying to sense its way toward it, and it's what we most fundamentally are. That's why the worst thing I can do for anyone is what I did to my enemy: push them further away. And the best thing I can do, for them and through them for the world, is stay in touch with it myself. Sometimes that rubs off on them, though I can't take credit for it.

---

## The spread

The harm I caused my enemy doesn't stop at him. It subtly affects his future choices, and through them, other people: the next stranger he won't give the benefit of the doubt, the kid who watches him do it. What gets passed along isn't my look, or anything that resembles it. It's the turning away itself, and the permission it gives: *if that's how the world works, why shouldn't I?* By the time it has passed through enough hands, it can take any shape at all.

And I send it *about* people, to my allies, with nobody's face in view. Or online, on social media. That spreads it too. Everyone in the conversation learns that the verdict is safe to hold, and leaves a little more practiced at it. Part of me knows it will spread, and that's part of why I send it.

When my contempt is aimed at what they are, and not just at what they did, my mind is doing the same thing: making the world darker for my own benefit, while telling itself a very convincing story of how what it's doing is good. Then I judge that darkness from afar, as if I'd had no part in it, and that lets me get away with more. This is why, if I want to end theirs, I have to see how to end mine.

Most contempt is quiet: a vague sense of *well, I could never do that*, or *I'm just a better kind of thing than them*. It isn't always moral, but it's always a sense of being more deserving. The stupid, the slow and the weak get the same verdict, with better cover: *I'm only being accurate.* It doesn't feel like contempt at all, and it can happen a thousand times a day without my noticing.

I send it to people I love. The *what's wrong with you?* that needs them to know they're broken. The sigh that tells them what I really think of them, dressed up to make me seem like the reasonable one. The look on their face that tells me it landed, which I pretend not to have seen, leaving them nothing to accuse me of. For one second the verdict flashes through my eyes, and I make sure they see it, and then I go back to loving them as if nothing had happened. It has the same flavor as what we call evil.

Here's what I've found in myself. The more responsibility I take for the harm caused by my own self-deception, the more clearly I see how the bad behavior in others comes from their own need to self-deceive. I cannot just willfully choose to see it. It takes work. If I try to pretend I see their "better nature" when I can't, I'm liable to cause more harm. A mind deeply lost in self-deception knows how to exploit a pretense like that. The only real solution I've found is to *actually* see it, and that only happens as I become more free of my own self-deception.

And fewer people look inherently defective, or less than me. Whether that number ever goes to zero is something you can discover for yourself.

Watch for it even now. The subtlest version I know is the one I feel toward people who haven't seen through this trap yet. It can look like patience, or even like care. Sometimes it looks like me writing this book *at* people, instead of *for* them.

If seeing this makes you want to hide, notice that's the same verdict ("you're *bad*"), pointed inward. It feels like accountability. It isn't. What I need in order to look is what my enemy needed from me, and what the corrector with integrity gave me: to be seen as more than what I do.
