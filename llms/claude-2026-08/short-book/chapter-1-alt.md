# Chapter 1: Limbo

Contempt is the claim that you're a different kind of thing than me. But it only works because you're the same kind of thing, and both of us know it. I want to show you where that contradiction is, and what it reveals: that evil is an absence that behaves like a presence, that believing otherwise reliably produces more of it, and that part of us has always known this.

Some of what follows I'll say before I've earned it. Don't grant it. Hold it the way you'd hold a puzzle piece you haven't placed, and judge it by whether it ends up fitting what you find in yourself. If it doesn't fit, drop it.

Seeing this depends on a state of mind that's easier to find in a memory than to describe, so I'll start by asking you for one.

---
## Limbo

Have you ever done something you knew was wrong at the time, but couldn't fully admit it to yourself until later? Maybe you took credit for someone else's work, said something cruel to someone who'd done nothing to you, or cheated on a test (or even a partner).

Recall what it was like to finally own up to it. The knowledge that it was wrong: did it feel like new information? Or like something you had always known, but somehow hadn't *let* yourself know?

We like to call such knowledge "unconscious," but it isn't. It was right there to see—fully available to consciousness—you were just *turned away* from it. It lived in a strange kind of limbo, in which you simultaneously knew and yet somehow didn't.

Any time someone came close to exposing it, or you started getting close to it yourself, did you find a way to deflect? Did that happen passively? Looking back, did some part of you know you were doing that, even as you did it?

Now try to recall how you knew it was wrong in the first place. Did that knowledge arrive before or after you'd reasoned it through? The part that knew might not have been able to say how it knew. Meanwhile, another part was busy providing reasons. *Everyone does it. It's not a big deal. Just this once.*

The part that knew may not have won the argument, but it never budged, either. It kept on quietly knowing what it knew.

Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?

Now imagine doing the same thing without any justifications. Could you have?

And the reverse. You know the feeling of remorse: that sickening sense of having gone against yourself. Have you ever felt it over something you did out of decency? Can you imagine even the worst person feeling it over that?

---
## The Two Strangers

Imagine that while you were in that state—doing wrong, justifying it, half-knowing—a stranger had caught you and publicly chastised you for it. Not just for doing something wrong, but for *being the kind of person who does*.

Does that make it *easier* or *harder* to confront what you already know?

For me, it's harder, because *who the hell is he to judge?* I can tell he's the kind of person who does this too—things he knows are wrong, while pretending they're not.

I'd take that bet at any odds. But on what evidence? I've never met him. I know nothing about him. All I have to go on is that he's standing there, doing what he's doing.

---

Now imagine a different stranger had caught you—but instead of treating you with contempt, he treated you with dignity. Not more gently. Just that his anger was directed at what you *did*, and not what you *are*.

Would you have dug in? Vowed revenge?

Why not?

The problem wasn't being caught. In fact, he could have been *harsher* than the first person—said things that stung more, in front of more people, left you less room to wriggle out—and it still wouldn't have triggered the digging-in response.

What was different about it?

It's like he was reminding you of something: that *you're better than this*. The opposite of what the first stranger was doing: claiming that *this is all you are*.

You might have disagreed with him on nearly everything—politics, religion, creed—and it wouldn't have mattered. Whatever he was standing up for wasn't *his*. It wasn't a rule, or a side. It was something you were already on the side of, and he was only reminding you.

And I'd bet he isn't the kind who sneaks around trying to get away with things.

What is that thing whose side you are both on; that two people who agree on nothing can both be serving?

---
## The Encounter

I've been the first stranger more times than I'd like to admit. I'll narrate what happens in my mind when I am. My target has just been cruel to someone who didn't deserve it. The threat is over, and now I'm calling him out. If I contradict myself, it's because two parts of my mind are in tension.

I've decided he's a *bad person*—not just someone who does bad things, but who *is* bad. My eyes are saying: *I'm a better kind of thing than you.* And I want him to feel the full weight of that, almost as a kind of punishment.

Part of me senses something dark in the message. If I were on the receiving end, I'd recognize it instantly: *this is all you are* carries a primal threat: *and therefore what I do to you no longer qualifies as harm.* I would defy a message like that too, and violently.

But if I admitted that, it would undermine the verdict I feel strangely attached to: he's **bad**, dammit. So I push it out of mind. I'm only holding him to account, and if he reacts poorly, that only proves my point. It's not like I'm actually threatening him. If anything it's an *invitation*, to return to the realm of those who matter!

But there's nothing inviting about my tone or gaze. How could there be, when I'm trying to convince him that *this is all he is*?

On the other hand, he can't prove I'm doing this, can he? No. And crucially, *neither can I*. So if he accuses me of it, he can only be projecting.

And there he goes, accusing me of it. Not in words. His eyes say: *We both know what you're really up to.*

*Shit.* He's onto me. But I can't afford to admit it. I don't even want to believe I have that kind of darkness in me—the kind that would forsake a fellow being just to prove I'm nothing like him, and then cover it up.

So I remind myself that what I'm doing is good. Sure, it may be a bit dark, but it's the *good* kind of darkness—the kind that fights the real kind.

I double down: *this really **is** what you are.* Yet for some reason, that only makes him more defiant.

---

Part of me knew the whole time that I could have done what the second stranger did: served justice without injecting my own darkness. But fierce integrity and contempt can be made to look alike from the outside, so much so that when I look back later, even I won't be sure which one I did. And I'll have produced the result I needed: proof that my enemy is beyond help, because look how he reacted.

I wasn't *exposing* his darkness. I was creating more of it.

This is the hot kind of contempt. It can also run cold. I just write the person off: a quiet filing-away, a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them. It's the same verdict, delivered by withdrawal, and it's meant to be felt: by them, or by whoever's watching. 

It needn't be about right and wrong, either. Someone slow, ignorant, or clumsy can get the same verdict (*you're a lesser kind of thing*) and I write it off as "just being honest."

---

Back to my enemy. He was probably feeling something like this:

> *What a hypocrite. If this is the kind of world I live in—where people can ignore their conscience and call it "righteous"—then why the hell shouldn't I? I'd be a chump.*

Of course, we never actually tell ourselves we're going to ignore our conscience. Our justifications simply feel more convincing, more *righteous*—as if they *are* our conscience. The two get just a little harder to tell apart.

I'm overriding the part that knows (I'm causing harm) in favor of the one that can prove (I'm doing good). And it teaches him to make the same mistake: to demote his inner knowing, because what good is it in a world as rotten as this?

I sense his hardening, and feel a brief flicker of satisfaction, vindication, even relief at being proven right about him—just before I relabel it disgust or dismay:

> *Ugh, I knew it. Other people are the problem.*

Any doubt I had about him is now erased. Which means I don't have to worry about being like the *bad people.* I can stop introspecting on my own motives and behaviors, because who cares about my minor misdeeds when there's *real* evil out there to fight?

If I'd looked carefully, I'd have seen my role in causing it. So I didn't—and that *was* my role in causing it.

---

Here are some checks you can run on yourself.

Bring to mind your worst enemy. Say you had two buttons in front of you. One delivers them the vengeance they so richly deserve. The other gives them a pill that instantly changes their heart. Imagine no one will ever know you were given this choice, let alone what you chose. Is some part of you itching to mash that first button? I'll be honest: there is in me.

And think of the last time you watched someone you despise get what was coming to him. Was the pleasure only in the harm stopping, or in the punishment?

Something in me craves punishment, quite independently of any good it might do; over and above the justice it might serve. I tell myself it's innocuous. That this lust for vengeance is just part of being human. It's not like I would ever act on it.

And that lie is exactly what lets me act on it, in ways I rarely recognize until later.

---
## The Corruption

The reason I don't have to argue myself into doing the right thing is that in the absence of justifications, it's what I naturally desire. I call that deepest part of myself my *better nature*, and it isn't following arbitrary rules. It is what sees myself in others. That's why I must *turn away* from it to cause harm. My excuses aren't there to convince *it* (which never works); they're there to convince *me* that it's just one more voice among the noise, and safe to ignore. It isn't.

Part of what it sees in others is that they, too, can only cause harm by *turning away*. But when I turn away from mine, I can no longer see this. Other people look defective—like they don't have to "turn away" from anything to do wrong, because they're just bad. But the perception of our similarity never disappears, because turning away doesn't truly make things unconscious. That perception is still conscious; I'm just *unaware* of it: limbo.

If you've ever been on the receiving end of a dehumanizing gaze, could you feel the sense in which the other person is somehow absent or "turned away" from himself? I think that's what we're sensing. What are they turned away *from*? Why would they have to turn away from anything, if what they are is essentially bad?

But part of me cannot stand to believe I'm anything like the *bad people*. So what do I do? I find clever ways to exaggerate our supposed difference. To make them worse, until their behavior is so grotesque that I'm forced to explain it by something else—some alien quality called "evil" that I could never hope to understand, only to valiantly fight.

And how do I do this? By revealing to them, through my tone and my gaze, that the darkness I see in them lives in me, too; and that I can wield it against them while being hailed a hero. We both know I could have served justice without injecting that dark behavior into the interaction, and that it would have worked better. But who can prove that I did any such thing? Where is this supposed "darkness"? Point to it!

My need to see others as bad—that is, as inherently defective—doesn't present itself to me as a need, because it is what helps me *make* them bad, and becoming aware of it would reveal my *own* badness. So it presents itself to me as truth. I can literally *see* the darkness in their eyes, and I believe it's what they *are*. What I don't see is that I can only believe this while being *turned away* myself. In that state, I'm not in control. My *need* is.

Look back at the encounter and watch it work. My stories kept changing; my conclusion never did. When he saw through one, the next was ready, and when he called me on it I wasn't embarrassed or confused. I doubled down, in exactly the way needed to keep both conclusions standing: that he was bad, and that I was only seeing it. Something tracked his reactions and steered around them. A merely unconscious process doesn't do that. It doesn't fight to stay hidden. Yours did, if you deflected when someone came close.

And if you've been on his end, you know how it closes around you. Deny it, and you're the kind of person who does. Get angry, and that's proof too. Stay calm, and you don't even care. Apologize, and it's too convenient. Did it feel like an accident?

My enemy can see that I'm in this state, even though I can't. *Because* I can't. And he sees it as *evil*, for the same reason I see him that way. And I *rely* on him seeing it that way, because that's what licenses him to abandon his morals, which in turn proves me right about him—because what kind of person reacts in such an unhinged manner to obviously-righteous behavior?

The harm I cause isn't physical—at least, not yet. It's psychological: showing him that I can provoke him into becoming the kind of person who can only be dealt with in *one way*, and get away with it. And he sees the horrible truth of that, which nudges him toward becoming exactly that kind of person, which is what makes it true.

---

It's as if I'm saying to him:

> *I know exactly how you cause harm, because I'm doing it to you **right now**. But I'll get away with it—because I'm only "fighting evil," and how could that ever be wrong?*

He knows it's true, and I *know* he knows it, and I want him to see that I know that, and so on—and for him to know he can’t say so without sounding unhinged. That was his accusation: *we both know what you're really up to.* And it's all plausibly deniable, *even to myself.*

You may have felt this before, as a maddening hall of mirrors. You both know what's happening, and that it can't be named.

Ask yourself: how could this trap work unless we both know we're the same kind of thing—beings who *turn away* to cause harm—and know that the other knows it, and that they know that, and so on? My claim that I'm a *better kind of thing* is maddening precisely because we both know it's false; that the only reason someone makes it is to do that thing we do, where we lie to ourselves to get away with self-serving behavior.

That’s why my signal needs no words. It can be carried entirely by gaze and tone—which have the added benefit of being unquotable, thus sealing the trap. The signal is the pretense that there is no signal.

This is how *turning away* propagates itself.

---

And notice where my signal lands. 

Recall the last time someone's contempt really got under your skin, and feel for *where* it was trying to reach. Not your pride: an insult to your pride is easier to shrug off. This reaches further in, for the *you* that knows you count: the same you that you'd have to turn away from to harm someone, and the same you the second stranger was speaking to.

**Love and contempt are addressed to the same place.** One says *I know you're in there.* The other says *there's nobody in there*, and they both say it to the somebody who is.

I am speaking directly to the part of my enemy that I claim doesn't exist—his better nature—and, in my desperate need to prove myself right (and righteous), attempting to extinguish it.

I've laid this all out as an argument, but for me it wasn't one. I saw it clearly in myself, the same way you found your own wrongdoing in the opening: part of me was knowingly darkening my enemy, and through him, the world, in order to better hide itself from me.

In one sense, it felt utterly malevolent: it was gleefully extinguishing light from this world. In another sense, it felt totally innocent. All that had happened was that I had forgotten my better nature, and out of a fear of what that implied about me, I desperately tried to hide it.

---
## The Way Out

So what is this "darkness"? Take two things you checked. You couldn't have done it without a story; something had to go out of view first. And remorse only ever ran one way: nothing you did out of decency ever felt like going against yourself, and you couldn't picture it in anyone. Put those together: what I turn from is the baseline, and the turning is a departure from it. Not a second baseline.

Badness is the result of turning away, not its cause.

When I try to make it a cause, I'm just recreating the problem one level deeper: I feel shame in myself and contempt for others, and the cycle continues.

But that doesn't make it nothing. Call it nothing, and I'll underestimate it: I'll stop looking at exactly the moment it's crucial to look more closely. It tracks, it aims, it fights to stay hidden. It's an absence that behaves like a presence. It has no substance of its own; everything it uses—my attention, my reasons, my eyes—is borrowed from me. When it's operating, it looks and behaves exactly like you'd expect a "bad essence" to look. That's what I looked like to my enemy.

Now look at why I needed the essence. I don't do any of this because I'm a bad person. I do it because I believe there *are* bad people—people who are fundamentally defective—and I only believe that because, at a level I can't let myself see, I fear I might be one of them. Every enemy gives that fear somewhere to point other than at me. If you can find even one memory where that's plainly true of you, that's the one to look at. Underneath it is a deeper fear: that there may be no such thing as real goodness, in me or in anyone, so I'd better protect myself at any cost. And that fear is exactly what I taught my enemy: *genuine goodness is a myth. Only a chump believes otherwise.* The same thing is happening in him, and each of us is sure we're free of it. That's how believing in evil produces more of it.

Go back to the memory at the start, to the knowledge you hadn't *let yourself* know. What were you hiding it from? When you finally owned up, did it feel like losing something of yourself, or like getting something back? And if you could keep only one of the two parts—the one that knew without arguing, or the one that justified—which would you keep?

Here's what I make of that. My better nature isn't one part among others. It's what I am in the absence of all self-deception. That's why I call it *myself*: not because it's nicer, but because it's the one that's home. And it's the same in him. It's what my contempt was aimed at.

This matters more than it looks. If darkness were as much a part of what I am as care is, then it's in him the same way, my verdict would just be seeing clearly, and contempt would be the honest response. Everything I've said turns on which of the two is home. If darkness were an essence, I would be right to be afraid of it, and ashamed, and I'd have to keep proving it's in others and not in me. But if it's something I *do*, then I can stop.

When I feel a fundamental difference between me and my worst enemy, I ask which part of me needs there to be one.

---
## The Spread

The harm doesn't stop at him. It subtly affects his future choices, and through them, other people: the next stranger he won't give the benefit of the doubt, the kid who watches him do it. What gets passed along isn't my look. It's the turning away itself, and the permission it gives.

And I send it *about* people, to my allies, with nobody's face in view. Everyone in the conversation learns that the verdict is safe to hold, and leaves a little more practiced at it.

Most contempt is quiet: a vague sense of *well, I could never do that*. It doesn't feel like contempt at all, and it can happen a thousand times a day without my noticing. I send it to people I love. The *what's wrong with you?* that needs them to know they're broken. The sigh that tells them what I really think of them, dressed up to make me seem like the reasonable one. The look on their face that tells me it landed, which I pretend not to have seen. For one second the verdict flashes through my eyes, and I make sure they see it, and then I go back to loving them as if nothing had happened.

*That wasn't the real me* can be used as an excuse, but I also think it's true. I did it, and it's mine to answer for. But I could only do it by turning away from myself.

The subtlest contempt I know is the one I feel toward people who haven't seen through this trap yet. It can look like patience, or even care. Sometimes it looks like me writing this book *at* people, instead of *for* them.

If seeing this makes you want to hide, notice that's the same verdict pointed inward. It feels like accountability. It isn't. What I need in order to look is what my enemy needed from me, and what the second stranger gave: to be seen as more than what I do.

---

So what is it that two people who agree on nothing can both be serving? We may never agree on a definition of "good," but we already agree on how to orient toward it, and part of us knows it. That's why the worst thing I can do for anyone is what I did to my enemy: teach them to forget it. And the best thing I can do, for them and through them for the world, is remember it myself.

I've gone missing—and he knows from the inside what that implies. This part of me *relies* on him knowing it, because how else would it elicit the charge of hypocrisy? It needs to know that he will recognize me as *turned away*, and that it's not an accident, because the intentional quality is what makes him dig in. It's what licenses his own turning away, which I'll hold against him—because what have I done wrong?

If it felt accidental, he might forgive or pity me instead. If it looked self-aware, then his accusation would have somewhere to land, making *me* the bad guy. It must look intentional, but without there being anyone there to claim responsibility—the limbo state. Recall being on the receiving end, and see if that's how it felt.

He cannot appeal to my conscience, because I'm convinced this *is* my conscience. 

And that's the whole trick. By turning away from my better nature, I see a world full of people I'm entitled to treat this way. They recognize what I'm doing even though I don't—*because* I don't—and feel compelled to return the favor, which increasingly shows me that I'm right.

I cannot convince you that evil isn't a different thing. I can only invite you to reflect on how you look when treating people with contempt, and on how hard it is to avoid feeling contempt when you're convinced that someone is "evil."
