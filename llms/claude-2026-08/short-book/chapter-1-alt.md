# Chapter 1: Limbo

> _For so long as the root of wickedness is hidden, it is strong. But when it is recognized, it is dissolved. When it is revealed, it perishes. ... It is powerful because we have not recognized it._ —The Gospel of Philip

I want to show you how my conviction that I'm nothing like the "bad people" compels me to act just like them, pushing them deeper into the very condition that enables their wrongdoing; how part of me knows I'm doing this, even as I do it; and how I hide this fact from myself, enabling me to keep doing it. Maybe you'll recognize something of yourself in it. Or maybe not.

I'm going to make some claims about your mind and what you know. I realize this can sound presumptuous. Hold them tentatively, until you see the picture they assemble. Then you can decide for yourself whether they hold. Here's the central one. Contempt is the claim that you're a different kind of thing than me, but for it to land, you must be the same kind of thing.

Seeing this depends on a state of mind that's easier to find in a memory than to describe, so I'll start by asking you for one.

---
## Limbo

Have you ever done something you knew was wrong at the time, but couldn't fully admit it to yourself until later? Maybe you took credit for someone else's work, said something cruel to someone who'd done nothing to you, or cheated on a test (or even a partner).

Recall what it was like to finally own up to it. The knowledge that it was wrong: did it feel like new information? Or like something you had always known, but somehow hadn't *let* yourself know?

Here's the first claim, and everything else rests on it: that knowledge was never unconscious. It was right there to see, fully available to consciousness, but you were *turned away* from it. It lived in a strange kind of *limbo*, in which you simultaneously knew and yet somehow didn't. If that doesn't match anything in your memory, the rest of this won't make sense, and I'd rather you know that now.

Was that knowledge just sitting there idle? Any time someone came close to exposing it, or you started getting close to it yourself, did you find a way to deflect?

Looking back, did some part of you know you were doing that, even as you did it?

Now try to recall how you knew it was wrong in the first place. Did that knowledge arrive before or after you'd reasoned it through? The part that knew might not have been able to say how it knew. Meanwhile, another part was busy providing reasons. *Everyone does it. It's not a big deal. Just this once.* 

Why did one side have to argue, and the other not? And which side did you listen to?

---

Why do we have to hide such knowledge from ourselves? For me, it's because I don't actually want to hurt other people. In fact, I *can't*, when I can clearly see myself in them. To cause harm, I must somehow overlook that perception. The part of me that sees myself in others, and that I must turn away from to cause harm: call it my *better nature*.

Here's a quick check. Picture someone who's done nothing to you, like the last cashier you spoke to, and see their face clearly. Try a small kindness first: a real smile, a thank-you. Then a small cruelty: a sneer, a cutting remark. Did either one need a reason before it would go?

Notice an asymmetry: I've felt remorse for harm I've done, but never for having been decent. Regret that it cost me, sometimes, but not remorse-the feeling of having gone against myself. Have you? Can you imagine it even in the worst person?

At some level, I know that this is how it works in others, too. But that's a painful thought. It means that under the right circumstances, I'm capable of the worst acts humans have committed. Some part of me needs to believe I'm made of "better stuff."

Watch what happens when I believe that.

---

## The Encounter

I'll narrate what happens in my mind when I confront someone like this. My target is someone who's just been cruel to an innocent person. The threat is over, and now I'm confronting him. If it sounds like I'm contradicting myself in places, it's because two parts of my mind are in tension.

I've decided he's a *bad person*—not just someone who does bad things, but who *is* bad. My eyes are communicating something like: *I'm a better kind of thing than you.* And I want him to feel the full weight of it, almost as a kind of punishment. 

Part of me senses something dark about my message, though I can't quite place it. If I were on the receiving end, I would recognize it instantly. The message *this is all you are* carries a subtle but primal threat: *... and therefore your wellbeing has ceased to matter.*

I would defy a message like that too, and violently.

But if I acknowledged that, it would undermine the verdict I feel strangely attached to: he's **bad**, dammit. Irredeemable. So I push that knowledge out of mind. I'm only being fierce, and if he reacts poorly, well, it only proves my point.

And it's not like I'm *actually* threatening him. I'm just hinting at what will happen if he doesn't stop. When you look at it that way, it's really more like an *invitation*—to give up his darkness and return to the realm of those who matter.

But the truth is, there's nothing inviting about my tone or gaze. Nothing that signals he'd be welcome back. How could there be, when I'm trying to convince him that *this is all he is*?

I sense something underhanded in my behavior—that I'm provoking him in a way that I'll later hold against him—but here's the thing: he's actually bad, so what does it matter? He would have reacted poorly no matter what. Who cares if I give him a little nudge? It just helps him reveal what he is. That's a net benefit to the world.

He can't prove I'm doing this, can he? No. And crucially, *neither can I*. So I know it's not on purpose.

But wouldn't you know it, he's accusing me of it. He thinks *I'm* the bad one. Ha! Classic projection.

He's not accusing me with his words, but with his eyes:

> *Don't pretend you're doing something noble here. We both know what you're up to.*

*Shit.* He senses it. But I absolutely cannot afford to admit it. I don't even want to believe I have that kind of darkness in me—the kind that would knowingly forsake a fellow being just to prove I'm right. That I'm nothing like him.

Surely that's not what I'm doing. I'm only holding him to account. So I double down on my righteous attack. *This really **is** what you are—and I'm going to make damn sure everyone knows it.*

Yet for some reason, this only makes him *more* defiant...

---

That's the hot version. Contempt often runs cold. I just write the person off: a quiet filing-away, a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them. It's the same verdict, delivered by withdrawal, and they feel it just the same. If the hot version didn't sound like you, read what follows with the cold one in mind; all of it applies.

---

## The Corruption

He leaves the interaction more certain than ever that his enemies are evil—unable to own up to their own darkness. Which means he feels less of a pull to stand by his own better nature. From the receiving end, it often feels like this:

> *If this is the kind of world I live in—one where people can lie to themselves and get away with it—then why the fuck shouldn't I?*

I immediately sense his hardening, and feel a brief flicker of satisfaction, vindication, even relief at being proven right about him—just before I relabel it disgust or dismay. If I'd looked carefully, I'd have seen my role in causing it. So I didn't look—and that refusal is exactly what caused it.

So: was it an accident, or was my turning away motivated?

Well, look what it got me: proof that he's evil. After all, he dug in against my righteous behavior.

> *I knew it. Other people are the problem.*

Any doubt I might have had that I'm better than him is now erased. I don't have to worry about being like the *bad people.* And that's a much bigger relief than I might notice in the moment. It means I don't have to introspect so much on my own motives and behaviors.

Here are two checks you can try on yourself. Forget about the "right" or "decent" answers.

Is there something primally, viscerally satisfying about seeing evil violently punished? 

Bring to mind your worst enemy. If you had the choice between delivering them the vengeance they so richly deserve, or a pill that would instantly enlighten them, is some part of you itching to mash that first button? I'll be honest: there is in me.

Something in me craves *punishment*, over and above any justice it might serve. I tell myself it's innocuous, as long as I don't act on it. And that is exactly how I act on it, without seeing that I am.

---

I'll go ahead and name what's happening. One part of my mind is provoking him into the reaction I need, while another part is kept in the dark, so that I can absolve myself of blame.

He can see both halves: that I *am* provoking him, and that it's hidden from me. This combination is uniquely maddening, because it communicates both *I know exactly what I'm doing* and *I'll never own up to it.* Normally, having my enemy see my self-deception would be horrible. In this case, it is ideal—because it *is* my provocation. That combination is both enough to produce the outcome I need, and also necessary. Notice: if it felt accidental, he might forgive or pity me instead. And if it were *purely* intentional, then it would read as overt malice—which would make me the bad guy.

In short: I am in limbo about provoking him, and showing him that I *am* in limbo is itself the provocation.

He receives it as a message, but one he is denied the ability to prove. Where's the evidence? There isn't any. It is carried entirely by my tone, my gaze, my manner. Nothing that can be audited, *even by myself*. He sees that he cannot appeal to my better nature, because I'm convinced this *is* my better nature—and that's a condition that part of me knows he will recognize as the hallmark of evil.

Here's another way to look at it. He's watching me reject the part of me that knows (I'm causing harm) in favor of the one that can prove (I'm doing good), and wielding it as a weapon against him in a way he cannot prove. That's what teaches him to make the same mistake. To demote his inner knowing, because what good is it in a world as rotten as this? It is a corruption that's passing from my mind to his.

We never tell ourselves "I'm going to ignore my conscience." The darker and more self-serving the world appears, the more our conscience feels naive; like it can't protect us. So our reasons feel increasingly obvious, even righteous. Like they *are* our conscience.

---

For me to do this, I must be present enough to plant the evidence—track his reactions, counter them, steer around obstacles, etc.—while being absent enough not to recognize it as planted. He sees it as intentional because it is: it's conscious and goal-oriented. And yet I cannot be *aware* of it. I can't sincerely hold against him evidence that I know I planted. Conscious but unaware—the limbo state: it's all right there to see, and I'm turned away from it.

In this way, it's not exactly "me" doing this. In the moment, it felt like me: like clear sight, even like righteousness. But if I could see that I was entrenching my enemy, could I really want it? No. So whose intention is it, exactly? 

The part of me that helps me get away with self-serving behavior, and that needs to know I'm nothing like the "bad people" who do exactly that: it acts as if it has goals of its own, and it pursues them *intentionally.* It is the part that craves punishing them, and for this very reason: it is how it keeps them trapped.

I desperately want to call this part of myself *unconscious* (if I can admit I have it at all), because otherwise what would it make me? Conscious misbehavior is the kind of thing only *bad people* do. In me it's always an accident; something I had "no idea" I was doing. In them, it's done with "full awareness." That's why I deserve understanding and they deserve contempt.

This part of me is working to widen that difference between us. And how does it do that? By *revealing* itself to them. Showing them:

> *Yeah, I can see it in you. And look, I have it too—and I can use mine against you and get away with it, because I'm a "good person."*

Turning away from my *better nature* produces a gap in my awareness—one that, by definition, I don't notice. In that gap, this aspect of my mind takes control. There, it produces conditions that I hate, but that help it hide itself better from me, and which convince me to give it more control. So the only thing that benefits is *it*.

It runs on the fear I started with, that I might be like them. Every enemy it helps me make gives that fear somewhere to point other than at me.

It's doing the same thing in him, and each of us is sure we're free of it. None of this lets me off the hook, though. It's my mind, and I answer for what it does. What this part of me is, and why it behaves the way it does, is the subject of Chapter 2.

If this part of me isn't me, then what is? Here's my claim: our better nature isn't one part among others. It's what we most fundamentally are. The evidence I have is this. When I hide what I'm doing, I say I'm hiding it from *myself*—and that's exactly what it feels like. But the only thing I would have to hide it from is the part that would object. I've hidden good things too, from the part of me that would call them weakness. But only one of those hidings has ever brought me remorse, the feeling of having gone against myself. And go back to the moment you finally owned up, in the memory from the start. Did it feel like losing something of yourself, or like getting something back?

---

I saw *genuine darkness* in my enemy, and my desire to stop him came from a good place. But my verdict that it's *what he is* did not. It required *turning away* from him—seeing him as something less. And as hard as I try to convince myself this is a good thing when directed at "bad people," my better nature will simply never fall for it. It is not something I can choose to override.

Not only can I not fool myself, but I can't fool him, either. The self-deception leaks out my eyes, and the harder I hide it, the more clearly he sees it.

As long as I have even a subtle need to be a *better kind of thing* than others, this trap is nearly unavoidable.

So what does it look like when someone fights evil without this need?

---

Think of a time when someone corrected you with integrity, without the slightest trace of contempt. Their anger was directed at what you did, and not what you are.

They might have been *more* fierce than I just was with my enemy. But did it make you dig in? Vow revenge?

For me, it has a very different effect. Even if they have different politics, religion, creed: something about their behavior feels deeply aligned.

It feels like it's reminding me of something—that *you're better than this*. The opposite of what I did to my enemy above.

It's true that contempt can sometimes stop people's misbehavior (at least, in public, and temporarily). But it isn't what does the stopping. It's a little darkness I add to something otherwise good, poisoning it: it turns a correction he could take into an attack he has to resist. Take it out and nothing is lost. The fierceness stays; only the poison goes.

---

## Common knowledge

This raises a curious question: how could the part of me that was provoking him be so sure of his reaction?

It had to know that he'd read my *turning away* as evil; that he'd see I knew he could read it (so he couldn't write it off as a slip); that he'd see I knew *that* (so he couldn't hope to embarrass me into stopping by pointing it out); and so on. Each level closes a way out that the one below leaves open, until his only move left is to protest, which I've already counted on. So for it to land as it did, we each had to know that the other knew that the other knew (etc.) what it meant.

Don't worry about tracing the logic. You may have felt it before, as a kind of hall of mirrors. You both know exactly what's happening (and that the other knows it, etc.), but also that it can't be named without sounding crazy. This is what you were sensing.

What allowed me to rely on such *common knowledge*?

Remember earlier I said that "at some level, I know this is how it works in others, too." Part of what I know about them is that they know this about me, because the faculty I'm seeing *with*—the one that reads our sameness—is the very thing I'm seeing *in* them. So, like two sets of eyes gazing into each other, each already sees that the other sees (etc.).

My claim is that even the worst person must turn away from a deep part of themselves to cause harm, just as I must, and that there's no "bad essence" underneath, driving it. Part of us already knows this. Here's a brief exercise that might help.

Have you ever been on the receiving end of a dehumanizing gaze? Can you feel the sense in which the other person feels absent or somehow "turned away" from himself? Why would he have to turn away, if what he is, is essentially bad? Is it possible that you look a little bit like this when treating others with contempt?

I'm not saying you're as bad as them. But ask yourself where that similarity might come from.

Think about what my enemy's eyes accused me of: *Don't pretend you're doing something noble here.* That's hypocrisy: doing to him the very thing I condemn him for. And hypocrisy only means something between two people who answer to the same thing.

We could not treat our enemies with contempt—and rely on it working—without it already being common knowledge between us that we're the same kind of thing. We don't normally call this knowledge "conscious," although it is. It exists in deep limbo. To turn away from them is to turn away from ourselves: from the part of us that knows this. That's the move they read in us, and that we rely on them reading.

Contempt is a violation of this common knowledge. It makes my enemy carry the knowledge that I *am* violating it, while I hide it from myself.

It works because its meaning is universal: he reads my *turning away* the way anyone would. My justifications, on the other hand, are believable only to me and my allies. I knew that too; it's how I could predict his reaction. So part of me already knows the difference between the "good" I've invented to excuse myself and the kind that everyone already agrees on. I just tell myself I don't, which helps him do the same.

I am speaking directly to the better nature I claim he doesn't have. And, in my need to prove myself right (and righteous), I am attempting to bury it under another layer of confusion.

Of *course* that's a painful thing to confront. No wonder I must hide it so carefully from myself. What I'm hiding isn't that I *could* do what they do, it's that I'm *already doing it*.

If seeing this makes you want to hide, notice that's the same verdict, pointed inward. It feels like accountability. It isn't. What I need in order to look is what my enemy needed from me, and what the corrector with integrity gave me: to be seen as more than what I do.

Believing I'm different and special is inherently self-deceptive. From the inside, it may not feel that way, but that's the whole point of self-deception. And yet it always leaks, producing the evidence it needs to sustain itself.

Here's my claim: the more free we are of self-deception in ourselves, the fewer people we see as inherently defective or less than us. Whether that number ever goes to zero is something you can discover for yourself.

---
## Other kinds of contempt

Most contempt is quieter still: a vague sense of *well, I could never do that*, or *I'm just a better kind of thing than them*. It isn't always moral, but it's always a sense of being more deserving. The stupid, the slow and the weak get the same verdict, with better cover: *I'm only being accurate.* It doesn't feel like contempt at all, and it can happen a thousand times a day without my noticing.

I send it to people I love. The *what's wrong with you?* that needs them to know they're broken. The sigh that tells them what I really think of them, dressed up to make me seem like the reasonable one. The look on their face that tells me it landed, which I pretend not to have seen, leaving them nothing to accuse me of. For one second the verdict flashes through my eyes, and I make sure they see it, and then I go back to loving them as if nothing had happened. It has the same flavor as what we call evil.

I don't only send it *to* people; I send it *about* them, to my allies, with nobody's face in view. Or online, on social media. That spreads it too. Everyone in the conversation learns that the verdict is safe to hold, and leaves a little more practiced at it.

---
## Self-serving

Most turning away isn't quite contempt. It's quieter: the mind angling, all day, for small advantages at the world's expense, and never letting us know it's doing so. It's the same turning away, at a low dose.

We see it easily in others: the private equity firm that buys nursing homes and calls the staff cuts efficiency, the politician who votes for the donor's bill to protect jobs. Each would sincerely say they're doing good, and I suspect each knows. It degrades the world through a causality that often no accounting shows, and yet one that part of us always knows.

When I'm sure someone knew exactly what they were doing, I've made them a villain. When I'm sure they had no idea, I've made them a machine. Either way, they're a different kind of thing from me. What I find hard to hold is what I found in my own memory: that they knew, and didn't let themselves know. And sometimes I'm simply wrong: what they did wasn't harm at all. I might even discover later that I only viewed it that way because doing so served me.

When my contempt is aimed at those people, and not just at what they did, my mind is doing the same thing: making the world darker for my own benefit, while telling itself a very convincing story of how what it's doing is good. This is why, if I want to end theirs, I have to see how to end mine.
