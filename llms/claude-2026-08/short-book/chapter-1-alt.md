# Chapter 1: Limbo

Contempt is the claim that you're a worse kind of thing than me. But it only works because you're the same kind of thing, and both of us know it. I want to show you where that contradiction is, and what it reveals: that evil is an absence that behaves like a presence, that believing otherwise reliably produces more of it, and that part of us has always known this.

Some of what follows I'll say before I've earned it. Hold it the way you'd hold a puzzle piece you haven't placed, and if it doesn't fit what you find in yourself, drop it.

---
## Limbo

Have you ever done something you knew was wrong at the time, but couldn't fully admit it to yourself until later? Maybe you took credit for someone else's work, said something cruel to someone who'd done nothing to you, or cheated on a test (or even a partner).

Recall what it was like to finally own up to it. The knowledge that it was wrong: did it feel like new information? Or like something you had always known, but somehow hadn't *let* yourself know?

We like to call such knowledge "unconscious," but it isn't. It was right there to see—fully available to consciousness—you were just *turned away* from it. It lived in a strange kind of limbo, in which you simultaneously knew and yet somehow didn't.

Any time someone came close to exposing it, or you started getting close to it yourself, did you find a way to deflect? Did that happen passively? Looking back, did some part of you know you were doing that, even as you did it?

Now try to recall how you knew it was wrong in the first place. Did that knowledge arrive before or after you'd reasoned it through? The part that knew might not have been able to say how it knew. Meanwhile, another part was busy providing reasons. *Everyone does it. It's not a big deal. Just this once.*

The part that knew may not have won the argument, but it never budged, either. It kept on quietly knowing what it knew.

Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?

Now imagine doing the same thing without any justifications. Could you have?

And the reverse. You know the feeling of remorse: that sickening sense of having gone against yourself. Have you ever felt it over something you did out of decency? Can you imagine even the worst person feeling it over that? When I've thought I had, it turned out to be something else: regret that it cost me, anger at being taken advantage of, guilt at breaking a rule I was raised on, or feeling foolish.

---
## The Two Strangers

Imagine that while you were in that state—doing wrong, justifying it, half-knowing—a stranger had caught you and publicly chastised you for it. Not just for doing something wrong, but for *being the kind of person who does*.

Does that make it *easier* or *harder* to confront what you already know?

For me, it's harder, because *who the hell is he to judge?* I can tell he's the kind of person who does this too—things he knows are wrong, while pretending they're not.

I'd take that bet at any odds. But on what evidence? I've never met him. I know nothing about him. All I have to go on is that he's standing there, doing what he's doing.

---

Now imagine a different stranger had caught you—but instead of treating you with contempt, he treated you with dignity. Not more gently. Just that his anger was directed at what you *did*, and not what you *are*.

Would you have dug in?

The problem wasn't being caught. In fact, he could have been *harsher* than the first person—said things that stung more, in front of more people, left you less room to wriggle out—and it still wouldn't have triggered the digging-in response.

It's like he was reminding you of something: that *you're better than this*. The opposite of what the first stranger was doing: claiming that *this is all you are*.

You might have disagreed with him on nearly everything—politics, religion, creed—and it wouldn't have mattered. Whatever he was standing up for wasn't *his*. It wasn't a rule, or a side. It was something you were already on the side of, and he was only reminding you.

What is that thing whose side you are both on; that two people who agree on nothing can both be serving?

---
## The Encounter

I've been the first stranger more times than I'd like to admit. I'll narrate what happens in my mind when I am. My target has just been cruel to someone who didn't deserve it. The threat is over, and now I'm calling him out.

I've decided he's a *bad person*—not just someone who does bad things, but who *is* bad. My eyes are saying: *I'm a better kind of thing than you.* And I want him to feel the full weight of that, almost as a kind of punishment.

Part of me senses something dark in the message. If I were on the receiving end, I'd recognize it instantly: *this is all you are* carries a threat: *and therefore what I do to you no longer qualifies as harm.* I would defy a message like that too, and violently.

But if I admitted that, it would undermine the verdict I feel strangely attached to: he's **bad**, dammit. So I push it out of mind. I'm only holding him to account, and if he reacts poorly, that only proves my point. It's not like I'm actually threatening him. If anything it's an *invitation*, to return to the realm of those who matter!

But there's nothing inviting about my tone or gaze. How could there be, when I'm trying to convince him that *this is all he is*?

On the other hand, he can't prove I'm doing this, can he? No. And *neither can I*. So if he accuses me of it, he can only be projecting.

And there he goes, accusing me of it. Not in words. His eyes say: *We both know what you're really up to.*

*Shit.* He's onto me. But I can't afford to admit it. I don't even want to believe I have that kind of darkness in me—the kind that would forsake a fellow being just to prove I'm nothing like him, and then cover it up.

So I remind myself that what I'm doing is good. Sure, it may be a bit dark, but it's the *good* kind of darkness—the kind that fights the real kind.

I double down: *this really **is** what you are.* Yet for some reason, that only makes him more defiant.

---

Part of me knew the whole time that I could have done what the second stranger did: served justice without injecting my own darkness. But fierce integrity and contempt can be made to look alike from the outside, so much so that when I look back later, even I won't be sure which one I did. And I'll have produced the result I needed: proof that my enemy is beyond help, because look how he reacted.

I wasn't *exposing* his darkness. I was creating more of it.

---

My enemy was probably feeling something like this:

> *What a hypocrite. If this is the kind of world I live in—where people can ignore their conscience and call it "righteous"—then why the hell shouldn't I? I'd be a chump.*

Think back to the wrong you remembered at the start. Did some part of you feel you *deserved* something? Or that everyone does it, and only a fool plays it straight?

Our justifications feel more *righteous* than our conscience, as if they *were* it. I'm overriding the part that knows (I'm causing harm) in favor of the part that can prove (I'm doing good). And it teaches him to make the same mistake: to demote his inner knowing, because what good is it in a world as rotten as this?

I sense his hardening, and feel a brief flicker of satisfaction, vindication, even relief at being proven right about him—just before I relabel it disgust or dismay:

> *Ugh, I knew it. Other people are the problem.*

Any doubt I had about him is now erased. Who cares about my own minor misdeeds when there's *real* evil out there to fight?

If I'd looked carefully, I'd have seen my role in causing it. So I didn't—and that *was* my role in causing it.

---

Bring to mind your worst enemy. Say you had two buttons in front of you. One delivers them the vengeance they so richly deserve. The other gives them a pill that instantly changes their heart. Imagine no one will ever know you were given this choice, let alone what you chose. Is some part of you itching to press that first button? I'll be honest: there is in me.

And think of the last time you watched someone you despise get what was coming to him. Was the pleasure only in the harm stopping, or in the punishment?

Something in me craves punishment, over and above any good it might do. I tell myself it's innocuous, just part of being human. It's not like I would ever act on it.

And that lie is exactly what lets me act on it, in ways I rarely recognize until later.

---
## The Corruption

Whatever your wrong was, it needed an exception: *this doesn't apply to me.* Contempt is the same move from the other end: *you're not really one of us.*

The reason I don't have to argue myself into doing the right thing is that in the absence of justifications, it's what I naturally desire. I call that deepest part of myself my *better nature*. It isn't following rules; it is what sees myself in others, and that's why I must *turn away* from it to cause harm. My excuses aren't there to convince *it*; they're there to convince *me* that it's one more voice among the noise, and safe to ignore. It isn't.

Part of what it sees in others is that they, too, can only cause harm by *turning away*. When I turn away from mine, other people look defective, as if they were just bad. But the perception of our similarity never truly disappears. It just goes into limbo.

If you've ever been on the receiving end of a dehumanizing gaze, could you feel the sense in which the other person is somehow absent or "turned away" from himself? I think that's what we're sensing. What are they turned away *from*? Why would they have to turn away from anything, if what they are is essentially bad?

Part of me refuses to believe I'm anything like the *bad people*. I need their behavior to come from something I don't have: some alien quality called "evil" that I could never hope to understand, only valiantly fight. But it doesn't feel like a need. It just feels like truth: I can literally *see* the darkness in their eyes, and I believe it's what they *are*. What I don't see is that I can only believe this while turned away myself. In this state, the need is in control.

My enemy sees I'm turned away, even though I don't. *Because* I don't. And he sees it as *evil*, for the same reason I see him that way. It shows him, through my tone and my gaze, that the darkness I see in him lives in me too, and that I can use it against him and be called a hero for it. And it doesn't look like an accident to him.

Notice how my stories kept changing, but my conclusion never did. When he saw through one, the next was ready, and when he called me on it I wasn't embarrassed or confused. I doubled down, in exactly the way needed to keep both conclusions standing: that he was bad, and that I was only seeing it. Something tracked his reactions and steered around them, in the same way yours did, if you deflected when someone came close. This was intentional, and done in limbo. I couldn't have held against him evidence I knew I'd planted; I was present enough to plant it, and absent enough not to recognize it as planted.

If you've been on his end, you know how it closes around you. Deny it, and you're the kind of person who does. Get angry, and that's proof too. Stay calm, and you don't even care. Apologize, and it's too convenient. Did it feel like an accident?

I've just nudged him one step closer to being the kind of person who can only be dealt with in *one way*—a way that part of me will then relish. I never realize that this is the very thing I'm revealing through my eyes, and that by doing so, I'm creating what I need.

I desperately want to call this behavior _unconscious_ (if I can admit I do it at all), because otherwise what would it make me? Evil behavior is unconscious in me, and fully aware in my enemies. That's why I deserve understanding, and they deserve contempt.

---

It's as if I'm saying to him:

> *I know exactly how you cause harm, because I'm doing it to you **right now**. But I'll get away with it—because I'm only "fighting evil," and how could that ever be wrong?*

He knows it's true, and I *know* he knows it, and so on—and he can't say so without sounding unhinged. That was his accusation: *we both know what you're really up to.* And it's all plausibly deniable, *even to myself.*

Even "it's the good kind of darkness" refutes itself. The good kind would have landed like the second stranger's correction, and left him a way back. Mine closed the door, and we both felt it close.

How could this trap work unless we both know we're the same kind of thing—beings who *turn away* to cause harm? My claim that I'm a *better kind of thing* is maddening precisely because we both know it's false. Without that claim, my turning away would just be malice, and malice frightens; it doesn't madden.

And look at what the sameness is. Not that we agree about what's good; we don't, and I don't even agree with my past self about it. It's this: I never think, *he turned from his better nature, good.* I don't check what his better nature would have done before I condemn the turn. And when his conscience seems to point at something terrible, I don't conclude that his better nature is bad; I conclude he's been fooled. So I already trust that whatever he'd do turned toward would be good, without knowing what it is, the same way I trust it in myself. His accusation of hypocrisy only stings because he's extending me that trust, and watching me betray it.

In contempt there is no separate deed. The look is the deed, and there is nothing else. And because what it means is already known to both of us, it needs no words. It can be carried entirely by gaze and tone, which have the added benefit of being unquotable.

In brief: I am in limbo about provoking him, and showing him that I am in limbo is itself the provocation. The signal is the pretense that there is no signal.

---

And notice where my signal lands. 

Recall the last time someone's contempt really got under your skin, and feel for *where* it was trying to reach. Not just your pride: an insult to your pride is easier to shrug off. This reaches further in, for the *you* that knows you count: the same you that you'd have to turn away from to harm someone, and the same you the second stranger was speaking to.

**Love and contempt are addressed to the same place.** One says *I know you're in there.* The other says *there's nobody in there*, and they both say it to the somebody who is.

I am speaking directly to the part of my enemy that I claim doesn't exist—his better nature—and trying to extinguish it.

What I've been hiding isn't that I *could* do what the "bad people" do. It's that I'm *already doing it*, and using them as a place to hide the fact that I am. And part of me has known it all along.

I've laid this all out as an argument, but for me it wasn't one. I saw it in myself the way you found your wrong in the opening: part of me was knowingly smothering the innocent part of my enemy, darkening his mind so that I'd never have to face my own. It was right there to see, but I was turned away from it.

In one sense, it felt utterly malevolent. In another sense, it was totally innocent. I had forgotten my better nature, and in that profoundly confused state, I felt forced to hide my shame in others.

As best I can tell, "pure evil" is this error turned up to maximum intensity. It looks intentional, just as mine did, because in an important sense, it is. It's right there for them to see, the way mine was for me and yours was for you, but they are turned away from it as far as a person can turn.

Confronting them from a turned-away state myself only makes it worse, and part of me has always known that, and used it. Who in their right mind would dissuade me from treating *evil people* with contempt?

---
## The Way Out

I've been calling it darkness, and I've been calling it the need to see myself as essentially different. They're one thing.

Take two things you checked. Could you have done your wrong without a story? And did remorse ever run the other way, over something you did out of decency? If your answers were mine, then something had to go out of view before the harm could go. Not out of sight: a torturer has his victim in plain sight. What goes is the face as a place somebody is looking out of. And nothing has to go out of view for decency. So what I turn from is the baseline, and the turning is a departure from it.

Badness is the result of turning away, not its cause.

Make it a cause, and I've recreated the problem one level deeper: shame in myself, contempt for others, and the cycle continues.

But that doesn't make it nothing. Call it nothing, and I'll underestimate it: I'll stop looking at exactly the moment it's crucial to look more closely. It tracks, it aims, it fights to stay hidden. That's the need from a few pages back: not a second thing in me, but what the absence does to keep itself in place. It's an absence that behaves like a presence. It has no substance of its own; everything it uses—my attention, my reasons, my eyes—is borrowed from me. When it's operating, it looks and behaves exactly like you'd expect a "bad essence" to look. That's what I looked like to my enemy.

The feeling that someone is evil to the core measures how far they've turned, not what they are. At the far end, what horrifies me is a conscience under crushing load, still showing through: a hostage held up to the window, not a monster on display. That doesn’t mean he wants out. To want out, he’d have to see the alternative to where he is, and from inside, he can’t. I think that’s what the second stranger gave you.

Now look at why I needed the essence. I don't do any of this because I'm a bad person. I do it because I believe there *are* bad people—people who are fundamentally defective—and I believe that, at least in part, because at a level I can't let myself see, I'm afraid of what I already know: that I'm doing what they do. Every enemy gives that fear somewhere to point other than at me. If you can find even one memory where that's plainly true of you, that's the one to look at.

When I'm sure someone was fully aware of what they were doing, I've made them a villain. When I'm sure they had no idea, I've made them a machine. Either way, they're a different kind of thing from me. What I find hard to hold is what I found in my own memory: that they knew, but didn't let themselves feel the weight of what they knew. Neither of us ever gets both views of the same moment, so each of us is accurate about the other and blind about ourselves.

The same thing is happening in him, and each of us is sure we're free of it. That's how believing in evil produces more of it.

Go back to the memory at the start, to the knowledge you hadn't *let yourself* know. Look at the grammar you used: you hid it from *yourself*. Not from a part of you; the part was the one doing the hiding. So what was it hidden from? When you finally owned up, did it feel like losing something of yourself, or like getting something back? And if you could keep only one of the two parts—the one that knew without arguing, or the one that justified—which would you keep?

What you hid it from is what you call "I" when nothing is being hidden. My better nature isn't one part among others; it's what's left when the hiding stops. That's why I call it *myself*: not because it's nicer, but because it's the one that's home. I'm not claiming there's a thing in there you could point to. Only that this is what the hiding was from, and what my contempt was aimed at in him.

Everything I've said turns on which of the two is home. If darkness were an essence, I'd be right to be afraid of it, and ashamed, and I'd have to keep proving it's in others and not in me. But if it's something I *do*, then I can stop.

When I feel a fundamental difference between me and my worst enemy, I ask which part of me needs there to be one.

---
## The Spread

The harm doesn't stop at him. It passes to the next stranger he won't give the benefit of the doubt, and to the kid who watches him do it. What gets passed along isn't my look; it's the turning away, and the permission it gives. And I send it *about* people, to my allies, with nobody's face in view. Everyone in the conversation learns that the verdict is safe to hold, and leaves a little more practiced at it.

What I narrated was the hot kind of contempt. It can also run cold. I just write the person off: a quiet filing-away, a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them. It's the same verdict, delivered by withdrawal, and it's meant to be felt: by them, or by whoever's watching. And it needn't be about right and wrong. Someone slow, ignorant, or clumsy can get the same verdict (*you're a lesser kind of thing*) and I write it off as "just being honest."

And it needn't be big. Watch what cutting in line does to you. The cost is thirty seconds. The heat is out of all proportion to thirty seconds, because what you're reacting to isn't the cost. It's the exception: somebody put you out of view to get ahead, and let you see it. The smallest morsel carries the whole, because there's nothing in it but the turn, and the turn has no size.

Most contempt is quiet: a vague sense of *well, I could never do that*. It doesn't feel like contempt at all, and it can happen a thousand times a day without my noticing. I send it to people I love. The *what's wrong with you?* that needs them to know they're broken. The sigh that tells them what I really think of them, dressed up to make me seem like the reasonable one. The look on their face that tells me it landed, which I pretend not to have seen. For one second the verdict flashes through my eyes, and I make sure they see it, and then I go back to loving them as if nothing had happened.

*That wasn't the real me* can be used as an excuse, but I also think it's true. I did it, and it's mine to answer for. But I could only do it by turning away from myself.

The subtlest contempt I know is the one I feel toward people who haven't seen through this trap yet. It can look like patience, or even care. Sometimes it looks like me writing this book *at* people, instead of *for* them.

If seeing this makes you want to hide, notice that's the same verdict pointed inward. It feels like accountability. It isn't. What I need in order to look is what my enemy needed from me, and what the second stranger gave: to be seen as more than what I do.

---

So what is it that two people who agree on nothing can both be serving? Not a definition of good. We may never agree on one. It's a direction. The part that knew without arguing was pointed in it, and so was the second stranger when he pointed you back. The part that proves can be pointed anywhere. Even when it's pointed away from goodness—disconnected from the part that knows—it can prove that it isn't.

Underneath the argument, my enemy and I were never fighting over whether a shared ground exists. We were fighting over which of us still stood on it. That's why the worst thing I can do for anyone is what I did to my enemy: teach them to forget it's there, by forgetting it myself. And the best thing I can do, for them and through them for the world, is remember it.
