So far I've asked you for glimpses: a stranger's eyes, a button, a memory of being caught. Now bring a whole case, if you're willing. It can be the person from the interlude, if you did more than think it. A time you treated someone with contempt and felt they'd earned it. Someone who'd really done something: the relative across the political divide, the colleague who took the credit, the ex. It doesn't need to have been loud. A look is enough, or a silence you made sure they noticed.

And before you file this under things we do to enemies: we also send it to people we love. The *what's wrong with you?* that needs them to know they're broken. The sigh that lets them know what we really think of them in that moment, dressed up to make us seem like the reasonable ones. The look on their face that tells us it landed, which we'll pretend not to have seen.

If no enemy comes to mind, think of a time you did this. For one second the same "something" flashed through your eyes, and you made sure they saw it, and then you went back to loving them as if nothing had happened. That will do as your case.

Keep it in mind through this chapter. I'll write "you" from here on, but every sentence is an offer. Hold it against your case and keep what fits. Wherever nothing fits, read it as being about me.

## How you sent it

Start with how it went out. Did it go where it would land: to them, at the moment they'd catch it? Could you have denied it afterwards? And did you know, on the way out, what they'd feel?

If you were fully unaware, you couldn’t aim it so precisely—making sure the verdict lands, anticipating their likely reaction, and preparing to use it against them. But if you were fully aware, you couldn’t sincerely mistake their reaction for evidence. It's the shape mine had: present enough to plant the evidence, absent enough not to recognize it as planted. Does your case have it?

Your target can see the state you're in even though, in an important sense, you don't. Because you don't. You aren't hiding the intention and then letting it slip. What you're showing them *is* that you're hiding it.

We all know what it is, while pretending not to, and that is why it works.

It enables a second conversation beneath the spoken one, which is why gaze and tone are enough to carry it—while leaving no evidence behind, even in the sender's own mind. In my case, his half of that conversation was in his eyes: *we both know what you're actually doing.* The sender's half says:

> *We both know what's really going on here, and that neither one of us will be able to prove it—meaning you'll have no recourse, and I'll have nothing to feel guilty about.*

## What it is

Limbo aimed at a person *is* malice. Malice that needs to be seen, and needs to deny itself, is contempt. And the darkness is what both are made of: the turning away, whether or not anyone is in front of you.

I will use another word for what I've been calling "darkness": evil.

That word may conjure serial killers and death camps. We usually reserve it for the people we've given up on, or for acts too big to think about. But what those acts are made of is what I sent through my eyes. The reason I'm using it is so that we never confuse what we're talking about. So that I never again believe that my mistake is a different kind of thing than what my enemies do. It may be a milder form of it, but it's driving toward the same end.

Check it against the cases you'd already use that word for. The murderer: he had to be turned away from his victim. The executive who signs off on the part he knows will fail. The official whose decision will cost lives but get him re-elected. It's one thing in all of them: real people, kept out of view, so that the harm doesn't register as harm. (By *out of view* I never mean out of sight. A torturer has his victim in plain sight. I mean not seen the way you saw the stranger at the start: as someone whose wellbeing matters.)

Malice, indifference, negligence, and contempt are different forms of the same move. Malice is the one that presents itself as dark. Contempt is the one that's also aimed, and shows, and *needs to know it's received* (while also pretending not to).

What we are revealing to our enemies is the real thing—the thing we all hate, and the source of all hatred—and the horror it evokes is accurate. We reveal it clearly enough to make sure it lands, but subtly enough to give ourselves plausible deniability. Even the tiniest flash, sent clearly, evokes the full sordid thing. **That** is why it is so effective. There is no "good kind." That is the great lie it tells us to sneak into our minds.

## What you knew about them

Now notice what this means for them. You're holding them in the very state that all harm comes from, showing them that you're doing it, and relishing their reaction. Which means you know how a basically decent person (you) can be evil: not through some rotten essence, but simply by hiding from yourself that you are.

I was working hard to keep him there, which only makes sense if part of me thought he could leave. Is that true in the case you picked? If it is: what did part of you believe about them, to need to work that hard?

In the cold form it looks different, because I'm not holding him anywhere. I'm leaving. But look at how I leave. I make sure he knows he's been written off: the correct tone, the politeness with nothing behind it. If he were only what I say he is, there would be nobody there to tell.

And here is the great irony of contempt: **we only treat people as irredeemable because part of us knows they're not**. We're addressing the part of them we claim doesn't exist, because that's the only part of them that can recognize the horror of what we're doing: smothering it, in an attempt to make them match our confused perception.

And here's what makes that recognition even harder to allow into consciousness. The more I saw it, the more I saw that *I had always known it*. If this book works, it won't be by giving you new information. It will be by exercises that help you check what you already know. And the more I saw, the more I saw that I wasn't just keeping a "redeemable person" (in the abstract) trapped: I was cutting off access to the one part of themselves that could have saved them.

I said that realizing this would be devastating. It was. The first time I recognized it in myself, it was like a punch to the gut. I saw that I'd known it, full well, even as I did it. I had just been *turned away* from it.

And here's the harder part to admit: despite being able to write a whole book about it, there are times I still fall into it.

---

Here's the saving grace, and it's a big one: the reason we have to hide it from ourselves is that we *couldn't do it if we saw it clearly for what it was.* Check that in your own case. It's a hint about your true nature.

The more clearly we see it in ourselves, the more we allow ourselves to see it in others. When we say that your enemy was "redeemable," we mean the same is true of them. The darkness you (correctly) identified in them was a *turning away*. Ask yourself: why would they have to turn away, unless a deeper part of them would otherwise not have allowed it?

And here's what makes it so difficult to catch: the thing we're turned away *from* is also the thing that warns us when we *are* turned away from it. That's why we so rarely recognize when we're in it. Seeing it clearly would, by necessity, destroy it.

## What is doing this

There's a strange thing here. The part of us doing this—it acts in a way that's intentional, goal-driven, evasive, and self-protecting, and yet it doesn't seem to be *us* in the usual sense, since we aren't fully aware of it while it operates. So what, exactly, is it?

I've been calling it *the darkness*. It's not a literal metaphysical entity, but in many ways behaves like one. It is the thing you revealed to them (or that revealed itself) in your message. You can think of it as what "takes control" of your mind when you are *turned away.*

The darkness acts *through* each of us, and it can only do so by convincing us that it's not—that we're the ones in control, and what we're doing is okay or even righteous. It uses each person's unique psychology to find a way in, and the way it commandeers your mind and an "evil person's" differ only in the details. (Of course, when a mind has turned far enough away, turning it back may be a different kind of work, and it's fair to draw a conventional distinction. But it doesn't change what the person is made of.)

You don't have to believe any of that as a fact. Not that the darkness is a thing, not that "bad people" are under its control, not that they don't want what they're doing. You only have to notice what happens when you believe the alternative: that they are equivalent to their darkness, or that they want it. You can't help but hand control to your own. That is the sense in which the picture is useful. It's a stance.

Look at how it got in this time. It didn't ask you to embrace darkness, because nobody falls for that. It offered you a *good kind* of darkness, and the good kind earned its name by what it accomplished: the verdict landed. Maybe it even scared them into pausing their misbehavior, or at least the part of it that could be caught. The longer-term harm was there to know too, and that's the part that went into limbo. That's the trick, and I've never seen it work any other way. Never *be evil.* Always *mine is different.*

In other words, the darkness wins by masquerading *as* the light.

None of that lets us off. We are always **fully responsible** for the harm we cause while under its sway. We want to write it off as "unconscious," but that's not true. There's a paradox here that makes it difficult to hold.

Notice what happens when we call it *purely conscious.* In our enemies, it gives us reason to feel contempt—and we know where that leads. In ourselves, it produces shame. In both cases, that makes it harder to confront, which only gives it more power by keeping it in the dark.

But if we call it purely unconscious, we run into the opposite problem. There's no accountability in ourselves. And we're *nice* to the perpetrators, when what we should be is *kind*—fiercely kind, in a way that stops the harm without cutting off their potential to return to goodness.

Both views are incomplete, and our inability to hold this paradox is what enables it to continue. The same challenge confronts us when we're forced to choose between calling it "us" and "not-us." Both are true, in their own way.

Notice, too, that we never get both views of one instance. Our own turning away is always the kind we didn't see; theirs is always the kind they chose. Same act, opposite interpretations.

## Where it goes

And look what "it" was doing while in control. When you make your enemies dig in, does the harm end with them? No. It ripples out, with no clean boundary.

Our contempt makes the *world as a whole* darker. Every time it's shown to someone and lands, it's easier for them to do, and for everyone they do it to, whatever unique form theirs might take. That's how the thing behind the atrocities is fed by a sigh.

There's another way of looking at the message you sent: you're trusting the part of you that can *prove* (that your behavior is good) over the part that *knows* (that it's wrong). You know *they* know you know it, and that they cannot prove it. And this pressures them to demote the part of them that knows—their conscience—in favor of the part that can prove. It feels like this:

> *If even the so-called "good people" will so easily embrace the darkness when they can get away with it, then why the fuck shouldn't I?*

All of this was the simplest case: two people, and a look. Most contempt also has an audience (the room, the feed, the people on the email thread), and they receive it too. And it holds even when nobody's face is involved. Take the executive from before, who never sees the people his decision hurts. Your contempt reaches him, if it reaches him at all, as *these people are unhinged*, or *hypocrites*, and he goes back to his spreadsheet a little more sure that nobody shouting at him is worth hearing. And part of us *needs* that reaction, because it makes our judgement of him feel more justified. That need is the only part to drop. The outrage at the harm stays, and so does the fight to stop it. And the only way to drop it is to stop seeing the coldness as what he *is*, which requires seeing that it isn't what we are, either.

## Why

None of this should be new, if the account so far holds. For the message to work at all, you'd need to already know that your enemies aren't identical with their darkness, and that treating them as if they were only shoves them deeper into its grip, even as you believe you're doing the opposite. See whether that matches what you find.

My darkness needs enemies so that I never look in here. Here is what that was like from the inside. Imagine what it would be like to know, with complete certainty, that even the worst people aren't fundamentally different from you. That the way you've been treating them has been making the world a darker place—and that you knew it even as you were doing it, but convinced yourself you didn't.

It would mean that the darkness behind the atrocities you read about isn't disconnected from your own behavior. You are somehow a part of it—of "what's wrong with the world."

For me, that knowledge was too horrifying to let into full awareness, and I don't think I'm unusual in that; some people find tiredness there instead, or grief, and the shape is the same. I needed to know that no, some people really are just fundamentally different. And that drove me to behave in ways that "proved" it beyond any doubt, by turning them into what I needed them to be.

*That* is the message I was actually sending to "evil people" through my cruelty:

> *You will be humanity's sacrifice. The dumping ground for our collective shadow. That way, we never have to face what is inside us.*

It was all there to see, when I finally looked. That it wasn't the "good kind" of darkness, because there's no such thing. That it would raise the net animosity in the world. And that this would give my own darkness more cover.

By turning away from my better nature, I turned away from theirs, and with it from everything my behavior would create. It's *all a single move.* The darkening of the world was implicit in it. Stated flatly: contempt is how the darkness in our mind leaks out, to create a darker world in which ours may hide itself better from us.

If this is horrifying, it may be because **you don’t actually want to be doing it.** The horror is a hint that the darkness is not your nature. And if it isn't yours, it's hard to see how it could be anyone else's.

For me it was a tremendous relief when that sank in, the kind you feel on putting down something heavy you'd forgotten you were carrying. Sometimes it's even funny: there's the little prosecutor in there with his files, so sure of himself.

None of it was a choice we make with our eyes open. As long as we aren't fully aware of our own nature, we have to locate the darkness in someone else, and our eyes will show it, and the rest follows. It rarely feels like suspecting you might be evil; it feels like being sure about them. So this isn't a verdict on you, or on me. It's the not-knowing that must go.

And that gives a clue about what it means to fight evil with compassion. It doesn't mean being nice, or soft, or lenient. It means being fierce without giving an inch to the darkness, theirs or your own. As far as I have ever seen, it is the only thing that works, and it is only possible to the degree that we've uprooted this confusion from our own minds.
