## Margaret (retired church organist; Episcopalian, the Jesus Prayer; low analytic)

Dear Aditya,

**Steady, and in places better than steady.** I read Chapter 1 in one sitting at the kitchen table and didn't want to stop. Chapter 4 held me too. My attention went slack in four places: the long common-knowledge paragraph in "The Mechanism"; the big "life" paragraph in Chapter 2; the middle of Chapter 3, where the parasite gets its goal and its drawbacks and its Mahayana note one after another; and the Epilogue from "Ideas that live in our collective shadow" onward. I'd have put the book down at Appendix 1, Section 2. I'm not one of the lawyers, and I knew it by the second paragraph.

First, what I did with the exercises, because that is where my verdict comes from.

**The wrong in limbo.** When our new rector broke up the choir, I told two of the altos what he'd said to me in his office. I made it a little sharper than he'd made it. I knew I was doing it while I did it. When I owned up years later, it wasn't news. It was exactly what you describe, something I'd always known and hadn't let myself know. And yes, the justifications came first and the knowing just sat there: *he's done worse, they deserve to know what he's like.*

**Remorse in reverse.** No. The one time I felt sick after a kindness was lending money to my brother-in-law. When I looked, it was feeling foolish, just as you said.

**The buttons.** The rector. Yes, there was an itch for the first button, a short one, and I was ashamed of it. With my late husband's mother, who held me in contempt for forty years, I was surprised. No itch. Just tiredness.

**The face.** It came easily with the girl at the checkout. With my mother-in-law, the story was there before the face, exactly as you put it: *she never thought I was good enough for Walter.* I've said the Jesus Prayer for twenty years, and I've never caught myself turning away that fast.

**Relief or robbed.** This was the most useful page in the book for me. If the rector had simply retired with nobody the wiser, would I feel relief or robbed? Robbed. I sat with that for a long while. That page alone is worth sending.

**The far end.** I tried it on an ordinary afternoon. I didn't get far. Something stopped me almost at once, and when I looked at what was stopping me, it felt like the prayer itself. I didn't push.

## CONTRADICTIONS

1. **"The light" means two things, and then a third.**
   Chapter 2: "That care is what I'll call the light: what I turn from to cause harm, and what anyone is turned toward when they aren't, in themselves and in everyone else. It's all the word will mean."
   Chapter 4: "That's what I've been calling the light: the thing that, when someone is aimed at it, you know it, and when they're aimed away from it, you know that too, and you can't define either."
   In Chapter 2 the light is the care itself. In Chapter 4 it's what the care is aimed at. Then the Epilogue: "There was only a pure singularity: blinding white Light; infinite Love." The Epilogue is marked, and the capital letter says it's different. But Chapter 2 promised "It's all the word will mean," and that promise isn't kept. Either soften the promise or make Chapter 4 say the same thing as Chapter 2.

2. **One awareness, or not?**
   Appendix 1, "What I think is really happening": "Not that there's one awareness."
   Epilogue: "So the reason I believe other people are also me is that I saw it clearly once".
   The Epilogue's "not one and not two" parenthesis is trying to square this, and a Buddhist friend may find it squares. To me they read as two different beliefs, both labeled as yours. Since Appendix 1 comes after the Epilogue, the last word is the cautious one, and it reads like a retraction.

3. **Is the parasite a thing?**
   Chapter 3: "That's all a habit is, and it doesn't need a tenant."
   The Picture I Use: "It is a thing with a bottomless appetite."
   The Picture is marked, so maybe this is allowed. But it's the same creature, and "doesn't need a tenant" against "a thing" is the kind of seam a skeptic will put a finger in.

4. **Who gains.**
   Chapter 3: "Look at the trade it offered: more self-serving behavior for me, in exchange for a darker world." Then, two paragraphs on: "And it was the only one who gained. Not me; I was getting darker." Both may be true in different senses, but read in one sitting they argue.

5. **Choice.** I can't tell whether these agree.
   Chapter 3: "There is one choice, and it's real."
   Chapter 4: "You don't get to choose whether the contempt arises."
   I think you mean the choice comes before the contempt and not after. If so, Chapter 4 should say so in a few words, because a reader who skimmed Chapter 3's paragraph (as I nearly did) will think you changed your mind.

With gratitude, and with my own feet, I hope, held to the same fire,

> There is one choice, and it's real. While you can still feel both pulls—the recognition, and the want the recognition would veto—you're still looking, and you can keep looking or stop.

I can reconcile these: the choice is real but never made under the description "stop seeing him," and you say so ("It came labeled *I've had a long day*"). But the reconciliation comes after the collision, and most readers will feel only the collision. One sentence at the join, something like "The choice is real; it's just never offered to you under that name," would do it.

**b. Effortless and harder.** Chapter 3, the far end:

> The hiding has become so practiced that it no longer takes effort, which is what makes it complete.

and three sentences later:

> The most lost person is carrying *more* of that knowledge than you are, not less, and holding it down harder, with more of themselves committed to the holding.

"No effort" and "harder" can both be true if "harder" means "more completely," but as written they pull against each other.

**c. Is every turn aimed?** Appendix 1, footnote to O1:

> When you hold a person out of view, it's in order to do something their being in view would stop, so the holding-out and the permission are one event seen from two sides.

Appendix 1, Section 1:

> And not every turn is aimed.

and "What is not claimed":

> Not that turning away is sufficient for harm; it also produces numbness and paralysis with nobody harmed.

"Turning away" is doing two jobs: the aimed turn that licenses harm, and any keeping-out-of-view, including numbness and grief. Chapter 2's "It's the turn itself" then calls both evil. Pick one word for each, or say in Chapter 2 which turn is meant.

**d. "Harm" in two senses.** Appendix 1, point 2:

> Harm here means more than damage, which can be an accident: hurting someone because part of me wants them hurt, for my own sake or for its own.

Chapter 4:

> If the choice really were between stopping a harm with contempt and letting it happen, stop it.

The Chapter 4 "harm" is damage. Under the Appendix's definition, "turning away is the only way to harm" comes close to true by definition: wanting someone hurt for its own sake is already being turned from him. That's fine if you say so. As it stands the slogan borrows its force from the ordinary sense and its safety from the narrow one.

**e. Does the most evil person think he's doing good?** Chapter 3:

> Even the most evil person believes that what he's doing is "good" or "right."

Appendix 1:

> the honest villain says *yes, I mean you harm, and no, you're not my kind.*

If honest malice exists (and the Appendix needs it to, to set contempt apart), then some evil people don't believe what they're doing is good. "Most people doing evil" would fix Chapter 3.

**f. The grade of the claim that care is what awareness is.** The Introduction promises to check it; Chapter 2 concludes it with "So"; Appendix 1 says the argument doesn't reach it and puts it under "What I think is really happening." The quotes are in section 2. The Appendix has it right.

## 5. Questions I did have

- **What counts as "in view"?** It arose at Chapter 2: "Out of view doesn't mean out of sight. A torturer has his victim in plain sight, and the cruelest watch the face closely." It's answered only in Appendix 3, which is optional. Too late, and in the wrong place.
- **Why "I knew it more"?** It arose at Chapter 3: "Was that because you knew the big one *less*? For me it was the reverse. I knew it more, and had to hide it correspondingly harder." My answer was that I had more at stake, not more knowledge. The far-end extrapolation rests on your reading, and the book never says why mine is wrong. A sentence on stakes versus knowledge would help.
- **The born-this-way case.** It arose at Chapter 3's parenthesis. See section 2.
- **What "fully responsible" means.** It arose at Chapter 1: "Innocent at the root, and fully responsible, the way I was." Chapter 3 answers it: "It lives in how fast you catch the blindness, in what you repair when sight returns, and in what you've done to give the blindness less room next time." That's a good answer, and a revisionary one. It's a tracing account, and "fully" is doing work it can't quite do if responsibility lives in catch-speed. I'd drop "fully" in Chapter 3, or say what it adds.
- **Can contempt ever be fitting?** It arose at Chapter 2's "**to wield contempt, we have to already know that it's evil, and that its target is redeemable.**" That is your argument against it, and a strong one. It is never set beside the view that contempt is the fitting response to settled vice. That's Bell's view, and it's the one a philosopher will bring. A sentence in Appendix 1's rivals would do.
- **Wrongs done without knowing.** It arose at the very first question: "Have you ever done something you knew was wrong at the time." What about wrongs I didn't know were wrong? Chapter 4 answers it: "A person fully turned toward others can still be wrong about everything else, and make a mess." Late but adequate.

## 7. Would I give it to someone I care about?

Chapters 1 and 4, today, to anyone. I'd give it to my sister, who has been carrying a verdict on our father for twenty years, and I'd tell her to skip the Epilogue for now. The whole book as it stands, no. These three changes must come first:

1. **The Epilogue's conspiracy and manifestation paragraphs.** Cut the clause with "whispered hints and clues" and "Satanic" in it. Add one sentence ruling out the reading that a victim's suffering is the physical shape of their own hidden wrong. Everything else in the Epilogue can be taken with the grain of salt you offer; those two can do harm.
2. **The definitional knot.** The claim that care is what awareness is should carry the same grade everywhere: in the Introduction's promise, in Chapter 2's "So" before "its own nature," and in Appendix 1's "Where this stops." Say which "turning away" is evil, the aimed one, so grief and numbness aren't swept in. Put the three marks of "fully seeing" where O1 and the torturer paragraph need them. Fix the "most love" reference. A philosopher will find all four in an afternoon, and they cost trust out of proportion to their size.
3. **Cut Chapters 2 and 3 by the paragraphs listed in section 6.** In Chapter 2: "Contempt aimed at someone works only if…" (shrink to the bold line), "The darkness presents itself…", and "This is not a call to be nice." In Chapter 3: the goal paragraph (shrink), the first "genuine goodness is a myth," "When we gather with allies," and "This is why the only way out I've found." That's roughly a page, and none of it is a seed. The chapters would move at Chapter 1's pace, and Chapter 1's pace is the book's best argument.

The book worked on me. I found I'd been using it. That's rarer than agreeing with it, and it's why the three changes are worth making.
