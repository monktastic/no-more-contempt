## Tomasz (47, philosopher of action and self-deception; runs cold) — chapter, then Appendix 1

### Stage 1

Dear Aditya,

I read the chapter twice. I did the exercises on the first pass and took notes on the second. I'll go in order and tell you where I bought, where I bought on credit, and where I stopped. You should know my trade going in. I teach self-deception for a living (Fingarette, Mele, Sartre, Davidson), so parts of this I've lectured on. That made some of it feel familiar. It did not make all of it familiar, and the parts that weren't are what I want to tell you about.

**The opening.** "Contempt is the claim that you're a different kind of thing than me, but for it to land, you must be the same kind of thing." I bought this on credit. It has the shape of a performative contradiction, and I've watched students fall for that shape many times. My first question was "same kind of thing in what respect?" I held on to that question through the whole chapter, and it came back at the end.

I liked "Maybe you'll recognize something of yourself in it. Or maybe not." I liked it more once I got to "If that doesn't match anything in your memory, the rest of this won't make sense, and I'd rather you know that now." That's a man telling me where his argument can fail. I trust that more than almost anything else a writer can do.

**Limbo.** Yes, I found it, and fast. In 2016 I wrote a paper on Mele's "twisted" cases. The central distinction came out of a seminar conversation with a graduate student, Priya Menon. I thanked her in a footnote "for helpful conversation." I remember drafting that footnote three times, adjusting it until it sounded generous and said nothing. Two years later I owned up in therapy, and then in an email to her. Was the knowledge new information? No, not remotely. The three drafts are the evidence. You don't tune a sentence three times unless something in you knows what it is for.

So "that knowledge was never unconscious. It was right there to see, fully available to consciousness, but you were *turned away* from it." I bought that. It's roughly Sartre against Freud, and roughly Fingarette's "declining to spell out." You found it with a memory rather than a theory, and that's the right way in. One flag, which grows later: you're already using "consciousness" as a term that carries a thesis. I let it pass here, because my own case fits.

"Looking back, did some part of you know you were doing that, even as you did it?" The answer is built into the question, and the "part" does a lot of work. Mele would say motivated bias gets you the footnote with no part knowing anything. But in my case the honest answer is yes, so I didn't resent it much.

"Why did one side have to argue, and the other not? And which side did you listen to?" This one has its answer built in too. Still, it's true of my footnote. The knowing side never argued. It just sat there, and I wrote around it.

**The cashier.** Here's what actually happened. I pictured the woman at the Kroger on Lane Avenue on Monday. Her name tag says Denise, and she's in her sixties with reading glasses on a chain. I tried the sneer. It didn't go, but not for the reason you want. An imagined sneer at an imagined person is acting, and acting doesn't need a reason. It felt like a rehearsal, not a cruelty. To make it feel like a real sneer I had to give her a fault: she'd been slow, or she'd rung up the pears twice. So a reason did have to arrive. But I supplied it because your question asked for one, and I can't separate that from demand.

Then something else happened, and I'd rather tell you than hide it. I tried *my* kind, the cold one: the polite, flat face with nothing behind it. It went at once. No reason needed. It's how I look at most strangers. So your check tests hot contempt, and I run cold, and the cold version passed through with no reason at all. That result is either a counterexample or evidence that my reason was installed so long ago it no longer has to arrive. I don't know which. I'd like to know which you think.

**The first refusal.** "Notice an asymmetry: to be helpful or decent, there's nothing I must hide from some "bad part" of myself. I don't think that's true of anyone." I refused this, and I have a case. My daughter Ania is nineteen. She left a philosophy major to start a lash-extension business. Last spring I sent her $1,200 for the chair and the lamp, and in the Venmo note I wrote "rent." I had to hide a decent act from the part of me that holds her choice in contempt. That part would have called the money capitulation, so I disguised the gift from it. That is decency hidden from a bad part. You may have a reply, maybe that the lie served my pride and not my kindness. But on the face of it, the asymmetry fails in my case.

I could follow everything after that. I just noted that I'd refused a premise, and waited to see how much you'd hang on it. You hang a lot on it later.

**The Encounter.** Believable. The hot version isn't me, but the logic of the rationalizations is exact. "When you look at it that way, it's really more like an *invitation*" is the kind of thing I catch in myself on the stairs after a meeting. The best line in the chapter, for someone in my field, is this: "He can't prove I'm doing this, can he? No. And crucially, *neither can I*. So I know it's not on purpose." I'd put that on a syllabus. It turns the absence of self-evidence into self-exoneration, and that's the move I spend a semester trying to get students to see.

I skimmed the italicised eye-messages and "*Shit.* He senses it." That's not because they're false. By then I'd got the rhythm and was reading ahead of you.

**The cold paragraph.** This is where the chapter stopped being about a hypothetical man. "a quiet filing-away, a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them." That's me with Gary Holcomb, who wrote the external letter that almost sank my tenure case and who now chairs our curriculum committee. I am scrupulously collegial to him. I've been proud of how collegial. Reading that sentence, the pride turned into something I'd rather not name.

"It's the same verdict, delivered by withdrawal, and they feel it just the same." My first reaction was that this is a claim about other minds with no evidence. Then I remembered that Ania told her mother I "look at her like a bad argument." So I have the evidence, and it came from the person I'd least like it from.

**The Corruption.** "If I'd looked carefully, I'd have seen my role in causing it. So I didn't look—and that refusal is exactly what caused it." I stumbled here. The refusal to look comes after the hardening, so how does it cause it? I think you mean the refusal was ongoing throughout. As written, the time order confused me.

"So: was it an accident, or was my turning away motivated?" This is a question with its answer on the next line, and I felt handled. It's minor.

The two checks. Satisfying to see evil violently punished? Yes, obviously. The pill: my enemy is Gary. I reached for the pill, and I was pleased with myself for about four seconds. Then I noticed why I wanted it. An enlightened Gary would reread his letter about me and suffer. I'd chosen the pill as vengeance. Your two buttons aren't cleanly separable, at least in me. That may be the most useful thing the chapter did to me, and it did it by the check failing as designed. "I'll be honest: there is in me." Good. That line marks the confession as yours, and it made me willing to make mine.

"I tell myself it's innocuous, as long as I don't act on it. And that is exactly how I act on it, without seeing that I am." Yes. My collegiality toward Gary is the not-acting-on-it, and I'm fairly sure it's also the acting-on-it.

**"I'll go ahead and name what's happening."** Insight, not lecture. This section held the one thing in the chapter that was new to me. The literature I teach treats self-deception as a private defensive state. What protects the deceiver is that nobody, himself included, can see it. You say the opposite: "Normally, having my enemy see my self-deception would be horrible. In this case, it is ideal—because it *is* my provocation." Self-deception as a *communicative* act, where its being visible is the weapon. Sartre's Look gets near this, and Mele never considers the audience at all. I want to think about it for a month.

"That combination is both enough to produce the outcome I need, and also necessary." I read it as a philosopher's "necessary and sufficient" with "enough" swapped in for "sufficient." So it read as plain English to me, but I'd bet it reads as jargon to anyone who hasn't taken logic. The argument for necessity is only two alternatives, accidental or overt, which is thin. But the two you give are the right two, and I bought it.

"In short: I am in limbo about provoking him, and showing him that I *am* in limbo (which is something I *do* from limbo) is itself the provocation." On first read this confused me, and the parenthesis was the reason. On the second read it meant something. It's the recursive point: the display is itself a limbo act, not a deliberate one, which is why it can't be audited. Without the parenthesis I'd have got it on the first pass. With it, I wasn't sure whether "from limbo" was a place, a manner, or a cause.

I skimmed "He sees that he cannot appeal to my better nature, because I'm convinced this *is* my better nature—and that's a condition that part of me knows he will recognize as the hallmark of evil." That's four minds' worth of attributions in one sentence, and I lost count.

"He's watching me reject the part of me that knows (I'm causing harm) in favor of the one that can prove (I'm doing good)." The knowing/proving distinction is good. It's close to Murdoch's point that moral vision isn't a matter of argument, and I'd like to see it kept.

**Conscious but unaware.** This is my second refusal, and the more serious one. "He sees it as intentional because it is: it's conscious and goal-oriented. And yet I cannot be *aware* of it." Then: "Conscious but unaware—the limbo state." You're now using "conscious" and "aware" as two different things, and you haven't said what either one means. In most of what I read and teach they're near-synonyms. The inference "goal-oriented, therefore conscious" is exactly the one I refuse. Goal-directed behaviour with no consciousness is the default in the literature, and it's how Mele explains cases like mine. "because it is" asserts where I wanted an argument.

Then you partly redeemed it: "Conscious misbehavior is the kind of thing only *bad people* do. In me it's always an accident; something I had "no idea" I was doing. In them, it's done with "full awareness."" That's a genuinely new point for me. The choice between "conscious" and "unconscious" is itself distributed self-servingly: unconscious for me, conscious for them. That told me *why* you insist on "conscious." It's an ethical refusal of an escape hatch, not a terminological whim. I respect the motive. But a motive for a word isn't a definition of it. As it stands, a careful reader can say you win by redefining "conscious" to include what everyone else calls unconscious goal-pursuit. I'd like to be able to answer that reader, and I can't yet.

"But if I could see that I was entrenching my enemy, could I really want it? No. So whose intention is it, exactly?" This one answers itself, with a "No." There's a real position behind it: what I wouldn't endorse on full information isn't mine (Frankfurt, idealized-desire views). It is a position, though, and you present it as if it were an observation.

"The only thing that benefits is *it*." Here I got uneasy about the reification. "It acts as if it has goals of its own" was honest; the "as if" was doing its job. By the next paragraph the "as if" is gone.

**Better nature.** "Here's my claim: our better nature isn't one part among others. It's what we most fundamentally are." Granted as your claim. Not found in myself. And yes, it sounded grandiose, or at least metaphysical, in a chapter that had so far been phenomenological.

Your evidence has two parts. First: "If the darkness in me were me too, why wouldn't I have to hide my good behavior from it?" That rests on the asymmetry I'd already refused, and I did have to hide my good behaviour from something, with the Venmo note. There's also a structural problem. When I hide something from myself, the one hiding it and the one it's hidden from are both "myself." You take the second to be the real self. Why not the first? The hiding points both ways.

Second: "Did it feel like losing something of yourself, or like getting something back?" That did better. With Priya it felt like getting something back. It also felt like losing something: my picture of myself as scrupulous. I'd guess you'd say that picture was never me, and I might agree. So the question moved me from "refused" to "granted, with some evidence I can weigh." It's phenomenological evidence, not proof, and you're right not to call it proof.

Nothing later changed my answer much. "my better nature will simply never fall for it. It is not something I can choose to override." made it worse, because that's a universal claim with nothing but your own case behind it.

**The corrector.** My dissertation advisor, Marjorie Lusk, once handed back a chapter and said, "This is cowardly. You're better than it." I didn't dig in. I rewrote it, and I've loved her since. You write "*you're better than this*," almost her words, and it stopped me. So this lands, and it's the best evidence in the chapter for the better-nature claim. It's better than the argument you give for it.

"Take it out and nothing is lost. The fierceness stays; only the poison goes." "Nothing is lost" is an empirical claim. Contempt does social work, status and deterrence among others. You may be right that it's net poison, but "nothing" is more than you've shown.

**Common knowledge.** I teach Lewis's *Convention*, so I read this closely. "Each level closes a way out that the one below leaves open, until his only move left is to protest, which I've already counted on." That's a nice use of the hierarchy. In Lewis the higher levels mostly aren't doing separate work; you've given each level a job. I followed it.

"Don't worry about tracing the logic." I bristled. I wanted to trace it, and the passage is too quick for me to.

The basis you give for the common knowledge: "because the faculty I'm seeing *with*—the one that reads our sameness—is the very thing I'm seeing *in* them." This is clever. For Lewis, common knowledge needs a shared basis, and you make the basis the shared nature itself, like two mirrors facing each other. But it's a clever move presented as a proof, and that's the kind I watch for.

This is where my opening question comes back. "We could not treat our enemies with contempt—and rely on it working—without it already being common knowledge between us that we're the same kind of thing." Same kind in what respect? For contempt to land, we need to share a capacity: to read a look and to be wounded by it. Contempt denies that we share a *moral* kind. Those aren't the same "kind," so there's no contradiction unless you show they are. Your whole central claim needs "same capacity to read and be hurt" to amount to "same fundamental nature." That's the step I most want argued, and as it stands the argument slides between the two.

"We don't normally call this knowledge "conscious," although it is. It exists in deep limbo." Same objection as before, and "deep limbo" made me wince. It's a new term arriving without a definition, just when I needed one less.

Then the question that did something: "Why would he have to turn away, if what he is, is essentially bad?" That landed. It turns an observation about the gaze into an argument against a bad essence, and it doesn't need the conscious/aware machinery. I'd put more weight on it than on the mirrors. The lead-in, "Can you feel the sense in which the other person feels absent or somehow "turned away" from himself?", tells me what I'll feel. I did feel it: my father's face when I told him at twenty-two that I'd stopped going to Mass. He looked absent. But I'd have got there without the steering.

"Is it possible that you look a little bit like this when treating others with contempt?" "Like a bad argument." Yes. That's the sentence in the chapter I'll remember in a year.

"If seeing this makes you want to hide, notice that's the same verdict, pointed inward. It feels like accountability. It isn't." This half-caught me, from an angle you didn't aim at. I don't hide and I don't collapse. I'm proud that I can look at my worst motives, and my therapist thinks the pride is its own problem. Your sentence made me wonder whether my looking is another way of turning away: holding myself at arm's length like a specimen. I didn't expect that.

"Here's my claim: the more free we are of self-deception in ourselves, the fewer people we see as inherently defective or less than us. Whether that number ever goes to zero is something you can discover for yourself." Good. It's marked, it's testable, and it leaves me room.

**Other kinds of contempt.** "The stupid, the slow and the weak get the same verdict, with better cover: *I'm only being accurate.*" Caught. I grade two hundred undergraduate papers a term, and "I'm only being accurate" is my whole professional cover. It's Ania too. The sigh paragraph caught me as well. The paragraph about sending it "about" people to allies caught me in the faculty lounge, talking about Gary with Lorraine.

The trees paragraph lost me. "Calling it "contempt" sounds ridiculous, but it has the same essence, and originates from the same place." "Same essence" is asserted, with no evidence I can weigh. I skimmed the rest.

**Self-serving.** "Each would sincerely say they're doing good, and each knows." I refused this. It's a claim about other minds with no evidence, and the examples are politically easy targets. But the next paragraph is the most careful in the chapter: "When I'm sure someone knew exactly what they were doing, I've made them a villain. When I'm sure they had no idea, I've made them a machine." Villain or machine is a new distinction for me, and a good one. It also quietly contradicts "each knows" a paragraph earlier, which is a problem you should know about.

**The ending.** It ends a section too late. "Part of me already knows that this is how it works. All that's left is to become fully aware of it, so that I can make a different choice." "All that's left" makes it sound small, and after everything above it isn't. The chapter's real ending, for me, was "Whether that number ever goes to zero is something you can discover for yourself." That line leaves the reader alone with the question. The last paragraph goes back to explaining.

**Length.** A fifth could go. What I'd cut:
- the trees paragraph;
- the private-equity examples;
- "Not only can I not fool myself, but I can't fool others, either," which makes the leaking point that "And yet it always leaks" makes again two sections later;
- one of the several "part of me already knows" sentences (I counted more than I needed);
- the stretch from "Turning away from my *better nature* produces a gap in my awareness" through "The only thing that benefits is *it*," which says in abstract terms what the Encounter already showed me concretely.

The Encounter itself I wouldn't cut.

**Words.** "Conscious" versus "aware" is the one that matters. "Deep limbo" made me wince. So did "darkness" as a noun for something in me; it arrived as metaphor and started behaving like a substance. "Enlighten," in the pill: I don't know what enlightenment means to you, and I suspect it means something specific. "Better nature" I'm fine with, since Lincoln owns it.

**Last.** Obvious? Limbo, yes. I've taught it for fifteen years under other names. The rationalization monologue, mostly. What was new is that self-deception can be a message, and that being seen is the weapon. So is the villain/machine distinction, and the self-serving way we hand out "conscious" and "unconscious."

What became newly consequential was the cold paragraph, with Gary and with Ania. I'm having dinner with Ania on Sunday. I don't have a resolution, and I distrust my habit of turning an insight into a project. But I'm going to ask her about the business and try to notice what my face does while she answers. My therapist would say that watching my own face is the specimen move again. She may be right. I'll try anyway.

Would I pass it on? To my brother Marek, yes. He hasn't spoken to our sister in three years, and he runs hot. The Encounter would find him, and he won't care about "conscious." To my colleagues, not yet. They'd go straight for "conscious but unaware," dismiss the rest, and miss the one real discovery, the performative self-deception, which deserves better than that.

With real gratitude, and some bruising,
Tomasz

## What the chapter should take

This is the longest part, because the answer is yes: the chapter doesn't carry enough of the mechanism for a careful reader. Here is what I'd move, most important first.

**1. The step from "same capacity" to "same kind."** My main objection to the chapter was that "same kind of thing" slides. For contempt to land, we need to share a capacity to read a look and be wounded by it. The verdict denies that we share a moral nature. The second file has the step the chapter is missing:

"There's a standard the two of you share, since what they felt was hypocrisy, and hypocrisy needs a shared standard and a departure from it."

And from the list of observations: "on the receiving end, what lands is hypocrisy, not threat; the sender appeals to your capacity to recognize the violation while treating you as the kind of thing that has none, and from the sending end you count on exactly that."

That's the bridge. The chapter never uses the word "hypocrisy." The enemy's look says it, "Don't pretend you're doing something noble here. We both know what you're up to.", but the chapter never names what he's feeling. Worse, the chapter calls it something else: "The message *this is all you are* carries a subtle but primal threat: *... and therefore your wellbeing has ceased to matter.*" So the chapter says threat and the second file says "hypocrisy, not threat." You have to pick. From the receiving end, with Gary's letter, it was hypocrisy. That's why it still stings, and a threat wouldn't have.

Even with hypocrisy in hand, the bridge carries weight only if recognizing a standard is the same thing as having the better nature. A knowing villain recognizes the standard and breaks it anyway. The verdict "you're bad" is consistent with "you can recognize badness." So the argument needs a further premise, and the second file states it where the chapter doesn't:

"O4: hold a stranger's eyes and the wanting that they be all right arrives with the looking: seeing carries care."

**2. That check itself.** O4 is the premise the whole "same kind" argument rests on. It's a claim about experience, not a definition, so it's the kind of premise I can weigh. The chapter tests only the negative: "Picture someone who's done nothing to you, like the last cashier you spoke to, and see their face clearly. Now try a small cruelty: a sneer, a cutting remark." It never asks for the positive, and the positive is what "better nature" needs.

So I did it now, with Denise from the Kroger. I held her face and waited, without trying to do anything. Something arrived, small and unmistakable: a wish that her feet don't hurt by the end of the shift. I didn't produce it; it came with the looking.

Then I tried Gary. I noticed I'd pictured his tie, and then the letterhead of his letter, not his eyes. I had to push to get to the eyes, and when I got there the same small wish was there. I found that more persuasive than anything in the chapter's argument for better nature. The chapter should ask for this.

**3. The replacement for the hiding asymmetry.** The chapter's evidence for better nature was "If the darkness in me were me too, why wouldn't I have to hide my good behavior from it?" I refused it with the money I sent Ania under the label "rent." The second file doesn't use that argument at all. It uses a different asymmetry:

"a drive gets outweighed while you're still looking, and this one can't be; you have to stop looking first, and it takes work to keep from looking."

My Venmo case doesn't touch this. It wasn't about care being outweighed while I looked at her. And the Gary experiment above is a small instance of "it takes work to keep from looking." I'll add a piece of evidence you didn't use: the hood on the condemned. Executions cover the face, and the hood isn't there for the condemned. This argument, not the hiding one, should carry the claim in the chapter. Put O4 next to it as the check.

**4. Aimed, not hot or cold.** This matters most to me personally. The chapter says: "If the hot version didn't sound like you, read what follows with the cold one in mind; all of it applies." The second file says: "Not the cold form; the argument is about contempt, the aimed form, and the general rule rests on O1." Those contradict each other. I think the second file has the better distinction and the chapter has the better instinct. The line that matters isn't hot versus cold. It's aimed versus not aimed. The look Ania described to her mother, "like a bad argument," was cold and aimed; she received it, which is the point. My flat face at Denise was cold and not aimed. The chapter should draw that line, because as it stands a cold reader like me either misapplies the mechanism or, as I did at the cashier, finds a false counterexample.

**5. The airport.** This resolves my cashier result, the one I asked you about. I could give Denise the flat polite face with no reason arriving, and I didn't know whether that was a counterexample. The footnote in the second file answers it:

"Walking through an airport, you don't take in the hundred people around you as people. They're in the background, and that isn't turning away: if one of them dropped a bag and started crying, they'd come forward into view at once, and you'd feel the pull."

That's what my face at Denise was: background, not turning away. If she'd started crying she'd have come forward, and I'd have felt it. So it isn't a counterexample. The chapter needs this distinction the moment it asks a reader who runs cold to try its check. Without it, the check misfires on exactly the readers the cold paragraph invites in.

I have a caution, though. The definition that follows, "Turning away is keeping someone out of view when they'd otherwise come forward, because their reality would place a claim on you.", builds the motive into the definition. Once that's the definition, "harm requires holding the person out of view" is nearly true by definition. Take the airport sentence. Leave the definition in the appendix, or state it as a finding and not as a definition.

**6. Why a look is a harm at all.** I didn't notice this gap in stage 1, and I should have. The chapter never says why a look, with no further deed, *injures*. It tells me the look provokes, which is a different thing. The second file says it plainly: "In contempt there is no separate deed. The look is the deed, and there is nothing else." Then it gives the contrast with malice, which is the best new distinction in the second file:

"But malice as such needs neither of two things contempt needs. It need not be received (the target can be asleep while the deed is done), and where it does demand reception (a threat, a sadist's display) it needn't disavow itself as righteous; the honest villain says *yes, I mean you harm, and no, you're not my kind.*"

And: "It's why malice is frightening and contempt is maddening."

The chapter has the seed of this: "Notice: if it felt accidental, he might forgive or pity me instead. And if it were *purely* intentional, then it would read as overt malice—which would make me the bad guy." In stage 1 I said the chapter argued "That combination is both enough to produce the outcome I need, and also necessary." from only two alternatives, and that this was thin. The malice contrast fixes that, in English with no logic vocabulary. "Frightening" and "maddening" are the right words. The chapter already uses "uniquely maddening," so you'd be paying off a word it has already spent.

**7. Common knowledge without the mirrors.** I bristled at "Don't worry about tracing the logic." I called the mirrors, "because the faculty I'm seeing *with*—the one that reads our sameness—is the very thing I'm seeing *in* them.", a clever move presented as a proof. The second file gives a basis Lewis would accept:

"It's common knowledge the way a convention is: nobody was told it, and everyone has been on both ends."

That's evidence I can check. I have been on both ends; so has everyone I know. It's a shared basis in exactly Lewis's sense. Put it in place of the mirrors, or at least ahead of them.

And this, which is the best thing in the second file for the chapter's "hall of mirrors" passage:

"Unsaid is not the same as not common knowledge. What's missing, for both, is acknowledgment. Nothing is on the record, so nothing can be quoted to anyone else, or to yourself."

The chapter says "You both know exactly what's happening (and that the other knows it, etc.), but also that it can't be named without sounding crazy." That's the phenomenon. The second file's version names the mechanism: common knowledge with no record. "Or to yourself" ties the interpersonal half to the self-deception half in four words. It also explains the chapter's "Nothing that can be audited, *even by myself*." better than the chapter does. The contrast with Pinker, Nowak and Lee is a real distinction. Innuendo is built so it never becomes common knowledge. This does become common knowledge, and is never acknowledged. I'd call it the second genuinely new idea in the book so far, after the performative self-deception.

**8. The loop, in one sentence.** In stage 1 I stumbled on "If I'd looked carefully, I'd have seen my role in causing it. So I didn't look—and that refusal is exactly what caused it," because the time order confused me. The second file says the thing cleanly: "Because he can't see his part in the target's reaction, the reaction shows up to him as the target's nature: the bad essence he perceives is his own act, unseen as his." That's the whole Corruption section in one sentence, and it removes my confusion. The chapter could use it where its own sentence is.

**9. A definition of limbo that doesn't lean on "conscious."** Here the second file helps only halfway. It never uses "conscious" at all; I checked.
