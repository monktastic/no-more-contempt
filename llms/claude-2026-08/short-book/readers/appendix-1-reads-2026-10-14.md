# Appendix 1 cold reads (October 14)

*Two cold readers read the short book's Chapter 1 and then Appendix 1, mid-revision, to check the appendix against readers who aren't its author. Brief: every passage that answers a question they never had; every objection they did form, where it arose, and when (or whether) the text answered it; where they got lost. No advice asked for. Alongside them, three audits (contradictions, terms and references, inference steps) ran on the appendix alone; their findings were applied the same day and are in the diff. Letters verbatim.*

## The analytic reader (philosophy PhD, now data science; not Buddhist)

Aditya,

**1. Steady through the chapter, faster in The Encounter. Steady through the appendix until "Now see how tight this is", then a drag until "Where this stops."** Read cold, I'd have skimmed "What I think is really happening" and stopped at the Samantabhadra paragraph. I came back for Section 2 only because, for someone with my training, the lawyers' section is the reason to open the appendix at all. I'm a philosophy PhD who left for data science, I'm not a Buddhist, and I'm suspicious of spiritual books. Read the letter with that in mind.

First, the exercises, done briefly and honestly.

- *The wrong.* At my second data-science job I reported a model's lift from the one holdout split that looked good, knowing the other splits were flat. My stories were *everyone picks the favorable split* and *the PM won't read past the topline*. When I finally owned it, the knowledge wasn't new. The knowing side never argued, and the story side never stopped. So far it fits you. One thing didn't fit, and I come back to it below: there was no person "in view" or out of it. My wrong was against the truth and an abstraction called the company.
- *Without a story.* I don't think I could have done it without one.
- *Remorse in reverse.* In grad school I let a weak student off a plagiarism case and later felt bad about it. When I looked, it was regret at what it cost me, because the professor was annoyed. That is one of your listed categories. I'll come back to how it felt to find my answer already filed.

**3. QUESTIONS YOU DID HAVE**

- **Is "harm" defined so that O1 can't fail?** It arose at "picture someone in front of you, fully in view, and see whether using them will go." (Limbo). A judge sentences a man he's looking straight at, and a surgeon cuts someone she's looking at. The answer I'd get is that those aren't harm. Then the appendix says "Turned toward, I can be as harsh as I like; it may hurt, but it hands him no license." (What the need was looking for). So hurt done turned toward is not harm, and "harm" looks like it means *hurt done from the turn*. In that case "harm needs the turn" is analytic. The chapter says "That makes part of this a definition, and I'll own it." (The Way Out), but it owns the definition of "in view," not of "harm." **Never answered.** This is the main thing I'd want argued.

- **Inattention vs. turning.** I once snapped at a waiter because I was tired, with no story, just not looking at him. It arose at "Could you have done your wrong without a story?" (The Way Out). The airport footnote answers it: "Turning away is keeping someone out of view when they'd otherwise come forward, because their reality would place a claim on you." ([^converse]) That's a real answer. If he'd cried, I'd have stopped. **Answered late, and in a footnote.**

- **My wrong had no face in it.** It arose with the cherry-picked split, at "with the person fully in view, harm won't go." (1. The trap, point 2). Who was out of view, the PM? **Never answered.** Everything in the appendix is two people and a look.

- **Remorse in reverse looked pre-filed.** It arose at "If you think you have, look at what it was made of. Mine turned out to be something else: regret that it cost me, anger at being taken advantage of, guilt at breaking a rule I was raised on, or feeling foolish." (Limbo). My counterexample was already sorted before I gave it. The worry is about what tells "remorse" apart from "guilt at breaking a rule I was raised on", apart from which direction it points. If the only difference is direction, the asymmetry is built in. The appendix repeats the claim ("Regret over what a decency cost, I've found, and so have you.", 2. The rivals) but doesn't give the criterion. **Never answered.** It felt as if every road I could take had been answered before I took it.

- **Why does the knowing side not need to argue?** It arose at "Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" (Limbo). My deflationary answer is that defaults never need defending, and an internalized norm is a default. That explains the asymmetry without a better nature. The appendix's nearest rival is "a universal human faculty that can't be outweighed and has an evolutionary history" (Where this stops). That isn't my rival. Mine is learned norms plus empathy that *can* be outweighed. **Partly answered** at "a drive gets outweighed while you're still looking, and this one can't be" (2. The rivals). But triage nurses and judges outweigh it while looking, which brings me back to the definition of harm.

- **Point 2 says the turn is needed for nothing else, but the footnote says it is.** It arose at "And the turn is never needed for anything else" (1. The trap, point 2). I didn't object then. Then the footnote says "People also turn from each other to escape grief, or fear, or a claim they can't meet." ([^converse]), and What is not claimed says the turn "also produces numbness and paralysis with nobody harmed." Escaping grief is something else the turn is needed for. And "Turning away, by itself, is malice." (1. The trap) sits badly next to numbness with nobody harmed. **Never reconciled.** I felt it as a contradiction.

- **Does the turn always show?** It arose at "And it shows, in a face, to anyone who has the faculty." (1. The trap, point 3). Later the con man's "warmth is the most reported case there is, and it's performed" (2. The rivals). Doesn't a successful con depend on the turn *not* showing? **Never answered.**

- **Psychopathy.** It arose at "I think "pure evil" is this error at maximum intensity" (The Corruption). It was answered at "Missing in some" (2. The rivals), which is very late, and the answer cuts against you. A con man who performs warmth without recognition is what the missing-care hypothesis predicts. "I've never seen it look like nothing in particular" didn't persuade me. Instrumental harm from people with flat affect is reported to look like exactly that. You do say "the one I can't rule out", and I respected that.

- **Hindsight.** It arose at "did it feel like new information? Or like something you had always known" (Limbo). *I always knew* is what reconstructive memory produces after the fact. The appendix comes back with "I've never met anyone who was surprised by a wrong they'd hidden when they finally saw it; surprised at its size, often." (Take one away). That restates the observation. It doesn't answer the bias. **Never answered.** Much of the derivation rests on recall ("The recalled ones carry the most weight, and the derivation leans on them.", 2. What you checked), so this matters.

- **Common knowledge vs. sincere belief in bad people.** It arose at "So the three points above are common knowledge between us." (1. The trap). The chapter has you say "I believe there *are* bad people" (The Way Out). How is it common knowledge that there's no essential difference, if one party sincerely believes there is? The [^ck] footnote's "nobody was told it, and everyone has been on both ends" is the answer offered, along with limbo. Being on both ends gives each of us a private basis. I'm not sure it gives a *shared* one. **Partly answered, on time, through the footnote.** I'm still unsure "and it sees back" does any work.

- **Contempt that's never received.** It arose at "Given a look aimed to be read and dressed as righteous" (Take one away). Most of my contempt is for people who never see it: a politician on TV, a stranger online, a colleague I describe to someone else. The chapter counts that ("I send it *about* people, to my allies, with nobody's face in view.", The Spread, and "Most contempt is quiet"). The appendix's whole mechanism needs reception. "Not contempt for the world" (What is not claimed) limits the scope, but doesn't say whether contempt sent *about* someone is the same act. **Never answered.** The appendix explains less contempt than the chapter names.

- **What lands: hypocrisy, or shame?** It arose at "O3: on the receiving end, what lands is hypocrisy, not threat" (2. What you checked) and "What he felt had to be hypocrisy" (The punchline). My own case was shame first and hypocrisy years later. The chapter's child, who "believes it, and carries it for years" (The Corruption), sounds like my case. **Never answered in the appendix.** The chapter allows for it and the appendix doesn't.

- **Did my look make him bad?** It arose at "The bad essence I perceive is my own act, unseen as mine." (What the need was looking for). Your enemy "has just been cruel to someone who didn't deserve it" (The Encounter) before you look at him. The look produces his hardening, not his cruelty. The sentence claims the whole perceived essence. "Do this enough times, with enough people" gestures at an answer. **Partly answered; the sentence still overreaches.**

- **Using "no such difference" as a premise.** It arose at "Nothing true can satisfy it, since there is no such difference." (What the need was looking for). That's the conclusion of the abduction, and here it does a premise's work. **Never addressed.**

- **Does the baseline follow?** It arose at "If harm needs the turn, and the turn is needed only for harm, then what you turn from is the baseline" (Why it had to be this way). A biconditional between harm and the turn doesn't make what you turn from the default. You need the further claim that not-turning is where you start. **Never answered.** It may be what point 1 assumes, but the sentence presents it as derived.

- **The clear-seers test.** It arose at "look too at people chosen for clear sight on other grounds, a good detective or a war reporter, and see whether the verdict thins as the sight sharpens." (What I think is really happening). Police and war correspondents are known for cynicism that *grows* with exposure. If I ran your test casually, I'd expect it to come out against you, or be confounded by burnout. "what I've seen fits the second" isn't enough. **Never answered.**

- **Isn't this Manne's point, or a performative contradiction?** It arose at "Contempt is the claim that you're a worse kind of thing than me. But it only works because you're the same kind of thing, and both of us know it." (opening). It came back at "Its explicit message denies equality. Its successful operation presupposes it." (The punchline). Kate Manne, Paul Bloom and others have argued that cruelty and humiliation recognize the target's humanity rather than deny it. Your version is sharper, because it's about the target's *moral* capacity and the sender's limbo. But I waited for the appendix to say how it differs, and "Where else to look" doesn't list them. **Never answered.**

- **Kant as a rival.** I'd formed this early, at the self-deception passages in the chapter. It arrives at "The stronger rival is a will that keeps conscience intact and silences it with self-deceit" (2. The rivals). That's late, but welcome. My doubt is that "A propensity of the will would have no reason to need its target's better nature" distinguishes the trap from Kant's account of evil in general. It doesn't make them rivals. A Kantian can accept your trap as the specific shape contempt takes. **Answered, but I'm not convinced it's a rival.**

- **Retributivism.** It arose at "Something in me craves punishment, over and above any good it might do." (The Encounter). Desert is a respectable position. Is retributive desire always contempt? **Never answered.**

**4. LOST**

- "**The turn knows itself, refuses to, and shows.**" (1. The trap, point 3). I had to reread to get *refuses to know itself*. What I think it means: the turn is self-aware, declines that awareness, and is visible.

- "That is the third condition again, done on purpose and in front of him: the turn’s own limbo, made into a message." (1. The trap). I reread this twice. I think it means contempt takes the limbo state any turn has and displays it deliberately.

- "This one carries nothing but what it stands on" (1. The trap), and "Here it has only the ground" (2. The fixed point, written down). Two paragraphs earlier the look says "_I mean you harm_ and _I’m in the right_" and the verdict says "*this is all you are*". That is content. My best guess is that you mean no content *beyond* the shared ground, the verdict being only the denial of that ground. But I'm not sure.

- "Write G(P) for *make him know, with nothing he can quote, that I intend P*, and call what the look delivers J. Then J = G(J)" (2). G takes a proposition and returns an act. J is "what the look delivers", so is it an act, a proposition, or a state of knowing? As written, the equation mixes types. Unwound, J also contains no harm and no verdict, only intending-to-make-known. I think the point is that the knowing itself is the injury, but the formalism doesn't show it.

- "the honest villain says *yes, I mean you harm, and no, you're not my kind.*" (Take one away), set against "Keep the verdict without C, and you get malice" (2. Necessary and sufficient). In the first, malice hides the sameness; in the second, malice is the verdict without common knowledge of sameness. And the honest villain issues a verdict ("you're not my kind"). I couldn't tell whether malice has C hidden or lacks C.

- The counting. "The trap rests on three things", then seven pieces, then "the five conditions", then O1 to O5, then "two conditions", C and H, then "H isn't a separate ingredient". By the end it's one condition plus a verdict. I couldn't map the three onto C and H.

- "O2: the message tracked its target's exits and got stronger when pointed at (*you're just projecting*), and the flash behind it had to be aimed, land, and go unrecorded, none of it planned, though holding someone out of view takes effort." I read it three times. I think it says the targeting is adaptive and effortful but not premeditated.

- "O4: hold a stranger's eyes, now, until the face becomes a place somebody is looking out of" (2). I hadn't done this exercise. The chapter doesn't have it. I was reading alone at home, so "now" wasn't available. Among primates and on subways, a long stare is a threat. I couldn't tell whether "seeing carries care" was something I'd checked or something I was being told I had.

- "The rules the page leans on, that an aimed turn is for harm and that hiding-while-revealing is the signature of harm dressed as righteousness, are that one fact seen from outside." ([^ck]). I don't see how two conventions about what looks mean are the *same fact* as "there's no essential difference between you".

- "Not that there's one awareness. Only that there's nothing to tell them apart, which is all the mutual recognition needs." (What I think is really happening). Two things with nothing to tell them apart are, on the usual reading, one thing. I couldn't tell what's being denied.

- "Not universality from this page; O1 to O5 are stated for everyone and open to counterexample, and the deduction needs only your own instance." (What is not claimed). The conclusion is about common knowledge between two people, so it seems to need his instance too.

**5. THE SHAPE**

The central claim: contempt is a message whose working requires the very things its content denies. The target must share the sender's moral faculty, still have it, and be able to turn back. Both parties know this, and the sender hides it from himself while showing it to the other. So the look "denies equality" while it "presupposes it." Then comes an abductive add-on: the best explanation of all this is that harm is a turning from a baseline of care.

The presupposition argument felt tight to me. The abductive add-on didn't. The passage that felt most needed was "Contempt can tell someone *this is all you are* only by addressing the capacity in him that the verdict denies exists. Its explicit message denies equality. Its successful operation presupposes it." (The punchline). That is a real philosophical observation, and I'd defend it in a seminar. The least needed was "Two regresses and a loop", with the "Now see how tight this is" paragraph close behind. That paragraph is the third telling of the common-knowledge point, and by then I felt the appendix was persuading itself. In between, the counts keep changing (three, seven, five, five, two, one), and "harm" does definitional work it never admits to. Together those made the structure feel less tight than it is.

A reader who left philosophy but still reads it

**4. Lost**

- Section 1, point 3: "That isn't one way among several to put something in limbo. It's the only way: limbo is what turning away is." Read it three times. I think it means there's no limbo that isn't a turning; they're one thing with two names. I didn't see why that had to be settled inside the third assumption.

- Section 1: "because what's being seen is itself a faculty of seeing, and it sees back." I think: what I see in him is his capacity to see me, so seeing it means being seen. I got there slowly. In practice terms it's lovely. On the page I had to stop.

- Section 1: "That is the third condition again, done on purpose and in front of him: the turn’s own limbo, made into a message." Reread. I think it means contempt is limbo performed for an audience.

- Section 1: "This one carries nothing but what it stands on: the three points, common knowledge between us, held out of view, and the fact of my holding them. Take those away and there's nothing for it to be about." I didn't follow. My guess: the look has no content except *we both know, and I won't say it*. This is where I nearly put the appendix down.

- Section 1: "The look is an attempt to split something that can’t be split." I had to reread the paragraph around it, but once I got there this sentence landed.

- "Take one away": "So the knowledge has to be present and operating and refused, all at once." And then: "And since hiding the turn means hiding the hiding, the not-looking is never partial. The whole stance turns, and the room stays lit: nothing goes out of sight, I just face away from it." Reread. I got it through practice, not through the argument. The room staying lit I understood at once; that's simply true. "never partial" I didn't: in my own experience the turning is often partial, flickering.

- "What the need was looking for": "Reception is the injury; corruption is the harm the injury licenses, and it's how the thing spreads." Two abstract nouns pointing at a third. I think: the hurt is him getting the message, and the damage is what he goes on to do with it.

- Malice. Section 1 says "Turning away, by itself, is malice." "Take one away" says "All malice hides one thing from the one doing it: the sameness of the target." So is malice the turn without the pretense, or the sameness hidden from the one doing it, or the verdict without common knowledge? I couldn't make the three fit, and I stopped trying.

- "Why it had to be this way": "The second of the three carries more than the trap needs." I couldn't see why we were back on the second assumption, or what "carries more" meant. Maybe: the second assumption also tells you care is the default. I'm not sure.

- Section 2, "What you checked", O4: "hold a stranger's eyes, now, until the face becomes a place somebody is looking out of". The chapter I read has no such exercise. - Section 2, "What you checked": "Nothing is assumed beyond the opening assumption and these five." Which opening assumption? Section 1 opened on three. I went back, found "Take that as an assumption" under point 1, and guessed that's the one. It's a guess.

- Section 2, "The fixed point, written down", the whole paragraph, from "Write G(P) for *make him know, with nothing he can quote, that I intend P*, and call what the look delivers J. Then J = G(J)". I gave up. Notation tires me, and it told me nothing the plain version hadn't.

- Section 2, "Two regresses and a loop": "Every level carries the same content, read through a convention that comes before the encounter: hiding-while-revealing means harm believed righteous." Skimmed. It felt like the fixed point a second time.

**5. The shape**

As I understood it: contempt tells someone *you're a lesser kind of thing than me*, but it can only land, and only maddens, because we both already know we're the same, both able to turn from the same care, and the one sending it hides that from himself while making sure the other sees it. What it says denies the equality; what it does depends on it. Through "The punchline" it felt tight. After that it felt like a second document, answering people who aren't me.

Most needed, from "The punchline": "Contempt can tell someone *this is all you are* only by addressing the capacity in him that the verdict denies exists. Its explicit message denies equality. Its successful operation presupposes it." That's the whole of it, and I'd have believed it from my sister's face alone. Least needed: "The fixed point, written down", and the Gödel footnote. They felt like things you needed to have said, not things I needed to read.
