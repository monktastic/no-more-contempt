# How the cold reads are run

*Stateless. The method that has worked, so the next round doesn't have to be reinvented. The letters themselves are the dated files beside this one.*

**What a cold reader is.** A subagent (general-purpose, in the background) that is given a person to be, a folder of clean files, and nothing else. It has not seen the repo, the theory, or any earlier draft. It is the only escape we have from our own reading of the text; Claude's "this lands" has been wrong every time it was tested.

**Preparing the files.** Build clean copies into the session scratchpad, never the sources: strip `[TODO…]` notes, HTML comments and any `~~struck~~` text (`bookparts.TODO_RE` plus a comment regex), and check the result for leaks. No preface and no intro unless they are what's being tested; they exist to make a real person want to read, and readers who were given them spent their letters on them. Number the files in reading order. When two versions are compared, don't say which is newer in the file name.

**Who the reader is.** A full person: age, work, how long and in what they've practised or not, what they've read, a real enemy, and a real cost their contempt has had at home. Two requirements learned the hard way. They must have done enough work on themselves (therapy, step work, a men's or women's group) that being shown their darkness doesn't make them shut down; otherwise the letter is all defence. And the non-sangha reader must be open to spiritual language ("darkness," "the light," "soul") while knowing nothing about Buddhism; a sceptic with no spiritual leanings won't get past Chapter 2 and only tells us that. Say what makes this person shut down: being handled, being told what they'll find, jargon, claims presented as proven. Use a new person each round.

**The brief.** Write letters to the friend who passed them the manuscript; say what happened in them, not what they think of it; don't be an editor or a coach; don't be polite at the expense of being true. Do the exercises with real cases from the persona's life. Warn them that the opening is the heaviest part and that relief comes later, so they don't feel left in it.

**Stages.** One file per stage, and the stage's letter is written to a file *before* the next file is opened, so the first reading can't be revised by the second. Typical stages: (1) the text being tested; (2) another version of the same stretch, for comparison, with "what should be recovered? quote it"; (3) the rest of the book, with, for the non-sangha reader, the seam named in advance: from here the author states marked beliefs; notice where you stop being able to follow, and whether it spoils what you saw in the opening. The agent returns the full text of every letter, not a summary.

**Questions that have earned their place.** What's working and what isn't. Did anything produce a real recognition you'll carry out of the room, and which sentence or exercise did it. Does any chapter feel like being told again what an earlier one showed; what would you keep if it were cut hard. Anything confusing, out of order, or repeated: name it. Is the monologue believable as what a mind does. Where were you handled, or told what you'd find. For the practitioner: what's wrong, thin or overclaimed from your seat; what's said better than your tradition usually manages. For the other: where did it assume something you don't believe or use a word you don't have; could a friend with no spiritual leanings have followed.

**Afterwards.** Save the letters verbatim in a dated file here. Apply only what both readers agree on; list single-reader points in `todo.md`; when two rounds disagree, say so rather than picking. Check every praised line is still in the book after the edits (grep them), and check every sentence cut from an earlier version for insights that now appear nowhere (a sentence-by-sentence similarity pass against the old file).

## The general book's rounds (7 to 10): the questions, by what they're for

The general Chapter 1 has been through four rounds (`general-book/cold-reads-round7…round10`). Each round's prompt asked the standing questions above plus the ones below. They are grouped by the decision they feed, so the next round can pick the ones that are still open. Every question is put in the reader's terms, never the book's ("did that feel found or told," not "was the claim earned").

**Who to send it to.** Two readers a round, new each time, both with enough inner work not to shut down. One fully secular (a Dana, an Ilse, a Nadia: no spiritual leanings, winces at "the light," reads Kahneman or history), one spiritually open but Buddhism-blind (a Marcus, a Teresa, an Owen: church background, twelve-step, Quaker). One of them should run cold contempt, since most readers over forty do and the chapter's narration is hot. Stage 2 is always the previous version, labelled only "another version," so each round also measures whether the last rewrite helped a cold reader or only the reads.

**What they buy, and how far.** For each of the chapter's moves, did you already have it, does the chapter's version add anything, and does the argument hold or lean on something you'd have to grant? The moves: limbo (knowing while not letting yourself know); the verdict, not the harshness; contempt provokes the reaction it then cites as proof; the sender knows enough ("knowingly"); the leak is the payload (revealing my self-deception is the point); harm needs the turn; "home" (the better nature is what's present when I am; the other part runs in my absence); the same thing in him; contempt is addressed to the part it denies; both parties already knew (common knowledge); evil as an absence that behaves like a presence. Ask them to grade each: found in themselves, granted as the author's, or refused.

**Found or told.** The round-10 question, still the central one for the secular reader: the passage "My better nature is what's here when I am… That's why I call my better nature *myself*… Because it's the one that's home": did that feel like something you had found in the exercises, or something you were told? Be exact about which half. Same for "There's someone here" in the face exercise (arrived on its own, or supplied because it was printed), and for the three checks now placed before it (the return, the woods, which part would you keep).

**Where it lands as the climax, and where it's a second explanation.** Which sentence did the chapter peak at for you? Did the section beginning "Now for the strangest part" land as the climax, or as a second explanation of ground you'd already been given? Did "you know better" name what you felt on the receiving end? Did the last sections (evil as absence; the second stranger; "I can stop") belong after the climax, or feel like a second ending? Did the chapter end at the right sentence?

**Where you were handled.** Quote every place you were told what you'd find or feel, asked a question with its answer built in, or heard a facilitator, a workshop, a pulpit, a brief. Count the "check"s. Where did the volume go up (bold, italics, "crystal clear," "end evil")?

**Where you skimmed, and what's too long.** Name the stretch. Is the hot monologue believable as what a mind does, and how much of it would you cut? Did the cold form get the narration it needed, or a summary? Did the mechanism section explain the loop once, or re-enact the narration?

**What's missing.** Whose case is it: did the author go first, with a name and an occasion, or ask you for yours and give a hypothetical? Is there a real second stranger? Does the cold form have a loop? Is there an answer for the enemy who is just a jerk and not in limbo? Did anything sound like being asked to go easy on the person who hurt you or yours?

**Order.** Did the opening claim make you want to read, or feel like being told the answer; did the memory that follows feel connected to it? Did "the place I send it most often is home" belong where it is? Where the face exercise sits relative to the strangest part and the evil section: does one order work better than the other, in the two versions?

**The test of the book.** Two questions, always last: Was any of it obvious, so that you thought "I already knew this"? And did anything you already knew become newly consequential, so that it changes what you'll do this week? The chapter works when the answer to both is yes.

**Words.** Any word you don't have, or that made you wince ("darkness," "evil," "the light," "soul," "better nature," "limbo"). Did "darkness" stay something the author does, or become a thing that does things, and where?

**For the practitioner or the reader who already has it** (a Jonah): where did boredom start and stop, sentence by sentence; what's new, named precisely against what you had before; where would a stranger have put it down.

**What each round found, in one line each, so the next round doesn't re-ask what's settled.** Round 7: the narration and Limbo land; the recursion as logic loses everyone; the pitch paragraph and "right?" go. Round 8: limbo-first beats the claim-first opening for both readers; "one memory," the let-down and the receiving-end checks are how purpose gets granted; the intentional argument needs the two checks before "knowingly." Round 9: three things are new even to the reader who has it all (argue/not-argue; the leak is the payload; addressed to what it denies); the "unconscious" straw man, the check tics, and "end evil" go. Round 10: "home" is half found, half told, and told for the secular reader; the strangest part reads as a second explanation with one new thing in it (the "why the fuck shouldn't I" block); the chapter ends at "I can stop"; the cold form has no loop and is the form most readers run. Round 11 (three readers): the teacher, the loved-ones paragraph in "I" and the trust check are what did the work; the teacher needs its outside (what, when, his name); two of the three checks before "home" were told or rigged; the chapter still ends twice, at the alignment and at "I can stop"; the cold form is still a summary. Round 12 (three readers, the compressed `chapter-1-alt.md`, each against a different older Chapter 1): the relief-and-relabel sentence, "speaking to the part of him I claim doesn't exist" and "believable only to me (and my allies)" landed with all three; cold readers are spectators until the cold form, so it moved up; "the nature of evil," "the great paradox of evil," the which-chain, the "etc." tower, the trees line and "that's part of why I send it" lost all three; all three, from three different comparisons, named know-versus-prove as the biggest thing missing.
