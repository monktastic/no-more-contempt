## Colleen (52, hospice chaplain, ELCA; twenty years in Al-Anon)

### Stage 1

Dear Aditya,

I read this at the kitchen table over two evenings, slowly, with a pencil, the way I read everything. I did the exercises. Some of them cost me more than I expected, and I'll tell you where. I'm going to go in order.

**The opening and the memory**

"Or maybe not." I liked you right away for that. You told me what you wanted to show me and then you let me off the hook before I was on it. Then "Hold them tentatively" and I thought, all right, I can do that. I've spent thirty years listening to people tell me what I already know about myself, starting with my father in the pulpit, so I came in with my guard up. You lowered it a little.

The memory came immediately, which surprised me. Not the one I'd have chosen. It was a Monday morning in maybe 2006. I called the county highway department and told Dale's supervisor that Dale had the flu. Dale was on the bathroom floor. Kate was fourteen and standing in the kitchen doorway listening to me do it. I knew it was wrong while I was saying it. I couldn't fully admit it for years, not until Al-Anon had worn me down.

"Did it feel like new information?" No. It felt exactly like something I had always known. You have that right, and I'd say so to anyone. When I finally said it out loud in a meeting, nobody in the room was surprised, and neither was I.

"If you did, did that happen passively?" Here is what came back. That same morning, after I hung up, Kate said, "He's not sick, he's drunk." And I said, "Don't you talk about your father that way." That was not passive. I turned on my own child to keep from hearing what she'd said. So your next question, whether some part of me knew I was doing it as I did it: yes. Bought, completely. That's the first claim and I can stand on it.

"Nobody ever looked honestly at something in themselves while being told they were worthless for having it." I bought that one with a footnote. I grew up on the general confession, "we are by nature sinful and unclean," every Sunday. It never made anybody honest. It made us good at confessing in unison and changing nothing. So yes.

"Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" That one I sat with. The side that argued was very busy that morning. *He'll be fine by Wednesday. They'd fire him. Who does it help.* The other side said nothing at all. It just stood in the doorway in a fourteen-year-old's body.

**The cashier**

I tried it with the young man at Woodman's last week, the one with the lip ring who bags too slowly. The smile went without anything. The sneer didn't need a reason so much as it needed a story, and the story was already half there: he's slow, he doesn't care. I'm not proud that the sneer was that close to ready. But you asked whether either needed a reason before it would go, and the honest answer is that the cruelty needed me to make him a little less of a person first. The kindness didn't need anything. So I bought it, though not as cleanly as you put it.

**The remorse question**

This is where I slowed down. My first answer was: yes, I have felt remorse for having been decent. Twenty years of covering for Dale, of being "kind" to him, of making his excuses and keeping the house quiet. When I finally saw it, I felt sick, the sick of having gone against myself.

Then I sat with it longer and saw that it wasn't decency I was sick about. It was fear dressed up as decency. My sponsor Marge used to say, "You're not helping him, you're managing him." What I'd called kindness was me not wanting him to leave and not wanting the neighbors to know. So your asymmetry survived, but I had to work for it, and I'd guess a lot of women in my rooms would stop exactly where I stopped the first time. You never say anything about the counterfeit, about people who have mistaken their own fear for goodness and then feel remorse about the "goodness." I bought it tentatively.

The bad person lying awake for his secret kindness: I've sat with dying men who were ashamed of having cried in front of their sons. But you'd already covered that with "guilt at breaking a rule I was raised on," and it isn't remorse. That's the right distinction. I've watched it at the bedside: the shame about tenderness is loud and the man fights it, and the remorse, when it comes, is quiet and he doesn't fight it at all.

**The Encounter**

I'll tell you where I refused first, and it was early here: "I would defy a message like that too, and violently."

I wouldn't. I didn't. I was on the receiving end of exactly this look from my father for most of my girlhood, from the pulpit and across the dinner table, and I didn't defy it. I believed it. I went quiet and good and small, and I married a drunk and spent twenty years earning a look I was never going to get. Some people harden. Some people agree with the verdict. Kate did the other thing; she defied it, and she's still defying it, at me. But the scene assumes everyone on the receiving end fights, and I didn't.

I could still follow what came after. I just read it as one of two ways a person takes this, not the only way. I kept waiting for you to say something about the ones who swallow the verdict, and you mostly didn't.

The monologue itself: yes, believable as what a mind does. I've seen it a hundred times at bedsides, the daughter who flew in from Phoenix giving the look to the brother who stayed and changed the bedpans, or the reverse. "I'm only holding him to account, and if he reacts poorly, well, it only proves my point." I have heard nearly those words in a family conference room. "It's really more like an *invitation*" made me laugh out loud, because that is precisely how my father talked about hellfire.

I skimmed at "*Ha!* Classic." and at "Surely that's not what I'm doing. I'm just being fierce with evil, right? Right." The first was a little performed, like you wanted me to see you being self-deprecating. The second I went back to and decided it worked; it's the mind talking, and the mind does that.

The cold version I knew in my bones. "A politeness with nothing behind it." That is my native dialect. I'm a Lutheran from Wisconsin. We don't yell. We bring a hot dish and never speak to you again. If you'd only written the hot version I would have quietly filed you under "not my problem," which I suppose would have proven your point.

**Integrity**

"So what am I supposed to do—be *nice* to evil?" Yes, that was my question, and you asked it right when I was asking it. The timing was right. Any later and I'd have been arguing with you instead of reading.

The person who corrected me with integrity: Ruth, my CPE supervisor, the year I was training as a chaplain. I'd spent forty minutes with a dying farmer and talked about grace the whole time, and she made me read my verbatim aloud in front of the whole peer group. Then she said, "You weren't with him. You were with your father." It was harsher than anything anyone had said to me in years, and in front of five people. I didn't dig in. I went out to my car and cried, and then I went back the next day and sat with the farmer and shut up. So yes, it matches. More fierce, and no verdict.

Your teacher story did something I didn't expect. It was the least performed thing in the chapter. "He was searching my face for something, some sign that I knew, and that made me look for it in myself." That's what Ruth did. I had never put that into words: that she was looking for something in me, and her looking made me look. And then "I don't want to make too much of that afternoon. It worked because there was something in me for it to reach" made me trust you. You didn't turn the story into a miracle.

One snag. "*you're better than this*." My father said those exact words to me, and it was a verdict every time. The words can carry either thing. I know you mean the spirit and not the phrase, but when I read it I flinched, because I've heard it from someone who was very much hiding something.

"What is it that two people on opposite sides can both be serving?" I have a one-word answer to that, and it's my job. I noticed you didn't give one. I wasn't sure, at first, whether that was tact or evasion. By the end I decided it was tact.

**The Corruption**

"Back to my enemy" is insight, not lecture, though it's dense. I had to read "Neither half alone would be enough to get me what I need: both his reaction and my innocence" twice. The second time it opened. If it felt accidental he'd pity me; if it looked deliberate I'd be the bad guy. I needed it to be both. That's right, and it's horrible, and I'd never seen the mechanics laid out like that.

"Deny it, and you're the kind of person who does. Get angry, and that's proof too. Stay calm, and you don't even care. Apologize, and it's too convenient." That is my house. That is every argument with Kate since she was sixteen, only I've been on both ends of it with her and I'm not sure anymore which end I'm usually on.

"My stories kept changing; my conclusion never did." Good. I underlined it.

"*I'd be a chump.*" I recognized it from Dale. He used to say, after one of my sighs, that he might as well drink, since he was the drunk anyway. I thought for years that was manipulation. Some of it was. Some of it was exactly what you describe.

"From the inside, being proven right and being right are the same experience. The only difference is who produced the evidence." That's one of the best things in the chapter.

**The two buttons**

Is there something viscerally satisfying about seeing evil punished? Yes. I don't need to think about it.

My worst enemy is Dale's brother Gary. He poured Dale a whiskey at Thanksgiving when Dale had ninety days, and at the funeral he told people I had nagged his brother into the grave. I put Gary in front of the two buttons. Some part of me reached for the first one, and fast. Then I made myself reach for the pill, and here's what I found: I wanted Gary to wake up changed and *know* he'd been wrong about me. I wanted the pill to come with a confession. So even my good button was a vengeance button. I don't know if you meant that to happen, but it happened, and it's the truest thing I learned in the first half.

**Whose intention**

"That's intent, just like your deflection was in the opening." This one pushed. You were telling me what my deflection was. As it happens, mine was intent; "Don't you talk about your father that way" was not an accident. So I agreed. But I noticed the push.

The part that "acts as if it has goals of its own." I didn't refuse it. I heard something I already know. In the rooms we say the disease is out in the parking lot doing push-ups while you're in the meeting. We say "my disease wants me dead, but it'll settle for me miserable." I heard that. I also heard Luther's old Adam, who has to be drowned daily and is a strong swimmer. So I bought it, because I've been living with that grammar for twenty years. And "None of this lets me off the hook. It's my mind, and I answer for what it does" came at exactly the right moment. If you'd waited a page I'd have stopped trusting you; it's the move every drunk I've loved has made, "it wasn't me, it was the drink."

"When I'm sure someone was fully aware of what they were doing, I've made them a villain. When I'm sure they had no idea, I've made them a machine." That paragraph caught me. I've done both to Dale, depending on the year. When he was alive he was a villain. Since he died he's become a machine, "the disease," which is kinder-sounding and just as far away. What I find hard to hold is exactly what you say: he knew, and didn't let himself feel the weight of it. Same as me on the phone with his supervisor.

"Underneath that is a deeper fear: that there may be no such thing as real goodness." Yes. That's the fear underneath every family fight I've ever mediated in a hospice room.

"*Only a chump believes otherwise.*" Landed, and I connected it immediately back to "*I'd be a chump*" in the italics earlier. The enemy's lesson and mine are the same lesson. That's well built.

**The way out**

"What does "yourself" mean here?" I tried it. I went back to the phone call and asked what I was hiding the knowledge from. I didn't find nothing. What I found had Kate's face on it, standing in the doorway. Not Kate herself; the part of me that saw what Kate saw. And underneath that I found that the part I was hiding it from was the part that would have had to *do* something: pack the car, call his brother, call a lawyer. The knowledge wasn't hidden from a witness so much as from the one who'd have to act. I don't know if that's what you're pointing at. It may be the same thing seen from the side. I'll leave it with you.

"Did it feel like *losing* something of yourself, or like getting something back?" Getting something back. No question. The first time I said it in a meeting I felt like I'd been away for a long time.

"our better nature isn't one part among others. It's a description of what we *are*." I granted it, and more than granted it; it's what I'd call the image of God, and in my tradition it's never erased, only buried. So it didn't sound grandiose to me. It might to my friends who don't believe in anything. "Because it's the one that's home" is a lovely sentence. I've sat with people in their last hours who finally came home to something, and that's what it looks like.

"Why would he have to turn away at all, if what he is, is essentially bad?" I tried to answer it with Gary in mind, and with my father. My father's theology had a ready answer, total depravity, that we're bad all the way down. But that answer doesn't explain why my father couldn't look at me when he gave the verdict. He always looked slightly past me. If he'd been simply bad, why would he need to avert anything? I couldn't answer your question in his favor. That surprised me, and it's stayed with me.

The bad-essence passages: half and half. "It's convenient to ascribe the worst behavior to some kind of "bad essence."" read a little like you heading off an objection. But "Like the *presence* of something, rather than the absence it actually is" caught me. That's Augustine, evil as privation, and I learned it in seminary and believed it the way you believe a footnote. At deathbeds I've seen it. There's a particular emptiness in some people's faces at the end that looks like a presence. It isn't.

"Could they have done it with kindness in their eyes?" The worst thing I know of personally is a man I sat with in 2019 who had abused his daughter for years. No. Whatever was in his eyes when he did it, it wasn't kindness. I believe that. I held it with him for six weeks, and I believe it.

**No one watching**

This felt stuck in. You'd left correction behind and gone deep into the bad essence and the gaze, and then suddenly we're back to correcting people, and then we jump to Common knowledge. It belongs closer to Integrity, or somewhere near the end.

I did try your question. I corrected Kate badly when she was nineteen and coming home drunk from Eau Claire. I came at her like I came at her father, every sigh and every silence I'd perfected on him. Six years later her sister Emily got a DUI, and I drove to the county lockup and sat with her and didn't say anything for an hour. It went better. Emily calls me. Kate doesn't. I'm not going to pretend that's the whole story of why, but I'd be lying if I said it's not part of it.

The integrity saying: my father used the same one, only with God as the one watching. Your "But someone always is: me" is stranger and harder. I knew it was true.

**Common knowledge**

I followed the first step, with some effort. "How could it do that, unless it already knew that he's the same kind of thing as me." That's plausible, and I bought it tentatively. The hypocrisy step I bought fully: "the charge only landed because I hold the one he accused me of breaking, and his outrage in making it showed that he holds it too." That's Dale calling me a hypocrite for the glass of wine I'd have at Christmas. It stung because I held the standard. And he was outraged because he held it too, sitting there with the bottle. We were never arguing about wine.

The hall of mirrors is Kate and me. "you both know exactly what's happening, and that it can't be named without sounding crazy, because to everyone else it's deniable." Every holiday. Her father's sister thinks Kate is cruel to me. Kate's husband thinks I'm cruel to her. Both of them are watching a conversation that isn't happening.

What surprised me: "**Love and contempt are addressed to the same place.**" I stopped there for a long time. It explains why my father's look went so deep. It went in by the same door love would have used. It's the best sentence in the chapter and I'd give it to every family I sit with.

"Try it now: imagine the same act without turning away." I tried it with the phone call. Could I have said "he has the flu" while fully looking at Kate in the doorway, at what I was teaching her? No. I'd have had to put the phone down. "Does it surprise you that a stranger could count on that about you?" Honestly, no. It's what the liturgy counts on every Sunday. But it moved me that you said you were counting on it.

Then I refused something: "I don't mean any of this as an inference." It is an inference. You just spent two pages building it, and I followed the building. I recognized the result, yes, the way I recognized the phone call. But you can't argue for something for a page and then tell me it was never an argument. That was the one place I felt you trying to have it both ways, and it's exactly the move your chapter is about.

"evil": I accepted the word throughout. I'm not squeamish. "It's why I don't think "pure evil" is a different kind of thing, but the same thing at full strength" I accept as theology I already hold. What made me flinch was the line before: "That's what I'd normally call "evil"—cruelty for its own sake; the thing I hate most in the world—and I'd needed them to know it." That stopped me cold, because I thought of the sigh I give Kate, and I didn't want it to be the same thing at a lower strength. I suspect it is.

"my allies are the entire rest of society": this arrived a little fast. But I knew it from the nurses' station. A man came onto our unit two years ago who'd been convicted of killing a child, and the way the staff spoke about him, in front of each other, never in front of him, is exactly "off the record." Nobody did anything you could write up. Everybody knew. So it followed, for me, from life more than from what came just before it.

"Fighting evil is the place where my own darkness is most likely to come out, because it has the best cover." This is the thing I have strong opinions about: piety that's really contempt. And here is what I caught: I enjoyed that paragraph too much. I read it and thought of my father, and of a chaplain I work with, and I felt a warm little vindication. Then I got to your subtlest-contempt paragraph and saw myself doing it. You set that trap well, whether you meant to or not.

**The same thing**

"The story I tell myself is that I am the one in charge, sitting between my better nature and my self-interest, deciding which one gets to win today." That's me. "set aside if the price is right." That's me with Gary. "But it's not an advisor." That landed. I took "the same thing" to be God, or what my tradition calls the law written on the heart. I don't think you need me to call it that. I think the passage works either way.

"Sometimes this reminds them to do the same—especially when I've been given every reason to abandon it." That's my work, on a good day. The furious son who screams at me in the hallway, and I stay. Sometimes it reminds him.

**The spread to the end**

"I send it to people I love." I put the pages down here and went and stood at the sink. "The sigh that tells them what I really think of them, dressed up to make me seem like the reasonable one. The look on their face that tells me it landed, which I pretend not to have seen, leaving them nothing to accuse me of." That's Kate. That's the phone call last Easter. I know her face when it lands and I have pretended, for years, not to see it. I don't have anything clever to say about this paragraph. It caught me completely.

"Sometimes it looks like me writing this book *at* people, instead of *for* them." I appreciated that, though part of me wondered whether it was a little performed humility. I let you have it.

"Seeing both at once is what makes full responsibility safe enough to bear." Yes. That's Step Four and Step Five in one sentence.

**The exercise**

I did it with a man at the bus stop on East Washington, an older man in a Packers jacket. It came quicker for me than you describe, because I do this for a living. When someone is unconscious and dying, their face is all you have, and you learn to wait until somebody is looking out of it even with the eyes closed. "*There's someone here. This is real from the inside, the way mine is.*" Yes.

Then I put Gary's face there. You're right: "the story is there before the face is." The funeral comment was there before his face had even come into focus. I didn't even get to see what he looks like. That's the turning away, live, as you say. I'm still a little shaken by how fast it was.

It gathered up what I'd read. It didn't feel like one ending too many. If anything, it's the only ending that asks me to do something instead of agree with something.

**Endings before the end**

The chapter seemed to end three or four times before it did. "Whether that number ever goes to zero is something you can discover for yourself" sounded like an ending. So did "how evil spreads while staying "off the record."" So did "especially when I've been given every reason to abandon it." Each one was a good last line, and then the chapter kept going.

**Length, repetition, words**

Things said twice: the flicker of satisfaction ("a brief flicker of satisfaction, vindication, even relief" and later "When it worked, I always felt that flash of satisfaction"). The quiet, cold contempt (the cold-version paragraph, and later "Most contempt is quiet"). The warning against self-condemnation at the start ("If seeing that makes you want to call yourself names, hold off") and at the end ("If seeing this makes you want to hide, notice that's the same verdict pointed inward"). That last pair I'd keep; it's a bookend and I needed it both times. And "Part of me knows" so many times I started to hear it as a drum.

If I had to cut a fifth: the "No one watching" section as it stands (move the saying, cut the rest); "Most contempt is quiet"; "I've found I'm almost always turned away to *some* degree"; some of the middle of "Whose intention," which I could feel you explaining after I'd already got it; and the first page of Common knowledge, which goes around once more than it needs to.

Words: "dammit," "*Ha!* Classic," and "mash" pulled me out. "as I increasingly contact mine" made me wince; nobody talks like that. "wellbeing" is a brochure word. "Limbo" I liked, though a Catholic friend will tell you it was abolished.

**Last**

Was any of it obvious? Some. Privation, the old Adam, "you're only as sick as your secrets." I already had the pieces. But "Love and contempt are addressed to the same place" was new, and the Gary pill was new, and the "what's wrong with you?" paragraph took something I already knew about my sigh and made it consequential. Kate's birthday is Saturday. I'm going to call. I'm going to try to say happy birthday and nothing else, and when there's a silence I'm going to try not to fill it with that breath. I don't know if I'll manage it.

Would I pass it to a friend? Yes, to Marge, my old sponsor, who is eighty-one and will read it with a pencil and argue with "I don't mean any of this as an inference" just like I did. I almost said I'd pass it to my colleague, the pious one, and then I saw why I wanted to, and I won't.

Thank you for this. It was harder than I expected.

Colleen

### Stage 2

Dear Aditya,

I read the second file a day later, with my first letter already sealed, so to speak. Most of it I recognized line for line. That made the differences jump out, and made me notice where I'd been leaning on something without knowing it.

**Which did more, and which treated me better**

The chapter did more to me. I'm sure of that. The biggest reason is the teacher, and the second is where Integrity sits. The second file has neither, and without them I think I'd have read the second half with my arms folded.

Which treated me better is closer. The second file is a little leaner and a little less insistent in places, and it does two or three things more honestly. But the chapter treated me better at the moment it counted most. I came out of the Encounter wanting to ask "So what am I supposed to do—be *nice* to evil?" and the chapter asked it for me, right then. In the second file I had to carry that question through the Corruption, the buttons, Whose intention and the whole of The way out before anyone acknowledged it. By the time the second file asks "So what does it look like when someone fights evil without self-deception?" I'd stopped waiting. And it's not my question. My question had an edge on it. The chapter's version has the edge, and so it sounds like someone heard me.

**What the second file has that the chapter should recover**

"If it does end up reminding them, I can't take credit for it." (the second file). This is the one I'd fight for. In my work, the worst thing a chaplain can do is take credit for someone's peace at the end. The minute you think your staying oriented produced their turning, you've made them your project, and they can feel it. That sentence is the guard rail on "Sometimes this reminds them to do the same," and the chapter leaves it out. Without it, "the best thing I can do, for them and through them for the world" could slide into the subtle contempt you describe later, the patience that's really superiority. I'd put it back.

"So part of me already knows the difference between the "good" I've invented to excuse myself and the kind we all recognize. I just tell myself I don't." (the second file). This is the piety sentence. It's the thing I have strong opinions about, and the second file says it plainly. My father's "good" was invented to excuse himself, and some part of him knew the difference. I'd have underlined this twice. The chapter doesn't have it, and the "same thing" passage is thinner for it.

"I get to pass off my contempt as a healthy part of righteous anger." (the second file), against the chapter's "I get to pass off my contempt as righteous anger." The second is more exact, and it matters to me. Righteous anger is real. Jesus turned over tables. The chapter's version can be read as saying all righteous anger is contempt in disguise, and I don't believe that and I don't think you do either. The second version says the contempt hides *inside* the real thing, which is how it actually goes.

"When I look at how we treat "evil people" together—on the news, in the comment section—I see the same thing, done by society as a whole. The message is: _you will be the dumping ground for our collective darkness._ It only works because we all already know what's happening." (the second file). In my first letter I said the chapter's "my allies are the entire rest of society" arrived a little fast. This paragraph doesn't. "Dumping ground" is the scapegoat in Leviticus, sent out into the wilderness carrying everyone's sins, and I recognized it at once from the nurses' station I told you about. I'd want this back, either instead of the allies sentence or beside it.

"It isn't always moral, but it's always a sense of being more deserving." (the second file), in the paragraph that begins "Most contempt is quiet." I'd recover it. It names the cold, everyday thing better than anything else in either version. Being more deserving is the Wisconsin version exactly: the woman who brings the better bars to the funeral lunch.

"What would happen if I could just stop pretending I don't already know this?" (the second file), at the end of its Integrity. I'm less sure of this one. It's a good question, and it's the kind that leaves room. In the second file it closes a section that has wandered, so it has a lot to gather. In the chapter there's no exact place for it. I mention it because it was the one place in the second file where I put the pages down for a moment.

And the order. In my first letter I said "No one watching" felt stuck in, and that it belonged closer to Integrity. The second file puts it there: the integrity saying, the stopping of misbehavior, the time you corrected someone badly, all inside Integrity. So the second file did what I asked for. But I don't think it worked as well as I'd hoped, and I'll come back to that.

**What the chapter was right to leave out or change**

The teacher. Not a change, an addition, but it's the biggest difference. "He was searching my face for something, some sign that I knew, and that made me look for it in myself." (the chapter). The second file has nothing in its place except "For me, it has a very different effect." That's a man telling me about an effect instead of showing me a face. Ruth came to me in both versions, because you asked and I already had her. But in the chapter, the teacher gave me words for what Ruth did. In the second file she was just a memory.

"I don't want to make too much of that afternoon." (the chapter). Gone from the second, necessarily. It was the place I first trusted you.

"There's no bottom to what can be done to such a person." (the chapter). The second file doesn't have it, and so it also can't later say "Remember the threat my look carried, and how there was no bottom to it." That bottomlessness is what I've seen in families who've written someone off. I'm glad the chapter put it in.

"That's what I'd normally call "evil"—cruelty for its own sake; the thing I hate most in the world—and I'd needed them to know it." (the chapter) versus "It's what I'd normally call "evil"—the thing I hate most in the world—and I'd needed them to know it." (the second file). The chapter's "cruelty for its own sake" is what made me flinch and think of my sigh at Kate. Without it, in the second file, "evil" is just a word and I slid past it.

"Take a moment, and see if you can find what you were hiding it from." (the chapter) versus "Just try to feel into it, until you get a sense of what you were hiding it from." (the second file). "Feel into it" is therapy-speak and I winced. The chapter's version just asked me to look, and I did.

"That's why I call my better nature *myself*. Not because it's nicer. Because it's the one that's home." (the chapter). Missing from the second. That was one of my favorite sentences, and without it "what we *are*" is a claim with no warmth.

"I'll just go ahead and name what's happening." (the second file). The chapter's "Back to my enemy" is better. The second file's opening is throat-clearing.

"Neither half alone would be enough to get me what I need: both his reaction and my innocence." (the chapter) versus "Neither half alone would be enough." (the second file).
