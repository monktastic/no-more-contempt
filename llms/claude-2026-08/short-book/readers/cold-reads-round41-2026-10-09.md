Dear Aditya,

I read it twice through: once at my desk in the lab, once on the deck at home after Pieter went to bed. I'll go in order and say where I bought it, where I bought it tentatively, and where I stopped.

**The opening.** "Maybe you'll recognize something of yourself in it. Or maybe not." I liked that. It's a scientist's sentence: here's a hypothesis, test it. And the central claim, "contempt is the claim that you're a different kind of thing than me, but for it to land, you must be the same kind of thing, and part of you must know it," I bought tentatively. It's elegant, and I distrust elegance, so I held it at arm's length as you asked.

**Limbo.** The memory came at once, which annoyed me. In 2009, as a postdoc, I dropped three participants from an experience-sampling study. Their probe-caught mind-wandering rates killed the effect. I excluded them on a compliance criterion I wrote after I'd seen their data. I knew at the time. I didn't let myself know until a retreat in 2016, on about day nineteen, when it came up during a sitting and I couldn't put it down again.

Did it feel like new information? No. It felt like walking into a room I'd been walking past for seven years. So the core of your claim matched. But I have to tell you where I balked: "fully available to consciousness." That is my field, and the word "fully" is wrong, or at least loose. What I'd say is that the knowledge was experienced but not meta-aware: present, the way you can be mind-wandering without knowing you are. We have a whole literature on that gap. You don't need "fully," and some readers like me will stop trusting you at it. That was my first refusal, of the phrase, not of the thing. I could follow everything after it.

"If you did, did that happen passively?" No. I remember a student, Mei, asking at lab meeting why the N was 41 and not 44, and I remember changing the subject to the probe timing. That was not passive. That was quick and skilled.

"Limbo comes in degrees. Would the worse things be easier to remember in this way, or harder?" Harder. There are things I can feel the shape of and can't get to this way. I'll leave it there.

"Nobody ever looked honestly at something in themselves while being told they were worthless for having it." Bought outright. My teacher says nearly this, and it took me years to believe him.

Then: "*Everyone does it. It's not a big deal. Just this once.*" Those were almost exactly my reasons, word for word; the first one especially, because in 2009 everyone did. And "Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" landed hard. The part that knew the exclusion was wrong never made a case. It just sat there. The part that wanted the paper wrote two pages of justification in the methods section.

**The cashier.** The young man with the septum ring at King Soopers on Table Mesa. The kindness went with no reason at all. ### Stage 2

Aditya,

Second letter. I read the other version with the first one still warm, so this is partly a comparison and partly what the second one did on its own.

**Which did more, which treated me better.**

They split. The second file did more to me in three places, and each time it was a sharp hit. The chapter treated me better throughout, and kept me reading in a way the second file didn't. Around the middle of the second file I started reading like I read a press release: looking for the move, not the meaning.

The reason it treated me worse is simple. The chapter is in your voice: "I," your enemy, your teacher, your sigh. The second file is "you" and "he" almost all the way through, and it answers its own questions for me. "Does he just let you go?" and then "Of course not." "Does it make it *easier* or *harder* to confront what you already know?" and then "Harder, almost certainly." "I mean, *obviously*." "It's hard to do, isn't it?" "Because we **all** know it." I counted the "we all know"s and stopped at four. Each one is a door closing. The chapter's "Or maybe not" and its line about having no argument if nothing fits are what made me willing to do the work in the first place. The second file never gives me that out, and so I kept looking for one.

And this, near the end of the second file, made me put it down for a minute: "And it's what this book exists to provide you." That's a book selling itself to me. The chapter's version of the same move ("Sometimes it looks like me writing this book *at* people, instead of *for* them") is the opposite, and it's why I trusted the chapter.

**What the second file has that the chapter should recover.**

1. The record. This is the big one, and it's big for me in particular. The second file says of the two strangers: "And yet there will be no objective evidence of this. The record will show that the second stranger was *harsher*—and so he should have been the one that made it worse." And later: "It's like a parallel layer of communication happening *beneath* the words—one that can't be recorded on any ledger or in any transcript, and yet is louder than the stuff that can." And near the end: "And the objective facts are what we have learned to trust *more*. So the very thing causing the problem has hidden itself better."

I live by the record. Documents, transcripts, the paper trail. My whole case against Vance is what's on paper. Reading that the actual cause of the harm never makes it onto paper, and that my trust in paper is part of how it hides, was the most disturbing thing in either file. The chapter has it in one sentence ("Later, everyone will argue about the things that can be measured—who said or did what—obscuring the actual cause"), and I went past it. In the second file it's a thread, and it caught me three times. Anyone who trusts the measurable over the felt (that's most of the people I know in this city) needs this. I'd put at least the "ledger or transcript" sentence back.

2. The two strangers, on the receiving end, before you ever ask me to look at what I send. In the chapter I watched your encounter as a spectator and only got caught by the cold version. In the second file I was the one being caught, and Tom walked straight in. Then this: "You'd take that bet at any odds. But on what evidence? You'd never met him. You knew nothing about him. All you have to go on is that he's standing there, doing what he's doing." That landed hard. I was sure Tom was a hypocrite about rigor before I had any evidence about his rigor. All I had was the way he looked at me. That's a better version of the hypocrisy point than the one in the teacher story, because I worked it out myself instead of hearing you work it out.

And this, about the second stranger: "And if you had to guess: is *this* the kind of person who sneaks around trying to get away with stuff?" Hal. No. Never. I'd recover that question; it's the inverse of the Tom one and it does real work.

3. "Both people may have stopped your misbehavior, and so both will leave confident that what they did "worked."" The chapter says contempt "can sometimes stop people's misbehavior," but it says it late, as a concession. Here it comes right inside the strangers scene, where I'd just felt both of them, and it's not a concession, it's the point. It also answers the pushback I had in my first letter about exposure working.

4. "Do you see what incentive we might have to call it that?" About calling it "unconscious." Short and fair, and it's a question that leaves room. The chapter makes the same point much later and in your own case ("I desperately want to call this behavior *unconscious*"). I'd want both. The early one lets me catch it in my own Denise memory before you catch it in yours.

5. "And yet, despite "losing" that battle, notice that it never budged an inch. It kept on quietly knowing what it knew." That's true of Denise. Eight years and the drawer never stopped pushing back. The chapter has the arguing side and the silent side, but not this: that the silent side doesn't give up. The second file's closing callback ("It's still there in you, quietly knowing what it knows") depends on it, and that callback was the one ending of the second file I liked.

6. "Contempt is malice that believes it's righteous, and that's what makes it **maddening**." One sentence that does what the chapter's whole "He can see both halves" paragraph does. I'd trade.

7. "The plan is Machiavellian. But he's not the one executing it. He *cannot* be, because the plan requires his absence to function." Clearer than the chapter's "I couldn't have masterminded a plan in which two aspects of my mind coordinate so precisely." It also answers my "motivated reasoning" objection better: motivated reasoning doesn't require my absence.

8. "*Our conscience is the only thing that warns us when we've strayed from it.*" Good sentence. Not in the chapter.

9. "**The only reason we ever turn away is to enable harm, and we cannot intend harm without turning away**. Check both directions for yourself." In my first letter I said the step to "beings who must *turn away* to cause harm" asked more than the look had shown. This sentence states it plainly as a two-way claim and hands me the check. I tried both directions. The second direction (can't intend harm without turning away) held for Denise and for Kelsey. The first direction (the only reason we turn away is to enable harm) I'm not sure of. I turn away from my father's illness and that isn't to enable harm. 10. "When You Send It," this passage in particular: "Bring back the moment itself: the thing you said, or didn't have to say. There was a flash in it. You felt it go out of you, and you felt it land." I did, with Kelsey at the briefing, and I could feel it go out of me. It's more physical than the chapter's "Think of the last time you sent that look yourself." And then: "You didn't have to calculate that sequence. It's there in a flash." That is what "Nothing has to be computed" was trying to say in the chapter, and I didn't have to read it twice here.

11. "Notice how even the smallest flicker can feel dangerous to admit—out of proportion to its size." The chapter's "I don't prepare a defense for something inconsequential" is the same idea, and good, but it's about you. This one I could check on myself right away: the sigh at Margaux, which is nothing, and which I'd still rather not admit to her.

12. "It's the same faculty that let you see him. That sees anyone. Demote it, and you see a little less of everyone." This is the cost of becoming the cynic, said better than anywhere in the chapter. Nate needs this sentence.

**What the chapter was right to leave out or change.**

**Which one I'd pass on.**

The chapter, to Nate, as I said. If I could slip one page of the second file into it, it would be the two strangers and the paragraph about the record, because Nate believes in the record more than I do, and that's the page that would get under his guard.
