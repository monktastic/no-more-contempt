### Stage 1

Dear Aditya,

I read it in one sitting at the kitchen table and then went back through it with a pencil. You asked for what happened, so here it is, in order. Some of it will be awkward to hear from me, because the chapter found me.

**The opening.** "Maybe you'll recognize something of yourself in it. Or maybe not." That relaxed me. A writer who leaves the door open in the first paragraph usually means it. The central claim I took tentatively, as asked: "contempt is the claim that you're a different kind of thing than me, but for it to land, you must be the same kind of thing, and part of you must know it." It sounded clever before it sounded true. I let it sit.

**The memory.** I used a real one. When my daughter Adaeze was sixteen I read her journal. I told myself it was about safety, because she'd been coming home late and quiet. I didn't fully admit what I'd done until my own analysis, some years later, and I didn't tell her until she was twenty-three.

Did it feel like new information? No. It felt exactly like what you say: something I'd always known and hadn't let myself know. I remember the moment on the couch when I said it aloud, and the feeling was not "oh!" It was "yes, of course, that." So the phenomenon is mine.

But here is my first snag, and it's a professional one. "That knowledge was never unconscious." In my trade, "unconscious" doesn't mean what you're arguing against. What you're describing, knowing and not knowing at once, is something Freud gave a name to; my field has spent a century on it. So I bought the experience and refused the word, or rather I refused the fight with the word. It felt like you were knocking down a version of "unconscious" that no analyst I know holds. Later in the chapter I changed my mind somewhat (I'll come to it).

What I did like: "If that doesn't match anything in your memory, the rest of this won't make sense, and I'd rather you know that now." That's honest, and it made me trust you.

"Any time someone came close to exposing it... did you find a way to deflect? If you did, did that happen passively?" No, it did not happen passively. When Adaeze asked, the year after, whether I'd ever gone into her room, I said "Why would I?" That is not passive. That is a question built to end a conversation. I hadn't connected those two things until your question did it, and you didn't do it for me. You left it to me. Good.

"If seeing that makes you want to call yourself names, hold off. That's not owning up; it's another way to hide." I say a version of this to clients every month. It still landed, because it came at the moment I was reaching for the names.

"Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" This is the best kind of question you ask. My answer: the side that knew never argued in me at all. It sat in my stomach while the other side wrote memos. I felt free there.

**The cashier.** The pharmacist's assistant at the CVS on Germantown Avenue, a heavy-set woman named Denise who calls everyone "baby." The smile went without a reason. The sneer wouldn't go at all; I couldn't even get it to form. So the check worked. But it also showed me something you may not have meant it to. My cruelty doesn't look like a sneer. With my sister-in-law Grace it looks like warmth: careful, attentive, patient warmth. She calls it "being handled." That doesn't need a reason either, because it doesn't look like cruelty to anyone, including me. I wrote "what about cruelty that looks kind?" in the margin and kept reading.

"So some part of me needs to believe I'm made of 'better stuff.'" Tentatively bought. I'd grant it of myself more readily than of "us."

**The confrontation scene.** Believable as what a mind does, yes. Several lines are better than believable. "he's **bad**, dammit" made me laugh out loud, because that is the tone of it exactly. And "He can't prove I'm doing this, can he? No. And crucially, *neither can I*. So I know it's not on purpose." That one is the whole mechanism in three sentences, and I have heard it, nearly word for word, from people in my office who were mid-affair.

"*Ha!* Classic projection." I winced and then laughed again, because I have thought that very word about Grace. It's the right word to put in the narrator's mouth: clinical vocabulary used as a weapon. I wasn't sure you knew how much of my profession you indicted with it, but I'll take it.

Where I skimmed: the "invitation" paragraph, and "Who cares if I give him a little nudge to help him reveal what he truly is? It's a net benefit to the world." Those felt a degree more cartoonish than the rest, as if the narrator were writing his own prosecution. A real mind is slyer than that. "Surely that's not what I'm doing. I'm just being fierce with evil, right? Right." was perfect, though, and earned the "right? Right."

The cold version paragraph is where I actually live. But "a politeness with nothing behind it" isn't quite mine. Mine has a great deal behind it, all of it aimed. I'd have liked one sentence for those of us whose withdrawal comes dressed as attentiveness.

**"I'll just go ahead and name what's happening."** Insight, mostly, not lecture. "Neither half alone would get me what I need: his reaction, and my innocence." That's the sentence I'd carry away from this section. "The signal is the pretense that there is no signal" I had to read twice, and then a third time; I understood it on the third, and I'm not sure it isn't more elegant than it is clear.

Then this, which stopped me: "Deny it, and you're the kind of person who does. Get angry, and that's proof too. Stay calm, and you don't even care. Apologize, and it's too convenient." I've been on that end. But more uncomfortably, I've *been* that end, and not only with Grace. In my field there's a word for whatever a client does that disagrees with the therapist: resistance. "The stories keep changing; the conclusion never does." I'll be thinking about that in sessions.

"We never tell ourselves 'I'm going to ignore my conscience.'" Yes. The sentence after it stumbles; the semicolon in "just an advisor that can be vetoed; one more voice" read like a typo.

**The two checks.** Is there something satisfying in seeing evil violently punished? Yes. I'm a Quaker and I'll still say yes.

The worst enemy took me a while, and the delay was its own information. My first thought was that I don't have enemies, which is what a Quaker therapist would think, and which is a lie. The real one is a former colleague, a psychiatrist I'll call Len, who once said "classic borderline" about a young woman *in her hearing*, in an intake meeting, and then went on with his coffee. I filed him away twenty years ago and he has stayed filed.

The pill or the vengeance. I chose the pill at once, and felt good about choosing it. Then I noticed what I wanted to go with the pill: I wanted him to know he'd needed it. I wanted him changed *and* to feel the full weight of what he'd been. That is the itch, dressed in white. So yes, some part of me is itching. "I'll be honest: there is in me." Your admitting it first is what let me find it. That's the line in the chapter that did the most work on me, and it's six words.

**Planting evidence.** "Conscious yet unaware: that's the limbo state." Here I relented on the word "unconscious," a little. You aren't fighting my field's concept, I think; you're fighting the alibi the word becomes in ordinary mouths, "I had no idea." I've watched people use it that way in my office for twenty years, and colleagues too, and, God help me, myself. When you later write "I desperately want to call this part of myself *unconscious*," I believed you, and I believed it of me.

**The part that "acts as if it has goals of its own."** I heard something older than psychology in it. Early Friends talked like this. Fox wrote of "an ocean of darkness and death" and an ocean of light over it, and he didn't mean a metaphor, quite, but he didn't mean a creature either. So I didn't refuse it. And the next paragraph came in time, just: "None of this lets me off the hook, though. It's my mind, and I answer for what it does." Without that sentence I'd have closed up, because "a part of me did it" is the most common sentence in my working week and it is usually the start of an excuse.

But this is the first place I refused something outright. Two paragraphs later: "It reveals itself to them through my eyes while condemning the same thing in them, knowing they will recognize what I'm doing, but without recourse." Now the part *knows* things and *reveals itself*. You took the disclaimer back almost as soon as you'd given it. I stopped granting the agent there. I could still follow everything after, because I kept translating "it" back into "I," and the chapter read fine that way. Better, actually.

**Better nature as "what we most fundamentally are."** I found this in myself, but I should say why that's cheap for me: it is close to the oldest thing my tradition says, that there is that of God in everyone. So it didn't sound grandiose to me. I suspect it will to readers without a tradition behind them, and I noticed you stated it as a finding rather than leading me to it. "Here's what I think" is honest, and I'd rather that than pretending I got there myself.

The reverse question did more: "is there anything you hide from yourself that your better nature wouldn't object to?" I tried it. I hide my ambition. I wanted, badly, to be made a training analyst, and I hid how badly from the part of me that thinks ambition is unseemly in a Friend. Do I feel remorse for that? No. I can look at it squarely. So your distinction held, on me. And "did it feel like losing something of yourself, or like getting something back?" Getting something back. When I told Adaeze about the journal she was angry for a week, and I felt more like myself that week than I had in years.

**The saying about integrity.** "But someone always was: me." I believed this one in my body. In meeting we'd call that the inward witness. Thirty years of sitting in silence and the one thing I can report with confidence is that you are never unobserved in there.

**The person who corrected me.** An elder in our meeting, Dorothy, now dead. After meeting one First Day I'd made a remark at the coffee table, something dry, about a Friend whose ministry ran long every week. Dorothy took my elbow and said, "Thee doesn't get to decide whom the Spirit uses." She was fierce. She was not gentle. And it didn't make me dig in. It felt exactly as you say: "*you're better than this*." I've thought about that sentence of hers for fifteen years.

**The time I corrected someone badly.** A supervisee, Rachel, early in my career as a supervisor. I told her that her case formulation was "undergraduate." She never brought me a difficult case again. Years later, with a different young clinician making the same mistake, I asked her what she thought the client was afraid of, and waited. That worked. So yes. Though I'd note that with Rachel I'd never have called what I did contempt. I'd have called it standards.

**Common knowledge.** I followed the argument from hypocrisy to a shared standard and I think it's the most original thing in the chapter. "Hypocrisy only means something between people who share a standard—and since we're enemies, we might share none." That's clean. What surprised me was that the shared standard turns out to be not a moral code at all but turning away itself. That's what both of us recognize. I hadn't thought of it that way and I think it's true.

But "each knows that the other knows that the other knows" and then "etc." twice lost me for a paragraph. I know the logic; it still read like a seminar.

The hall of mirrors, yes: Grace at Thanksgiving three years ago, the last one she came to, when I asked how she was "really doing" and she looked at me and both of us knew, and neither could say it without sounding mad.

Where someone's contempt reached in me: a senior analyst at a case conference, years ago, who said "Interesting" about my formulation and smiled at the man beside him. Not my pride. Pride I could have handled. It reached the place that wondered whether a Nigerian woman with an accent belonged in that room at all. Is that "the *you* that knows you count"? I tested it, and I think it is, in this way: the hurt was so sharp because some part of me knew I counted and he was addressing *that*. You don't wound nobody. I took it tentatively.

**The last time I sent that look.** Two Sundays ago in meeting. A Friend I'll call Frank rose for the third week running to speak about his garden. I lowered my eyes. Not a look, a lowering. But I lowered them a beat late, so that he'd catch it. Did I know he'd read it? Yes. Did I give myself an out? Yes: I was centering down. Did I count on his seeing that too? I think I did. That question of yours caught me fully. I put the pencil down.

**The unrolled message** I refused. "I want you to know that I know this about you, so that you can feel the maddening hypocrisy of what I'm doing to you." It's too explicit, too much intention spelled out. Whatever my lowered eyes said to Frank, it wasn't that; it was nearer to "you're tiresome and I'm above it." And "were you surprised that I knew you needed limbo to cause harm? If not, why not?" felt pleased with itself. It's the one place in the chapter I felt a trap closing rather than a question opening.

Then the best sentence in the chapter, for me: "Love and contempt are addressed to the same place. One says *I know you're in there.* The other says *there's nobody in there*, and they both say it to the somebody who is." I thought of Grace. I'm still thinking of Grace.

"It's hard to describe the excruciating pain I experienced the first time I saw this." I believed you. Honest self-report like that is what keeps me reading through the parts that push. And "Maybe I'm wrong, and this isn't how it works in others" gave me room again right when I needed it.

"My better nature *is* what sees yours." Fox again: answering that of God in everyone. I didn't need persuading. A reader without that phrase in her head might.

The gaze check did little new for me after the case-conference memory; I'd already done it. "I'm not saying you're as bad as him." I didn't need that reassurance and slightly resented being given it. The remorse check ("never for having been decent") held.

**"The harm I caused my enemy doesn't stop at him" to the end.** "the kid who watches him do it" caught me; I thought of Len's residents.

But the paragraph that did the most to me in the whole chapter is this one: "If I try to pretend I see their 'better nature' when I can't, I'm liable to cause more harm. A mind deeply lost in self-deception knows how to exploit a pretense like that." That is me and Grace. My careful warmth is a pretense of seeing her better nature. She can tell. "Being handled" is her name for the pretense. I have been doing to her a refined version of the thing in your confrontation scene, and calling it patience. I sat with that for a long time. I can't say you showed it to me, exactly. You left a space and it walked in.

"Whether that number ever goes to zero is something you can discover for yourself." Good. Room again.

"Other kinds of contempt" caught me twice. "When I'm sure they had no idea, I've made them a machine." That is the diagnostic stance, and it is what I hold against colleagues who treat clients as diagnoses, and it is what I do to those colleagues, and it's what I do to Grace when I think of her as "very defended." Then "I send it to people I love." My daughter told me I listen like a judge. I'd taken that as a comment on my manner. Your sentence about the sigh "dressed up to make me seem like the reasonable one" made it a comment on something else.

**Did it end in the right place?** The last paragraph is right: "What I need in order to look is what my enemy needed from me, and what the corrector with integrity gave me: to be seen as more than what I do." That's an ending. But the chapter felt to me as if it ended twice. "Whether that number ever goes to zero" had the cadence of a close, and "Other kinds of contempt" then reads a little like an appendix, though it was some of the most useful material for me. I'd rather it came earlier, or was woven in.

**Length.** It drags in three places. The run from "If I'd looked carefully" through "the right to be *more* cruel to them" says again what the confrontation scene already showed. The block beginning "To see him as something *less*" through "this trap is nearly unavoidable" restates. And Common knowledge circles: "No wonder I must hide it so carefully from myself, and call it 'unconscious'" repeats what you'd said a page earlier. If I had to cut a fifth: those three passages, the invitation and nudge paragraphs, the unrolled message, and the "etc."s. I'd keep every question.

**Words.** "Classic projection" made me wince, as intended. "Double down" and "here's the thing" sounded like someone other than you. "a little darkness I sprinkle" is too cute for what it describes. "Plant the evidence" and "goal-oriented" are a prosecutor's and an engineer's words, respectively, in a chapter that's otherwise neither. "Limbo" I accepted, though it carries a whiff of the catechism. "Corruption" I'll accept from a man who also wrote "excruciating pain."

**Last.** Was it obvious? A good deal of it I knew, professionally and religiously: disavowal, projection, that of God in everyone, the elder's correction. If you'd told me any one of those ideas I'd have nodded and turned the page. What I didn't have was them in one line, so that the warmth I give Grace sits on the same line as the look the narrator gives his enemy. That made something I already knew consequential.

What I'll do this week: on Sunday I will not lower my eyes at Frank. And I'll write to Grace, a short note, and I'll try to write it without the careful voice. I don't yet know what that voice sounds like when it's gone. Maybe that's what I'll find out.

Would I pass it on? Not to Adaeze; giving it to her would be handling her. To Ellen, in my peer consultation group, yes. She'll fight you on "unconscious" for an hour, and that's the conversation I want to have, because I think by the end of it she'll find she's on your side and so will I.

With gratitude, and a little rawness,
Miriam

### Stage 2

Dear Aditya,

I've now read the second file. Reading the same chapter twice, a day apart, is a strange experience. The second time I wasn't doing the checks so much as watching them work, and noticing where I'd have answered differently if I'd met the other wording first.

**Which did more, and which treated me better.** The chapter did more. It's tighter where it matters most, it has two or three sentences the second file lacks that I'd fight to keep, and its narrator is slyer. But the second file treated me better in a few specific places, small ones, the kind of place where a reader decides whether she's being asked or steered. I'll go through them.

**What the second file has that the chapter should recover.**

The cashier question. The chapter asks: "Which one of those needed a reason before it would go?" The second file asks: "Did either one need a reason before it would go?" The second is the honest question. The chapter's version tells me one of them did, and I'm only choosing which. When I tried it on Denise at the pharmacy, my real answer was odd: the sneer didn't need a reason so much as it wouldn't form at all. "Did either one" has room for that answer. "Which one" doesn't. It's a one-word difference and it's the difference between a question and a quiz.

"Now: was it an accident, or was it a *motivated* turning-away?" (the second file). This stands alone on its line after "If I'd looked carefully," and I wanted it in the chapter. It hands me the question before you answer it. In the chapter you go straight to "And look what it got me," which is the answer, so I never get a moment to put the question to my own case. With this line I'd have had a breath to ask it about Grace, and I'd have got there before you did. That's the kind of moment that opens me.

"In fact, I *can't*, when I can clearly see myself in them." (the second file). I want to be careful here, because I don't think the chapter should take the paragraph this comes from. But this one sentence is honest first-person report of the kind I trust, and it's stronger than the chapter's "harming them is difficult." Difficult is a hedge. "I *can't*" is a man telling me something about himself that I can test against myself, and when I test it, it's true of me with Denise and false of me with Grace, which is exactly where I want to be sent.

"Here's something you can check: in moments where you're feeling fully whole—not a trace of either shame or pride: is it easier to forgive others?" (the second file). I tried this one, and it did something. The moments I can think of when I've been most whole are in a gathered meeting, the rare First Days when the silence goes all the way down. In those moments, yes, Frank's garden ministry is not tiresome. It's just Frank, who loves his garden. That's verifiable in my own interior, which is what you do best. The second half of the question, "and to help correct their behavior in a way that actually works?", leans on me a bit, and "fully whole" is vague. But the check itself is good and the chapter has nothing quite like it.

"Watch what happens when I believe that." (the second file). I'm less sure of this one. It's a bridge into the confrontation and it works as one. But the chapter's "I'm usually not aware of this need. That doesn't stop it from shaping my behavior." is better self-report. If I had to choose, I'd keep the chapter's and not add this.

The placement of the remorse asymmetry. The second file puts "I've felt remorse for harm I've done, but never for having been decent" right after the cashier, followed by "Have you? Can you imagine it even in the worst person?" Early, it gives me a second test of my better nature before you ask me to believe anything about it. In the chapter it comes late, after the gaze check, where it felt like one more piece of evidence on a pile I'd already accepted. I'd move it up. ("Have you?" is also a better question than anything the chapter puts there.)

The second file's "I sense something underhanded in my behavior—that I'm provoking him in a way that I'll later hold against him" names the trick earlier and more exactly than the chapter's "I sense something disingenuous in my behavior." I'm of two minds. It explains, and I've told you I shut down when a writer explains what he should show. But as a description of the narrator's half-awareness, it's accurate: that's what the half-awareness actually contains. If you keep "disingenuous," keep it; if you want the reader to catch the scheme in the narrator's own voice before the "I'll just go ahead and name" section, this does it.

**What the chapter was right to leave out or change.**

The central claim. The second file has "Contempt is the claim that you're a different kind of thing than me, but for it to land, you must be the same kind of thing." The chapter adds "and part of you must know it." I first felt that addition as presumptuous. Having now read the chapter without it, I see it's the hinge of everything, the limbo and the common knowledge both. Without it the opening promises less than the chapter delivers. Keep the chapter's.

"Surely that's not what I'm doing. I'm just being fierce with evil, right? Right." (the chapter). The second file has "Surely that's not what I'm doing. I'm only holding him to account." and loses the "right? Right." That little exchange is the most human moment in the scene. It's the sound of a mind talking itself down. Keep it.

"I've denied him the ability to prove I've done anything wrong." (the chapter) against "He is denied the ability to prove I've done anything." (the second file). The second is passive and hides who did the denying, which is precisely the move the chapter is about. The chapter is right.

"And that lie is exactly what lets me act on it, in ways that I almost never recognize." (the chapter) against "Confined to my skull. And that lie is exactly how it escapes my skull, causing harm." (the second file). The skull is vivid and a little pleased with itself. "In ways that I almost never recognize" is a confession, and confession is what I trust. The chapter is right.

The chapter's "As best I can tell, most of us don't want to harm each other... All they do is distract us from the fact that we've turned away from the part of ourselves that wouldn't allow it." The second file opens that section with "Why do we have to hide such knowledge from ourselves?" and then makes it about the narrator alone. The chapter's idea, that reasons don't permit the harm but only distract us from what we've turned from, is the better insight. It's the one I'll use in my office. (Just take the "I *can't*" sentence back into it.)

"How is it possible that we all know this about each other? It's because my better nature *is* what sees yours." (the chapter). The second file doesn't have this paragraph. It answers a question the Common knowledge section raises and otherwise leaves hanging, and for me, with Fox in my ear, it's the floor the whole section stands on. Keep it.

"If you still feel a fundamental difference between you and your worst enemy, notice which part of you needs it to be there. In me, it's the one that wants to excuse my contempt." (the chapter). Missing from the second file. It pushes, but the second sentence pays for the push by turning it on yourself first. Keep it.

The chapter hedges where the second file asserts: "as best I can tell there's only one thing in us" (the chapter) against the second file's flat "there's only one thing in us that is trying to sense its way toward it." The "as best I can tell" earns its four words.

**Order and length.**

The biggest structural difference is where you name what's happening. In the chapter, "I'll just go ahead and name what's happening" comes straight after the confrontation and the cold-version paragraph. In the second file, his hardening comes first, then "If I'd looked carefully," then the accident-or-motive question, then the two checks, and only then the naming. Reading both, I prefer the second file's order in one respect: I get to feel the consequence (he hardens, I'm relieved) and get asked the question before I'm told the mechanism. That's showing before explaining. But the chapter's order put "Neither half alone would get me what I need: his reaction, and my innocence" right after the scene, while the scene was still warm in me, and that's why it landed as hard as it did. I'd try the second file's order with the question kept and the naming still close behind it, and then see.

The second file also moves "I saw *genuine darkness* in my enemy, and my desire to stop him came from a good place" down to the block that begins "To see him as something *less*." There it gives that block a reason to exist; in the chapter, I'd marked that block as repetition and would have cut it. Where the sentence sits in the chapter, opening the naming, it's better. Either way the block after it still restates what came before.

On length, they're near enough the same. The comparison showed me that the repetitions I flagged in the first letter are in both, word for word: "If I'd looked carefully," the "better kind of thing" being "erased," the "No wonder I must hide it" paragraph, the "etc."s. Those weren't drafting accidents, then; they're load you've chosen to carry. I'd still put them down.

One more thing the comparison showed me. Every time the two versions differ, the better one is the one where you're telling me something about yourself or asking me something with room in it. Every time a version is worse, it's because it explains, or hides an actor, or decides my answer for me. You already know how to write the better kind. Both files prove it.

Miriam

I did test it, though. My neighbor, Mrs. Pruitt, calls the police on my grandson and the boys playing ball in the street. Police. On children. For a ball. In this country, for boys that color, you know what that can mean. I smile at her at the mailbox with my church smile, and that smile says everything you wrote about. I asked myself, if I take the poison out, does the fierceness stay? I believe it does. I can tell her flat out that calling the police on children could get one killed and I won't have it. I don't need the smile for that. The smile is for me. But I'll be honest with you, I'd watch close how a white reader takes this chapter. One of them could use it on me: "Don't you look at me with contempt" while she's dialing. You didn't say that, but somebody could hear it.

**The time I corrected somebody badly.** My first years on the route I hollered. I'd stop the bus and stand up and let them have it in front of everybody. Now I pull over, cut the engine, walk to the boy's seat, and talk low so only he can hear. It works ten times better. I didn't know why until your chapter. Hollering in front of everybody was me telling the whole bus what he was.

**Common knowledge.** I followed the argument from hypocrisy to a shared standard. It held for me. Of course a hypocrite needs a standard. You can't be a hypocrite about something nobody believes in. The parents who cuss me out at the stop know exactly what's decent; that's why they get so hot when I don't answer them. "Hypocrisy only means something between people who share a standard—and since we're enemies, we might share none." Then you said there's one we share. Yes. That's the law on the heart.

Where it lost me for a minute: "And it only reads as a signal (and not a slip) if each knows that the other knows that the other knows." That's a lot of knowing. I got the meaning but my head spun. The "hall of mirrors" paragraph after it says the same thing plainer, and the plain one is better.

What surprised me: "Love and contempt are addressed to the same place. One says I know you're in there. The other says there's nobody in there, and they both say it to the somebody who is." I read that out loud in my kitchen. That's the truest thing in the chapter. That's what Mother Ruth Ann did, and that's what I do in the mirror.

And "In the opening, were you surprised that I knew you needed limbo to cause harm? If not, why not?" That was clever. I wasn't surprised. You got me there.

**Where someone's contempt reached in me.** At Calvin's funeral, Vera looked at me across the fellowship hall like I was the help. Like I'd been a nurse she hadn't had to pay. It didn't hit my pride. Pride I could have handled. It went for the part of me that knew I loved that man and did right by him. It tried to say I didn't count. You're right that it reaches further in than pride.

**The last time I sent that look.** Here it is. There's a boy on my route, Tavion, eleven years old, smart mouth, and his mama cussed me at the stop in front of all the children back in August. Ever since, when Tavion acts up, I don't say a word. I look at him in that big mirror. Slow and steady. Last month he told the principal I hate him. When she called me in I said, "I have never said one unkind word to that child." And that's true. And it's a lie.

You asked: did you mean them to know you meant it, while giving yourself an out? Yes. That's what the mirror is for. Did I count on him seeing that, so he couldn't name it without sounding crazy? Yes. An eleven-year-old boy telling the principal "she looks at me" sounds crazy. I knew it would. I wasn't looking at Tavion. I was looking at his mama through him. He's eleven.

I've been trying to decide since Thursday whether I hate him. I don't. But I can see why he thinks so. That's the part of the chapter that's going to change something this week.

**The dehumanizing gaze.** I've had it plenty. A man at the bank who looked through me. And yes, I could feel him gone somewhere, absent, like you said. "Is it possible that you look a little bit like this when treating others with contempt?" My granddaughter Kiana asked me at Easter why I "look at Aunt Vera like that." So, yes. I didn't need you to ask me. A nine-year-old already did.

I'll tell you where I felt pushed, though. "If you still feel a fundamental difference between you and your worst enemy, notice which part of you needs it to be there." That one doesn't leave me anywhere to stand. If I still feel the difference, it's my bad part talking. I didn't care for that. The rest of the time you left me room. "Whether that number ever goes to zero is something you can discover for yourself." That's the kind of room I mean.

**From "The harm I caused my enemy doesn't stop at him."** This caught me, because I drive children. "the next stranger he won't give the benefit of the doubt, the kid who watches him do it." I have forty-some kids watching me every morning. They saw me stare down Tavion's mama. Every one of them learned something.

And the part about people we love. "The sigh that tells them what I really think of them, dressed up to make me seem like the reasonable one." I do that to my daughter Monique when she's late picking up the babies. "For one second the verdict flashes through my eyes, and I make sure they see it, and then I go back to loving them as if nothing had happened." That's me. "It has the same flavor as what we call evil." That's strong, and I didn't push it away.

Did it end at the right place? The last line is right: "to be seen as more than what I do." That's grace. That's where it should end. But it felt like it ended once before that, at "Whether that number ever goes to zero is something you can discover for yourself," and then started up again with "Other kinds of contempt." That last section is good, maybe the most useful for regular folks, but it felt like an extra plate after I'd pushed back from the table.

**One more thing about your confession.** You admit your sin, and that opened me. But "It's hard to describe the excruciating pain I experienced the first time I saw this" made me want to ask, who? Who was your enemy? In church when you testify, you say what you did. Your "bad person" in the confrontation doesn't have a face. I gave you Vera and Tavion and Odessa. I'd have liked one of yours.

**Length.** It dragged in the second half of Common knowledge, from "How is it possible that we all know this about each other?" through "Whatever 'good' turns out to mean." It's true, but you'd already said it. "Better kind of thing" shows up so many times I started counting. "Turned away" too. The part right before the corrector, "Believing I'm fundamentally different and special is inherently self-deceptive," says again what the confrontation already showed me. If you made me cut a fifth, I'd cut that, the "invitation" paragraph, the "each knows that the other knows" stuff, and the paragraph that starts "Here's what I've found in myself," which tells me what to do before I'm ready. Keep every question. The questions are what worked.

**Words.** "Limbo" is fine; I know what it means. "Goal-oriented," "inherently," "entrenching," "plausibly deniable," "commonality": I know them, but they made me feel like I was reading something for work. "Classic projection" made me wince. The cussing didn't.

**Last.** Was it obvious? Some of it. Jesus already said take the beam out of your own eye before you go after the mote in your brother's. I've heard a hundred sermons on it. What I hadn't put together is that the stare with the out built in, "I never said one word," is the beam. I thought the beam was something I did. It's something I do and make sure I can't be caught doing.

This week: Monday morning I'm going to say good morning to Tavion by name when he gets on, and look at him like he's eleven, because he is. I don't know if I'll do anything about Vera yet. And if Kiana asks me again how I look at her aunt, I'm going to tell her the truth.

Would I pass it on? To Pastor Reed, yes, with a note about the language. Not to Sister Bernice, because she'd read it and come back with a list of everybody else who needs it. And maybe to Monique, if I can give it to her without my face doing the thing.

Sincerely,
Donna Pierce

### Stage 2

Dear Aditya,

I read the second file the next evening. It's long. I'll tell you plain what it did.

**Which did more, and which treated me better.** The second file did more to me in its first half. The chapter treated me better all the way through. They're not the same thing, and here's why.

The second file starts me on the receiving end. A stranger catches me and comes at me for what I am. I didn't have to imagine that. I've had it. Where the chapter makes me watch you be the one with the look, the second file lets me be the one getting it first, and I'm a woman who has been getting it for fifty-eight years. So when I read "Of course not. Deny it, and you're the kind of person who does." in the second file, I was all the way in. You had me on your side before you asked anything of me.

Then the second stranger. I put Mother Ruth Ann in that spot again, but this time she stood in front of me as a whole scene, not a memory you asked for halfway down the page. It hit harder.

But the chapter treated me better because you were in it. In the chapter you say "I'll be honest: there is in me," and you put yourself in the confrontation and let me watch you lie to yourself. In the second file it's "he" and "you" the whole way. The first stranger does the wrong, and then I do the wrong in "When You Send It," and you, Aditya, never stand up and testify. A writer who confesses his own sin, I'll follow a long way. A writer who tells me what I did, I start checking his hands. By the end of the second file I was checking your hands.

And the second file talks for me more. "Harder, almost certainly." "It's hard to do, isn't it?" "I mean, obviously. Why trust something that can't even justify itself?" That last one I could hear you being smart, and I didn't care for it. The chapter asks and waits more.

**What the second file has that the chapter should get back.** More than I expected. I'll go in order.

"Do you see what incentive we might have to call it that?" from the second file, about calling things "unconscious." That's a good question and it's short. The chapter gets to the same place much later, and does it by telling me.

"If all you could hear was your own conscience—no justifications to hide behind—could you have even done what you did?" from the second file. When I put that to the prayer request I told on Odessa, no. I could not have. That question does the work of a whole paragraph in the chapter.

"Would the big ones be easier to recall, or harder?" from the second file. That one stopped me. I thought about the big things I might not be remembering. That scared me a little, in a useful way.

The two strangers, together, as one picture. The chapter has the corrector with integrity, but it comes after a lot of argument, and it's a memory I have to go get. In the second file they're side by side and I could feel the difference in my own body. Especially this, from the second file: "Whatever he was standing up for wasn't his. It wasn't a rule, or a side. It was something you were already on the side of, and he was only reminding you." The chapter only says "Even if they have different politics, religion, creed: something about their behavior feels deeply aligned." "Deeply aligned" is a work word. "Something you were already on the side of" is how Mother Ruth Ann felt. And "You knew you couldn't win against it—and more importantly, you didn't want to." That's exactly true. I didn't want to win against her.

"And if you had to guess: is this the kind of person who sneaks around trying to get away with stuff?" from the second file. Good, and short. It turned me back to my own mirror without pushing.

"You'd take that bet at any odds. But on what evidence?" from the second file. About the first stranger. That's how I am with Vera. I'd bet my house she's a hypocrite. On what evidence? On the look. That's the whole thing.

This one most of all, from the second file: "The record will show that the second stranger was harsher—and so he should have been the one that made it worse." I wrote you about the principal's office. "I have never said one unkind word to that child." The record shows that. The record from my first years, when I hollered, would show I was the harsh one. The record has it backwards, and I've been standing behind the record. The chapter has "plausibly deniable," which says the same in a lawyer's word. The record line I felt.

"Could you have named what was happening out loud? What would you have said—you're being mean? How would that have turned out?" from the second file. That's Tavion. He named it out loud. "She looks at me." And it turned out exactly the way you'd guess. Eleven years old and he tried anyway. That question made me sorrier for him than anything in the chapter.

"He was judging you from the very state he was judging you for." from the second file. That's preaching. That's the beam and the mote in eleven words, and it's the line I'd keep if I could keep one.

"Our conscience is the only thing that warns us when we've strayed from it." from the second file. Simple and true.

"That's what turning away is: refusing to see the humanity in someone. It isn't a matter of where your eyes are. You can do it staring straight at them." from the second file. Aditya, that's the bus mirror. In the chapter "turning away" kept bothering me a little, because I don't turn away from Tavion. I look right at him. I look at him hard. This sentence fixed that for me. The chapter needs it.

From "When You Send It" in the second file: "Somebody who had it coming." And "It felt like justice, a little sweeter than justice needs to be." That's the truest thing either file says about me. Tavion's mama had it coming. And the stare was sweeter than justice needed. That sentence is plain and it's Scripture-shaped without quoting any. Put it in the chapter.

"The flash itself was never recorded." from the second file. Goes with the record line.

"It's the thing we call evil. Not as a judgement, but as the very essence of what "evil" means: to turn away from the life in front of us in order to pursue our own ends." from the second file. I liked the word evil said plain. The chapter only gets as far as "It has the same flavor as what we call evil." I'm a church woman; I'm not scared of the word. And that definition is one I could teach in Sunday school.

"Stopping bad people surely is good." from the second file. I needed that said out loud, for Mrs. Pruitt's sake. The chapter has "The fierceness stays; only the poison goes," which I still like better, but the second file also says "We confuse "fierce" with "dark" not because we truly don't know the difference, but because we need to not know it." That's sharp and I think it's true of me.

And the ending of the second file. "It's still there in you, quietly knowing what it knows, patiently waiting to be remembered." Then "Just as it is in him." That's the still small voice. That's a better last line than either of the chapter's two endings, because it ends on him. It ends on Vera. It made me think Vera has it in her too, waiting. I didn't want to think that, and I thought it anyway.

**What the chapter was right to leave out or change.**

"then fuck it. Game on." I'm sorry, no. I let "shit" and "dammit" go in the chapter. That one I can't hand to my pastor, and I wouldn't hand it to anybody at my church. The chapter's version, "why the hell shouldn't I?", says the same thing.

The long speech in "The Taunt," from "We both know exactly what I'm doing to you" down to "Good luck." It read like a movie villain. The last line, "I know you can hear this, but you can't prove it. Good luck.", I knew. The rest was too much. The chapter's shorter version was enough.

"The plan is Machiavellian." I know the word. Nobody on my bus route does. "Commandeers," "externalize," "telegraphing itself" too. The second file has more fancy words than the chapter, and they come right when I'm tired.

"And it's what this book exists to provide you." That sounded like the back of a book at Walmart. The chapter never says that, and it's better for it.

"Try this: go out in public, find a stranger, and shoot them a dehumanizing gaze. It's hard to do, isn't it?" I'm not going to do that, and I'm not sure it's hard. With a stranger, maybe. With a parent who cussed me in front of children, it's the easiest thing in the world. The chapter's cashier check asks the same thing better, because it lets me answer.

"The Loop" and "The Tragedy" in the second file go around the same ground the earlier sections already covered. I skimmed most of "The Loop." The chapter's "Common knowledge" with the hypocrisy argument is better than the second file's way of getting to common knowledge. In the chapter I could follow the steps: a hypocrite needs a standard, so we share one. In the second file you mostly tell me "Because we all know it."

The chapter was also right to say "I'm not saying you're as bad as him." The second file doesn't have anything like that, and when "When You Send It" turned on me, I felt it.

**Order and length.** The second file showed me the order matters more than I thought. Starting me on the receiving end, with the two strangers, got me in the door as a woman who's been looked at that way. If the chapter opened its argument with the two strangers, then put you in the confrontation with your "I," then turned to me, I think I'd have gotten the best of both: I'm on your side first, then I watch you confess, then I'm ready to look in my own mirror. The second file turns the "you" on me after three hundred lines of me being the injured one, and the switch felt like an ambush. In the chapter you went first, so it didn't.

Length: the second file is too long. The chapter is about the right length, maybe a fifth long, like I said. If you take the lines I listed from the second file, take them as lines, not sections. Most of what's good in the second file is one or two sentences sitting in a lot of explaining.

Sincerely,
Donna Pierce
