## Paul (57, philosopher of mind, self-deception and agency; raised Lutheran, agnostic)

### Stage 1

Dear Aditya,

I read the chapter on Thursday evening at the kitchen table. Ingrid had gone up early. I did the exercises as you asked, with real people, so this letter has names in it. I'll go in order and tell you where I bought, where I bought on credit, and where I stopped.

**The opening and the first memory**

Your central claim, "contempt is the claim that you're a different kind of thing than me, but for it to land, you must be the same kind of thing, and part of you must know it", I recognized as a cousin of things I teach: Strawson on the reactive attitudes, Darwall on address. Contempt has to be addressed to a person, or it isn't contempt. So the first half I granted at once. The second half, "part of you must know it", is the expensive part. I held it on credit, as you asked.

Then the memory. You list "took credit for someone else's work" first among your examples, and I had one before I'd finished the sentence. In 2004 a graduate student of mine, Ruth Abernethy, said in seminar that self-deception is a refusal to finish an inference you've already begun. I used that line, nearly word for word, in a talk at the Eastern APA that December. It went into the published version. There is no footnote. Ruth is a professor in London, Ontario now. I have never told her.

Did the knowledge feel like new information when I finally owned it? No. It felt like something I'd been walking around for twenty years, the way you walk around a coffee table in the dark.

"Here's the first claim, and everything else rests on it: that knowledge was never unconscious." I should tell you that professionally I sit near Al Mele, who argues that self-deception doesn't require holding both beliefs at once; motivated bias will do. Your claim is a dual-belief claim, and I'd have argued with it in a seminar. But I wasn't in a seminar. I was in my own memory, and the memory doesn't match Mele. I knew. So I bought it for my case and held the general form on credit. "If that doesn't match anything in your memory, the rest of this won't make sense, and I'd rather you know that now." That sentence did more for my trust than anything else on the first page. You gave me an exit and I didn't need it.

The deflection question: yes. About a year after the talk, a colleague said, over lunch, that my line sounded like something Ruth would say. I said the idea was "in the air in that literature." It was not passive. I chose those words; I remember choosing them.

Worse things harder to remember this way? Harder. I'm fairly sure I have one I can't quite get to.

"Nobody ever looked honestly at something in themselves while being told they were worthless for having it." I was raised Lutheran in Minnesota, and I grew up inside a theology that tells you you're a worm before it tells you you're forgiven. This sentence is true of me. "Nobody ever" is a big quantifier, and I noticed it, and I let it go, because I couldn't find the counterexample in myself.

Before or after reasoning: before. The reasons came after, and they were exactly the type you give: Ruth would have said it in print eventually, I'd shaped her thinking, it was in the air. The side that knew never argued. Bought.

"Why would we only ever need limbo when we're causing harm?" Here I tried hard, because it's the sort of claim I'm paid to break. My father hid from himself, for years, that he had forgiven his brother. He needed to be the wronged party. That's hiding a decency from yourself. But when I looked at it, what the hiding protected was his grievance, and the grievance was doing harm: to his brother, to the family at Christmas. So I couldn't make it a counterexample. Bought tentatively.

**The cashier and the remorse question**

The cashier was the woman at the Loblaws on Bloor, mid-fifties, reading glasses on a chain. The smile went. The sneer would not go until I gave her something: I found myself imagining she'd been short with the man ahead of me. I invented a reason in order to sneer. That stopped me for a minute. "Did either one need a reason before it would go?" is a good question because it leaves the finding to me, and the finding was mine.

Remorse in reverse. My first thought was Huckleberry Finn, which Jonathan Bennett made famous in my field: Huck lies awake sick about helping Jim escape. But you'd already named it: "guilt at breaking a rule I was raised on". That is exactly Huck's case, and Bennett's point is that Huck's sympathy is the better faculty and his "conscience" is the rule. So your distinction holds where I'd expected it to break. Then Himmler, who told his officers at Posen that the hard thing was to stay "decent" while doing it. That isn't remorse for kindness; it's a man training himself out of the faculty you're describing. It supports you. "This suggests" is the right verb there. Bought tentatively.

**The Encounter**

Believable as a mind? Yes, mostly. But the hot version isn't my vice. I don't confront. Where I skimmed was the middle: "And wouldn't you know it, there he goes, accusing me of it anyway." and "*Ha!* Classic." That's the monologue putting on a show for me, and it felt staged. "*Shit.* He's onto me." I believed.

Then the cold version, and there I was. "a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them". That's how I treat Gerald, a senior colleague in my department who has, twice that I can document, presented graduate students' ideas as his own. I say good morning to him. I sit two chairs away at meetings. There's nothing behind it, and the nothing is meant to be seen, by him and by the students.

"There's no bottom to what can be done to such a person." With Gerald I'd have said this overstated what my look carries. I'm not threatening him. But my brother-in-law Karl, Ingrid's brother in Duluth, would recognize it. I've given him that look over a turkey and I've watched him decide I'd given up on him. He wasn't wrong.

**Integrity**

It came at the right time. "So what am I supposed to do—be *nice* to evil?" was my question, nearly in those words, about Gerald.

My corrector was Mr. Sorensen, physics, Moorhead, 1984. He caught me copying Dale Henning's lab report. He kept me after class and said very little. I braced for "cheater," for the call home, and it didn't come. He asked me what I'd been trying to find out in the experiment. I didn't know, and he waited until I knew that I didn't know, and then he told me to do it again. I did. I didn't dig in, and I didn't vow anything. I didn't copy again.

Your teacher story did two things for me. It brought Sorensen back, which no argument could have done. And "I don't want to make too much of that afternoon." told me you weren't going to use him as proof. I trusted you more after that line. "He wasn't being a hypocrite, and hypocrisy was what I'd always used to counter-accuse the people who tried to shame me for what I was." I read twice. It felt like a second point arriving inside the story; I see now it's planting something for later.

"What is it that two people on opposite sides can both be serving?" I answered faster than I expected. The phrase that came was Romans 2: the law written on the heart. I'm agnostic now and the phrase came anyway. I felt free with that question; nothing told me the answer. "Whatever they're standing up for isn't theirs." is asserted, and I held it on credit.

**The Corruption**

Insight, not lecture, and the best stretch in the chapter for me. I had to read "Neither half alone would be enough to get me what I need: both his reaction and my innocence." twice, and the sentences after it twice, before I saw the structure: a provocation that's deniable, and visibly deniable. On the second pass it came together. "In brief: I am in limbo about provoking him, and showing him that I'm in limbo is itself the provocation." That is new to me, and I've read most of what's been written on self-deception since 1970. The literature treats self-deception as something you hide. You're describing a self-deception that's displayed, as a weapon. "The signal is the pretense that there is no signal." I'll be thinking about that one for a while.

"Malice is turning away from a life in order to harm or use it. Contempt is that same turning-away, experienced as righteous." I granted these as definitions. But they sit in the middle of a description like a pair of index cards someone set down there. They read as stuck in.

"If you've been on his end, you know how it closes around you." Yes; I've been on Karl's end too.

The next paragraph lost me. "And when someone's contempt made you dig in, did it feel like an accident on his part? A slip I can shrug off, or even forgive." It goes from "you" to "his" to "I" in two sentences, and I couldn't tell whose position I was in. Then "My stories kept changing; my conclusion never did." I can't tell whose stories. Mine about Ruth? The narrator's about the enemy? It arrived from nowhere and I skipped it.

The demotion of inner knowing, "It won't feel like a loss to him. It will feel like he's gotten a bit wiser, a little more realistic about how the world works.", caught me, and not about Karl. After Gerald's second case went to the chair and nothing happened, I stopped talking about my unfinished work in the reading group. I timestamp my drafts now. I've called this realism. It is a little less of everyone.

"Did some part of you feel you *deserved* something?" Yes. In the Ruth case, I felt I'd earned the line because I'd trained the mind that produced it. I hadn't seen that until your question.

"From the inside, being proven right and being right are the same experience. The only difference is who produced the evidence." and "If I'd looked carefully, I'd have seen my role in causing it. So I didn't—and that *was* my role in causing it." Both landed clean. I'd have put the second on the board in a seminar.

The two checks. Satisfaction at evil punished: yes, I know that one from the news. The buttons: I put Gerald there. I found I wanted the vengeance button, and specifically, public exposure. Then the pill: and here's what I didn't expect. I felt disappointed by the pill. If his heart changed, he'd never have to pay. "I'll be honest: there is in me." Same here. And your next line, that telling myself it doesn't matter is what lets me act on it: I granted it, and I could see it in the two chairs' distance.

**Whose intention**

This is the first place I refused. "That's intent, just like your deflection was in the opening." Tracking, countering, steering around obstacles: that's goal-directedness, and goal-directedness isn't intention. Michael Bratman would say intention is a plan state that the agent settles on; Mele would say motivated bias produces the tracking without anyone intending it. My "in the air" was intended, yes, I've said so. But the steering in your encounter, I'm not sure. So I stopped there.

I could still follow, because you backed off two paragraphs later: "In that sense, it is not "my" intention." Then "But then whose intention is it, exactly?" And I refused the question too. Perhaps nobody's. Goal-directed behavior without an intender is a perfectly respectable category.

Then "It acts as if it has goals of its own, and it pursues them intentionally." "As if" I'll take; "intentionally" undoes the "as if" in the same sentence. But I heard something else in this passage, and it's why I didn't put the chapter down. It's Romans 7. "It is no longer I that do it, but sin that dwelleth in me." And then, right on time, you said what Luther would have said to anyone using Romans 7 as an alibi: "None of this lets me off the hook. It's my mind, and I answer for what it does." "Don't mistake the grammar for the claim" came exactly when I needed it. If it had come a paragraph later I'd have been gone.

"I desperately want to call this behavior *unconscious*". That caught me professionally, and I didn't enjoy it. The deflationary view I've defended in print is comfortable for exactly the reason you name. I haven't decided what to do with that.

"When I'm sure someone was fully aware of what they were doing, I've made them a villain. When I'm sure they had no idea, I've made them a machine." This was the hardest-landing sentence in the chapter. Gerald is my villain; he knows exactly what he's doing. Karl is my machine; he's a pipe that cable news runs through. I do both. Strawson called the second one "the objective attitude." I've taught that paper for twenty-five years and never once turned it on Karl.

"If you can find even one memory where it's plainly true of you, that's the one to look at." I had it already. The man I hold in contempt for taking students' ideas is a man who did what I did to Ruth. My contempt for Gerald gives that fear somewhere to point. I sat at the table a while.

The deeper fear, that there's no real goodness, I granted but didn't find.

"*Only a chump believes otherwise.*" It landed, and I connected it twice: to "I'd be a chump" in the hypocrite's voice earlier, and to my own deserving. It's a good place for the chump to return.

**The way out**

"What does "yourself" mean here?" My training says this is a philosophical question, and you told me not to treat it as one, so I didn't. "Take a moment, and see if you can find what you were hiding it from." I tried. First I found the man who'd write the letter to the chair: the indignant one, the one who despises Gerald. That's who I'd been hiding the Ruth business from, and it's grimly funny. But that wasn't the bottom. Under the indignation there was something quieter: the part of me that had liked Ruth, and had been glad, in the seminar room, when she said it. That's who I was hiding it from. I think that's what you're pointing at. I found something, anyway.

The three questions in paragraph after that came all at once, and that's the one place in "The way out" that felt like an interrogation. "And why don't "bad people" ever have to turn away to do *good*?" Himmler again: his men had to harden against pity. They turned away from good, not toward it. Supports you.

Losing something, or getting something back, when I owned up? Getting something back. Though I owned up only to myself, and to Ingrid. Not to Ruth.

The month alone. "Can you picture them saying that underneath everything, what they'd found was that they wished they could harm others more?" No, I can't. But "coming back honest" is doing a lot of work. If "honest" already means turned toward the good, the thought experiment answers itself. Granted, a little stacked.

"Here's what I think: our better nature isn't one part among others." I granted it as your claim. I couldn't find it in myself as a fact. Grandiose? It would have been, if you hadn't said "Not because it's nicer. Because it's the one that's home." That I heard. And "Everything I've said about contempt turns on which of the two is home." is the honest thing to say: you told me where the weight sits. Then "It's an absence that behaves like a presence. It has no substance of its own; everything it uses—my attention, my reasons, my eyes—is borrowed from me." That's Augustine on privation, and it's better than Augustine, because Augustine never quite explains why an absence acts. "Behaves like a presence" does. That changed my answer to "home" from granted toward found. I'm an agnostic who was raised on the *Confessions*, and that sentence reached me from both sides.

The dehumanizing gaze. Mine was at a job talk in 1997, a senior man in the front row. When I tried "Why would he have to turn away at all, if what he is, is essentially bad?", I tried to build his defense. If he were simply bad, his look would be blank. It wasn't blank. It was effortful; he was working to keep me out. You don't have to work to keep out what isn't there. That's where the question caught me, and it caught me because I answered it, not you.

The worst thing I know of: Rwanda, and the T4 doctors, who called their killing mercy. "Could they have done it with genuine kindness in their eyes?" No. "Genuine" is carrying weight again, but I agree.

The "bad essence" passages: half of each. "When I try to make it a cause, I'm just recreating the problem one level deeper." caught me. The rest read as heading off an objection I hadn't made, until "absence that behaves like a presence," which earned it all back.

"But someone always is: me." Did the saying about integrity land at the close of "The way out"? Yes. It sounds like the last line of a sermon, and I grew up on sermons, and it landed anyway.

**Common knowledge**

Did I follow it? Mostly, with one step that asked more than it had shown. "Part of me counted on him seeing me go missing and knowing what it meant" I followed. Then "which I knew he would, because I knew it's how *he* does wrong" is an argument from one's own case to another's. My field has a long, unhappy history with that argument. But then you sent me to my own look.

"Think of the last time you sent that look yourself." Three weeks ago, a curriculum meeting. Gerald proposed a new graduate requirement and I looked at him over my glasses a beat too long. "Did you know they'd read it? How did you know?" I knew because I'd received that exact look, from the man at the job talk. I knew it from inside. So the step from my own case held, for me, because it wasn't an inference. It was memory.

"Try it now: imagine the same act without turning away." I imagined giving the APA talk with Ruth in the front row, her face in view, saying her sentence as mine. I couldn't. I could not have said it. "Does it surprise you that a stranger could count on that about you?" Not surprise. Something less comfortable than surprise.

"Contempt only works because we already know we're the same kind of thing: beings who must *turn away* to cause harm." For my cases, granted. The "only" asks more than my cases show, and I noticed it. "Nothing has to be computed." I read twice and then understood: recognition, not inference. "That's what made it a taunt." was clear.

"My teacher gave me no hook, and nothing to fight. Contempt is built to give one." It's true, but it sits between the taunt and the hypocrisy charge like a callback set down out of order. Stuck in.

"We were never disagreeing about what the ground was. We were fighting over which of us still stood on it." This I found, with Karl. Karl's charge against me is that people like me don't care about working people. Mine against him is that he doesn't care what happens to migrants. We each accuse the other of indifference. "Said out loud, we'd both hear that we were standing on the same ground." Yes. We'd both hear it, and that's exactly why neither of us has said it. Found.

Where someone's contempt reached in me. My father, when I told him I'd left the church: "I don't know who you are anymore." "Not your pride: an insult to your pride is easier to shrug off." Right. The job talk man hit pride. My father reached past it, to the part that knew it was still his son.

"**Love and contempt are addressed to the same place.**" This is the sentence in the chapter that no argument could have earned and the page did. "and they both say it to the somebody who is." I'd teach this.

"I found it the way I found my own old wrongdoing: it didn't feel like new information, because it wasn't." Do I believe you? I believe it of the core. But the chapter is built as an argument, with steps and an "only," and you say so: "I've laid this out as an argument, but that isn't how I came to it." I believe that sentence, and I'd still say some of the argument's joints are where you were building rather than finding.

On "evil": I didn't flinch. I'm Augustinian enough to accept "evil" as the name for the bottomless thing. "It's why I don't think "pure evil" is a different kind of thing, but the same thing at full strength: I tasted that quality myself." As an argument, degree versus kind is unsettled. As testimony, "I tasted that quality myself" is the right ground, and I accepted it as testimony.

"my allies are the entire rest of society" onward followed. The part about contempt sometimes stopping misbehavior I already knew. "Part of me still fears that dropping the contempt would mean going easy on them." That's the Integrity question asked a second time, and I'd already answered it with Sorensen.

The time I corrected someone badly: Tomas, a second-year, 2015. I wrote "This isn't philosophy" on his chapter draft. Two years later, a different student with the same problem; I asked her to tell me in two sentences what she wanted to show. What was different? I'd thought about Sorensen. And Tomas, I'll admit, wrote like I wrote at his age.

Would I trust someone who treats others with contempt? Less. Would I tell them something I was ashamed of? No. Known already; the questions were quick and didn't need to be longer.

"What passes between us can't be recorded on any ledger or in any transcript, and yet it's louder than what can." Yes. Gerald's file at the chair's office is all record. None of what happens in that hallway is in it.

The closing of "Common knowledge." I took "the same thing" to be the good itself, or, in my old vocabulary, conscience: the scholastics' synderesis, the spark that never goes out. "But it's not an advisor." landed hard; I've treated it as an advisor my whole adult life. "We may never agree on a definition of "good," but we already agree on how to orient toward it, and part of us knows it." Granted, and found with Karl. And yes, it felt like an ending. "and yet I can't take credit when it does." I thought the chapter was over.

**The spread**

It wasn't over, and I didn't mind for the first page. "the kid who watches him do it" caught me; Karl's grandson sits at that table.

"The *what's wrong with you?* that needs them to know they're broken." Last Sunday my daughter Maren told me on the phone that she'd missed a deadline for her licensing paperwork. I sighed. I know the sigh. "While you were sending it, did it feel like you?" Yes. "It felt like the most reasonable version of me." Exactly that. Then with her face in view and without the reasons: no. It didn't. That's the one I'll act on.

"*That wasn't the real me* can be used as an excuse, but I also think it's true." I believed it, and it's the one place a Lutheran is at home in this chapter: simul iustus et peccator. Both at once.

"Whether that number ever goes to zero is something you can discover for yourself." Room. Thank you.

"Sometimes it looks like me writing this book *at* people, instead of *for* them." This was good to read. It disarmed the worry I'd been carrying since "only."

The final exercise. I did it with the Loblaws cashier and then with Gerald. With the cashier, "There's someone here" came, slowly. With Gerald: "For me the story is there before the face is." Yes. The word "plagiarist" arrived before his face did. "That's the turning away, live." That was a good last line and a real experiment. "the hood, the number instead of the name, the word for vermin." I knew this, but it belonged.

Did it gather or feel like one ending too many? It gathered. But the chapter had already ended twice: at "can't take credit," and at "to be seen as more than what I do." The exercise is better than either of those endings. I'd keep it and lose one of them.

**Length**

It drags in the middle of the Encounter, in "Whose intention" where intent is argued and then un-argued, and in the run from "Fighting evil" through the trust questions, where I already knew most of it. Said twice: "be *nice* to evil" and "going easy on them"; "Why does it matter so much to me that he's bad" and "I fear I might be one of them"; the two closes before "The spread." To cut a fifth: the staged middle of the Encounter, half of "Whose intention," the paragraph after "If you've been on his end," the advisor passage or the spread's first page, not both.

Words. "Darkness" was in my way for the first half; as an agnostic I kept hearing a metaphysics I hadn't agreed to. "Home" and "absence that behaves like a presence" cleared it. "Limbo" is a Catholic word, which a Lutheran notices and then lets go. "mash that first button" made me wince.

No contemplative practice to report. What I have instead is Luther and Augustine, and the chapter matched both more than I expected and contradicted neither.

**Last**

Was it obvious? Pieces: that contempt doesn't persuade, that we trust contemptuous people less. What became newly consequential was the Ruth memory sitting next to Gerald. I've known both facts for years. I'd never put them on the same table.

This week. Karl and Linda arrive Sunday for Thanksgiving. I'm going to try to hold the face before the story. I'll write to Ruth; I've decided that. I'm still going to the chair about Gerald, because a student is owed it, and your teacher tells me the fierceness can stay. But I'll write it about what he did, and I'll read it over for the other thing before I send it. And I'll call Maren back.

Would I pass it on? Not yet to my colleagues; they'd stop at the "only" and the "intentionally" and never reach "Love and contempt." I'd send it to Tom Engstrom, a Lutheran pastor in Saint Paul I've argued with since we were nineteen. He'd hear Romans 7 and Augustine in it and wouldn't need it argued, and he has a congregation split down the middle on everything Karl and I fight about.

With thanks, and with more of my evening than I'd planned to give,

Paul

### Stage 2

Dear Aditya,

I read the second file on Friday morning, with the first letter already sent to myself and sealed. I'll say up front that the two are the same chapter for most of their length, so this letter is about a dozen places, not a whole book. But several of those places were where the first one did the most to me.

**Which did more, and which treated me better**

The chapter did more to me. The second file treated me better, by a little.

The chapter did more because three of the four sentences that changed what I'll do this week aren't in the second file at all. "Did some part of you feel you *deserved* something?" is the question that showed me I'd felt entitled to Ruth's sentence because I'd trained the mind that made it. "It's an absence that behaves like a presence." is the sentence that moved "home" from something I granted toward something I found. And "Said out loud, we'd both hear that we were standing on the same ground." is where I found Karl and me, accusing each other of the same indifference. The second file has none of these. I'd have finished it with fewer names on the page.

The second file treated me better because it asks fewer questions and doesn't misfire. It lacks the paragraph in the chapter that loses its pronouns, "And when someone's contempt made you dig in, did it feel like an accident on his part? A slip I can shrug off, or even forgive.", which I skipped. It lacks "Have you ever felt that?" after the "Ugh, I knew it" line, which I didn't need asked; the line had already asked it. It lacks the month alone, which I called stacked. And in "The way out" it goes from three questions to "Here's what I think" without the fourth question and the month, which is where the chapter had felt like an interrogation. Reading the second, that stretch felt like room.

**What the second file has that the chapter should recover**

Not much is unique to the second file; it's mostly the chapter with things taken out. But three things are better there.

First, the order and wording at the end of the Limbo section. The second file has "The part that knew never won that argument, but it never budged, either. It kept on quietly knowing what it knew." and then ends the section on "Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" The chapter has "The side that just knew: notice that despite "losing" that battle, it never budged an inch." The scare quotes on "losing" and "an inch" make it louder without making it truer, and "notice that" tells me to notice something I'd already noticed. The second file's sentence is plainer and I'd recover it. The chapter's extra question, "And which did you listen to—the one that had reasons or the one that "just knew"?", I didn't remark on in my first letter. Looking at the second, I don't miss it.

Second, "My stories kept changing; my conclusion never did." I said in my first letter I couldn't tell whose stories these were and that the line arrived from nowhere. In the second file it sits directly after "Deny it, and you're the kind of person who does. Get angry, and that's proof too. Stay calm, and you don't even care. Apologize, and it's too convenient." There it's obvious. Whatever he does, I have a new story for it, and the verdict stays. The chapter put the accident paragraph between them, and that's what broke it. The line isn't wrong; the paragraph in front of it is. That's the clearest single improvement in the second file.

Third, "Surely that's not what I'm doing. I'm just being fierce with evil, right? Right." The second file stops there. The chapter goes on: "And if it's dark, it's the *good* kind of darkness—the kind meant to fight *real* darkness." I'm of two minds. The extra sentence is true to life; it's the "dirty hands" defense my field has argued about since Walzer, and people do think it. But "Right." is a better last beat, and the next line, "So I double down.", comes harder after it. On balance I'd take the second file's version.

There's one change I'd reject. Where the chapter has the passage beginning "But that doesn't make it nothing. Call it nothing, and I'll underestimate it", the second file has "Here's what makes it difficult: when profound self-deception is operating, it looks and behaves exactly like you'd expect a "bad essence" to look. Like the *presence* of something, rather than the absence it actually is." This states the privation view without earning it. "The absence it actually is" is asserted, flatly, in a subordinate clause. The chapter instead warns me not to call it nothing, then gives the reason it acts: "everything it uses—my attention, my reasons, my eyes—is borrowed from me." That is the sentence that did what Augustine never quite did for me. The second file's "profound self-deception" is plainer, and an agnostic reader might prefer it to "it." But it's a description, and the chapter's version is an explanation. Keep the chapter's.

**What the chapter was right to add, and where it should have left well alone**

Since the second file is mostly the chapter minus things, the real question for me was which of the chapter's additions earn their place. Going one by one:

"Have you ever had to hide from yourself something you did out of decency? I can't think of a time. Why would we only ever need limbo when we're causing harm?" Keep. It's the one place in the opening where I had to work against you, and the work (my father, his forgiveness he hid from himself) is what made me trust the rest.
