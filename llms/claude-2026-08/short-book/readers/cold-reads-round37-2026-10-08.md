## Theresa (49, Catholic elementary school principal; daily Mass, spiritual direction)

### Stage 1

Dear Aditya,

I read this over two evenings, the first at the kitchen table after the kids were in their rooms, the second in the car in the school lot before 7:30 Mass. I did what you asked with every question. Some of them cost me more than I expected. I'll go in order.

**The opening.** You ask me to "Hold them tentatively, until you see the picture they assemble." Fine. I hold everything tentatively that isn't the Creed. Your central claim, that contempt says "you're a different kind of thing than me, but for it to land, you must be the same kind of thing," I bought tentatively on the first read. It sounded clever. Clever makes me suspicious; I hear clever from parents every week.

**The memory.** You ask for a wrong I knew at the time but couldn't admit until later. Mine came fast, which told me something. When Linh was thirteen I read her texts on her phone while she slept. I told myself it was a mother's duty. There was a boy, there were rumors, there was the internet. I put the phone back exactly where it was, face down, and I knew while I was doing it. I didn't say it out loud until two years later, to my spiritual director. I have never told Linh.

When I finally said it, was it new information? No. "Or like something you had always known, but somehow hadn't *let* yourself know?" Yes. That is exactly it. I bought this step outright.

Then "If you did, did that happen passively?" No. When Paul once said, half joking, "You don't read her phone, do you?" I changed the subject to Linh's grades so smoothly I impressed myself. That was not passive. I bought that too.

"Nobody ever looked honestly at something in themselves while being told they were worthless for having it." I have said a version of this to teachers for twenty years. I've never heard it said back to me. I was grateful for it here, because I was starting to call myself names.

"Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" I sat with that a while. The part that said *you're her mother, you have a right* had a whole brief prepared. The part that knew it was wrong just knew. I'm a Catholic; I'd call that second part conscience. You don't, and you don't need to yet. I noticed the absence of the word and I'll come back to it.

**The cashier.** I pictured the young woman at the register at Lion Market on Tully, the one with the jade bracelet who calls me *cô*. The smile went out on its own. The sneer would not go. I had to invent a reason: she was on her phone when I walked up. Then it would go, a little. I didn't like how quickly I found the reason. Bought.

**The remorse question.** This one I tested hardest, because I have a case that I thought might break it. Six years ago I gave a teacher a second chance after a complaint. Three months later he grabbed a fourth grader by the arm hard enough to bruise. I lay awake over that for weeks. Was that remorse for having been decent? I took it apart, slowly. The remorse was for not watching him, for wanting the second chance to work so much that I stopped looking. It was not remorse for the mercy itself. So your asymmetry held, but only after I made it pass through my worst case. "Remorse is what turning back feels like: you turn toward someone you've harmed and see them properly." That was true of the fourth grader's mother's face in my office. Bought, and bought harder for having fought it.

**The Encounter.** My contempt is for a priest. I'll call him Father Keane. Nine years ago a mother came to me about our parish youth minister. I took it to Father Keane, who was then my pastor. He told me it would be handled. It was handled: the man was moved to another parish, and no one called the police until I went to the diocese myself four months later. I have not forgiven Father Keane. He's retired now. I still see him at diocesan events.

I did confront him once, in his office, a year after. Your narration of that is almost uncomfortably accurate. "There's no bottom to what can be done to such a person." Yes. I wanted him to feel the bottomlessness. And "I'm just being fierce with evil, right? Right." I laughed out loud at the kitchen table, the bad kind of laugh, because I think I actually said "someone has to be fierce here" to him. Believable as what a mind does? Completely. I skimmed nothing in the scene. If I'd skim anywhere it would be "*Shit.* He's onto me." Not because of the word. Because I recognized the moment and didn't want to stay in it. His eyes did say *don't pretend you're doing something noble.* I remember thinking he was the last man on earth entitled to that look.

**The cold version.** This is how I treat him now, and how I treat certain parents. "a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them." At the diocesan Christmas luncheon I shake Father Keane's hand and ask after his health, and I make sure he can tell I am asking the way you'd ask after a stranger's dog. And there is a father at our school, I'll call him Mr. Hendrickson, who opens every email with what his family has "invested" in us. I am exquisitely polite to him. He knows. I am glad he knows. I'm glad you included the cold version, because the hot one I only did once. The cold one I do weekly.

**Integrity.** "So what am I supposed to do—be *nice* to evil?" That was my question, word for word, and it arrived exactly when I was asking it. If it had come later I think I would have started reading defensively, the way I read diocesan policy memos. So yes, the right time.

"Not more gently. Just that their anger was directed at what you did, and not what you are." I knew this one before I finished reading the sentence. My first year teaching, at twenty-four, I made a boy named Marco stand in the hall and then made a joke about him to the class while he could hear. My principal, Dolores Reyes, walked past, heard it, and came in. She corrected me right there, in front of my aide, with Marco listening. She was much harsher than I'd been with Marco. Did I dig in? I wanted to, for about an hour. But she wasn't telling me what I was. She was telling me what I'd done, and looking at me as if she knew I already knew. I apologized to Marco the next morning. I've been trying to be Dolores ever since.

Your teacher story did something particular for me. "*responsive* is the closest word." That's the word I've been missing for twenty years. Dolores was responsive. She was looking for something in my face. When I correct a child or a teacher now and it goes badly, I think it's because I've stopped looking for anything; I've already decided what I'll find. And I appreciated "I don't want to make too much of that afternoon." That's honest. I've watched the same fierceness bounce off kids who had nothing in them yet for it to reach, or had it buried under more than I could get to.

"What is it that two people on opposite sides can both be serving?" I answered it immediately: God. Then I wondered whether you wanted me to say it. You don't say it. You leave room for me to, and I felt free there, not pushed. Later I realized my answer for Father Keane and me is the same answer and that I'd never once applied it to him. That was the first time the chapter got under the skin.

**"Back to my enemy" and The Corruption.** Mostly insight, a little lecture. "In brief: I am in limbo about provoking him, and showing him that I'm in limbo is itself the provocation." I read that twice. The second time it held. "The signal is the pretense that there is no signal." I knew that one from the other side, from parents who smile at me. And from Linh. Linh is very good at the signal that isn't a signal, and I taught her.

"Deny it, and you're the kind of person who does. Get angry, and that's proof too. Stay calm, and you don't even care. Apologize, and it's too convenient." That's what happens to a teenager in a principal's office when the principal has already decided. I have done that to children. I had to put the book down there.

"My stories kept changing; my conclusion never did." That's Father Keane in my head for nine years. Bought.

"From the inside, being proven right and being right are the same experience. The only difference is who produced the evidence." Good. But by then I felt you'd made the point; "If I'd looked carefully, I'd have seen my role in causing it. So I didn't—and that *was* my role in causing it." is a third ending to the same section. It started to feel like lecture there, not because it was wrong, but because I already had it.

**The two buttons.** Is evil punished satisfying? Yes. When the diocese published its list of credibly accused clergy I read the comment section for an hour, and I was not praying. The buttons: I wanted to say I'd give Father Keane the pill. I'm supposed to. But what I'm itching for isn't violence. I want him made to stand up at a priests' convocation and have it read out, in front of the men who respect him. "I'll be honest: there is in me." I'm grateful you went first. It let me be honest too. "And that lie is exactly what lets me act on it, in ways I rarely recognize until later." The handshake at the luncheon. Bought.

**Whose intention.** "It acts as if it has goals of its own, and it pursues them intentionally." I heard the devil. Of course I did. My mother would have said *ma quỷ* without hesitating. Then you said "Don't mistake the grammar for the claim: I'm not saying there's a separate thing living in me." It came in time; I didn't have a chance to settle in either reading before you corrected it. But I'll tell you honestly I was half disappointed. I believe in the devil, and the Church teaches me he works exactly through the thing you describe, through my own mind dressing itself as righteousness. You don't have to agree, but you closed a door I would have liked left open a crack. "It's my mind, and I answer for what it does." That I want kept no matter what. That's what makes the devil talk safe.

"When I'm sure someone was fully aware of what they were doing, I've made them a villain. When I'm sure they had no idea, I've made them a machine." This caught me hard. I have made Father Keane a villain: he knew exactly. And I have made Mr. Hendrickson a machine: he can't help it, he's entitled. Both are ways of not having to deal with a person. I'm going to be thinking about that one in parent conferences.

"If you can find even one memory where it's plainly true of you, that's the one to look at." The fear that I might be one of them. I tried. I didn't find it quickly. Then I did, and it was this: nine years ago, before that mother came to me, I had heard a rumor about the youth minister from another teacher. I didn't take it to anyone. I told myself rumors aren't reports. It was three weeks. Nothing happened in those three weeks that I know of. But my contempt for Father Keane has a very convenient shape: it's exactly the size of the thing I don't let myself think about. I didn't want to find that. I'm writing it to you because I said I'd do what the chapter asked.

**The way out.** "What does "yourself" mean here? I'm not asking a philosophical question." I tried it with Linh's phone. Who was I hiding it from? Not Paul; I could have lied to Paul. Not Linh; she was asleep. I was hiding it from the place in me that would have walked into her room and woken her up and told her. The Council document says conscience is a sanctuary where we are alone with God; my director quotes it to me. What I found was something like that, without a word I'd have to defend. I found what you point at. Whether it's what you mean by it I can't tell.

"did it feel like *losing* something of yourself, or like getting something back?" Getting something back. That's what confession feels like when it works, which isn't always.

"Not because it's nicer. Because it's the one that's home." I granted the claim that our better nature is "what we *are*." I didn't find it grandiose. It's close to what I was taught: the image of God isn't lost, only wounded and covered. But I'll be honest: "in the absence of all self-deception" made me a little uneasy as a Catholic. It sounds as if seeing clearly would be enough, with no grace in it. You may not want grace in it. I noticed it and kept reading.

**The dehumanizing gaze.** I've received it. When I brought the report to Father Keane, he looked through me. "Theresa, these things are handled." He was absent. I've always read that look as proof of what he was. Then: "Why would he have to turn away at all, if what he is, is essentially bad?" I tried to answer it honestly and couldn't. If he were simply a bad man he could have looked at me straight. He turned away because there was something to turn away from. He knew. That doesn't make me forgive him. It made him a person again for the length of a paragraph, and I didn't like it, and I think you were right.

"Could they have done it with genuine kindness in their eyes?" The worst thing I know of anyone doing is what the youth minister did. And here I pushed back, because groomers do it with kindness in their eyes. That's the method. I had to read the sentence again and lean on "genuine." Looked like kindness, yes. Was it? No. So it held, barely, and only because of one word. If that word ever comes out, you'll lose every parent who has been through what our parish went through.

The "bad essence" passages did catch me, but partly because I already knew them. Evil as an absence of good, not a thing in itself: that's Augustine, I learned it in tenth-grade religion. "Like the *presence* of something, rather than the absence it actually is." What the chapter did was make it apply to Father Keane, which no religion teacher ever did. "If darkness were an essence, I would be right to be afraid of it, and ashamed, and I'd have to keep proving it's in others and not in me." That caught me, after what I'd found about the three weeks. Not an objection being headed off. It read like you were describing me.

"When I feel a fundamental difference between me and my worst enemy, I ask which part of me needs there to be one." I know which part. I've told you.

**The integrity saying.** "They say integrity is what we do when no one is watching." That's on a bulletin board in our sixth-grade room. "But someone always is: me." I expected you to say God. You said me, and then "the part I'd turned away from, so I wouldn't feel it watching." For me those are close enough to the same place that I didn't mind. Did it land at the end of The way out? Yes, as a sentence. But the section had already ended for me on the question about which part of me needs a difference. The saying felt like a second ending, a good one, after the first.

**Common knowledge.** I followed the argument from the look to "**Contempt only works because we already know we're the same kind of thing.**" I followed it because I'd lived the hall of mirrors, twice: in Father Keane's office, and at my own dinner table with Linh. When Linh tells me the Church is a racket and I give her the look, she reads it, and I know she reads it, and she knows I know. Nobody else at the table can see it. Paul can't. "it can't be named without sounding crazy" is precise.

Then hypocrisy. "But the charge only landed because I hold the one he accused me of breaking, and his outrage in making it showed that he holds it too." Here is what surprised me. Linh's whole charge against the Church, and against me, is hypocrisy. I have always heard it as her leaving. Reading this, I heard it as her holding the same thing I hold, and being furious that we broke it. She's not outside it. Her outrage is the proof she's in it. "**Whatever else divides us, we answer to the same thing—and we both know it.**" I would never have gotten there with Linh by argument. It held, and it added something I didn't have.

"Try it now: imagine the same act without turning away." I tried it with the phone. Could I have read her texts while looking squarely at her face asleep, at what it meant to her? No. I had to keep the story running the whole time: safety, the boy, the internet. "Does it surprise you that a stranger could count on that about you?" Yes, a little. Not unpleasantly.

**Where contempt reached me.** I was nine, at the DMV with my mother. A man behind us told her to learn English or go back. My mother looked at the floor. That reached a place my pride doesn't live. "**Love and contempt are addressed to the same place.**" "One says *I know you're in there.* The other says *there's nobody in there*, and they both say it to the somebody who is." That's the best sentence in the chapter for me. That man was talking to someone. He knew it. That's why he bothered.

**The last time I sent that look.** Back-to-School night, to Mr. Hendrickson, when he asked whether the "investment" covered his son's seat being moved away from the window. "Did it feel like justice, just a little sweeter than justice needs to be?" Yes. Sweeter. And I gave myself an out: my words were perfectly professional.

**"Only a chump believes otherwise."** It landed, and I connected it to the earlier passage, "then why the hell shouldn't I? I'd be a chump." The two together made me think about what I taught Linh. Not Father Keane; she never knew him. Me. Nine years of talking about him at the dinner table, the edge in my voice when the diocese comes up. "Everyone in the conversation learns that the verdict is safe to hold, and leaves a little more practiced at it." Linh learned that the Church is run by men like Father Keane from her mother, who still goes to daily Mass. Of course she thinks it's hypocrisy. I taught her the verdict and the hypocrisy in the same breath. That's the thing in this chapter that will change what I do this week.

**"pure evil."** "It's why I don't think "pure evil" is a different kind of thing, but the same thing at full strength." I accepted it with my head; it's sound doctrine. My stomach flinched, because of the youth minister. I didn't stop trusting you. But I noticed I wanted one exception, and I noticed that the wanting is what your chapter is about. I'm leaving it there.

**"my allies are the entire rest of society" to "fierce kindness."** It followed. And most of it I already knew professionally. "Can you remember a time when you corrected someone in a nasty way, but then later took an entirely different approach to the same problem?" Yes: a second-year teacher, Kevin, who didn't report a playground injury. I dressed him down in front of the office staff. He was polite to me for a year and told me nothing. The next time, a different lapse, I closed the door and was just as hard, harder, but I was talking to the teacher I knew he wanted to be. He cried and then he fixed it. So "Everything I've been able to accomplish with contempt, I've been able to accomplish better without it." I believe that, for a school. What was new was seeing that I've never once tried it with Father Keane, or with a parent. I've only tried it with people I'm responsible for. "Fighting evil is the place where my own darkness is most likely to come out, because it has the best cover" explains why.

**"That wasn't the real me."** I believed it. Saint Paul said nearly the same thing, that it's no longer I who do it but sin in me, and people have used it as an excuse for two thousand years. "and it can be used as one" and "it's mine to answer for" are what make it true rather than convenient.

**"I've laid this out as an argument, but that isn't how I came to it."** I believed you, mostly. The sections that felt found were the Encounter, the teacher, the love-and-contempt sentence, the loved-ones paragraph. The sections that felt argued were the middle of Whose intention and the first half of Common knowledge. They read like a proof. I could follow them, but nothing in me moved until the examples returned.

**The spread and the end.** "I send it to people I love." The sigh. Last Sunday Linh said she wasn't coming to Christmas Mass this year. I sighed. It was a *what's wrong with you* sigh, dressed as patience. I saw her face, and I pretended I hadn't. "It felt like the most reasonable version of me in the room." Yes, it did. With her face in view, it doesn't. It looks like a woman making her daughter carry her disappointment so she doesn't have to say it out loud.

"When someone treats others with contempt in front of you, do you trust them more, or less?" Less. And the faculty room: when I roll my eyes about the Hendricksons in there, every teacher learns I'm not someone you bring your shame to. I hadn't put that together.

"Sometimes it looks like me writing this book *at* people, instead of *for* them." I'm glad you said that. There were a few places where I felt written at, and it helped to know you know.

The last paragraph before the exercise, ending "to be seen as more than what I do," felt like the end. That's where I put the book down the first night, thinking it was over.

**The exercise.** I did it in the car before Mass, with the face of the old man who always sits in the third pew, Mr. Phan, whom I don't know. "a place somebody is looking out of." It came. I didn't want anything from him. Then Father Keane's face. "For me the story is there before the face is." For me too: the office, the file, "these things are handled." The story arrived first, and his face came after it, already labeled. I couldn't get the recognition. I got about halfway: an old man, alone at a luncheon table. Then the story came back. So the exercise did gather the chapter up for me, because it was the only place I tested the whole thing at once. But it was one ending too many coming after "to be seen as more than what I do." I'd have been happy with either. "the hood, the number instead of the name, the word for vermin" is strong and I flinched at it. I'm not sure it belongs at a moment when you're asking me to be quiet with a stranger's face.

**Where it seemed to end early.** After "to be seen as more than what I do." Also after "Sometimes this reminds them to do the same—especially when I've been given every reason to abandon it." which sounded like a last line.

**Length, and what's said twice.** It drags in Whose intention, from "I desperately want to call this behavior *unconscious*" through "each of us is sure we're free of it." The villain-and-machine paragraph is the jewel; the rest of that stretch I'd tighten. The Corruption ends three times. "**Contempt only works because we already know we're the same kind of thing.**" restates your opening claim, which I'd already accepted; that's fine once. The idea that I hide from myself in order to harm is said in the opening, in Whose intention, in Common knowledge, and again near the end with "I've found I'm almost always turned away to *some* degree." If I had to cut a fifth, I'd cut from Whose intention, from the end of The Corruption, and the paragraph beginning "This, I've found, is how evil spreads."

One small thing: "Most is quiet:" right after the trust question. Most what? I had to go back to find what it meant. Most contempt, I assume.

**Words.** "Limbo." You may not know this, but for a Catholic of my age, limbo is where the unbaptized babies went, the ones who weren't damned and weren't saved. I was startled every time. Oddly, it fits: not punished, not home, and no way to get back on your own. But some of your Catholic readers will trip on it. "mash that first button" is too cute for what you're asking. "rot" made me wince, which I think was the point. "chump" is fine; it sounds like a man in a bar, which is who says it.

**Last.** Was it obvious? Parts of it, yes. Augustine told me evil is an absence. Dolores Reyes taught me what correction without contempt looks like. My director has told me for years that my anger at Father Keane is "something to bring to prayer." I knew all of it. What I didn't know was that these were the same thing, and that it applies to the people I'm most sure about. What changes this week: I'm going to stop talking about Father Keane at dinner, and I'm going to tell Linh I read her phone. I'm not sure which of those is harder.

Would I pass it to a friend? To Maribel, who runs the school at St. Martin's and has her own Mr. Hendrickson; she'd get it in one read and argue with me about the devil. And, maybe, eventually, to Linh. It might be the first thing about conscience I could hand her without a crucifix on it. She'd use it against me. That's probably fine.

With gratitude, and some irritation,

Theresa

I've now read the second file. Most of it is the same chapter word for word, so I read it slowly, looking for where it parted from the first. Here's what I found and what it did.

**Which did more, and which treated me better.** The chapter did more to me, by a long way. In the second file the spread section tells me about the sigh and the look. In the chapter it hands them back to me to check. Those are different experiences. Reading about your sigh, I nodded. Being asked about mine, I put the book down.

Which treated me better? Also the chapter, though more narrowly. Two small things in the chapter left me room the second file closes. First, the chapter says "If you're not ready to go that far, keep the question open." before "When I feel a fundamental difference between me and my worst enemy, I ask which part of me needs there to be one." The second file has the second sentence without the first. With the permission, the question was mine to keep. Without it, it reads like the next step I'm expected to take. Second, and this one matters more to me than anything else in this letter: the chapter asks "Could they have done it with genuine kindness in their eyes?" and the second file asks "Could they have done it with kindness in their eyes?" I told you in my first letter that I leaned my whole weight on "genuine" to get past the youth minister. Groomers do it with kindness in their eyes; that's how it works. Without "genuine," my answer to the second file's question is simply yes, and the paragraph after it falls down. A parent from our parish would close the book there. Keep "genuine."

**What the second file has that the chapter should recover.**

"So part of me already knows the difference between the "good" I've invented to excuse myself and the kind we all recognize. I just tell myself I don't." This sits between "looked for someone worse than me to prove I wasn't so bad after all" and "We may never agree on the details of what is "good,"" and it's the bridge between them. In the chapter I went straight from my own self-excusing to disagreement about the good, and I felt the ground shift under me: is he saying the good is just whatever we each think? This sentence answers that before I can ask it. It also names what I did with Father Keane for nine years: I invented a "good," fierceness, protecting children, that happened to require me to despise him. And I knew the difference. I'd recover it.

"If it does end up reminding them, I can't take credit for it." I said in my first letter that "in the absence of all self-deception" left no room for grace. This sentence is the closest either version comes to it. It isn't me who brings someone back; I only stay where I am. As a principal I know this in my bones. I've never once been able to take credit for the child who turned around, even when the parents tried to give it to me. Without this sentence, "Sometimes this reminds them to do the same" sounds a little like a method. With it, it sounds like what actually happens. Recover it.

"Most contempt is quiet:" In the chapter it's "Most is quiet:" and I had to go back to find what it referred to. The second file's version is simply clearer. Use it.

The integrity saying's placement. In the second file it closes Common knowledge, right after "And I think goodness spreads the same way—not through the visible acts, but what we all know they carry, whether or not we can prove it." Then "They say integrity is what we do when no one is watching." That connection is better than the one in the chapter. The whole paragraph before it is about what's off the record, what nobody can prove. And then: someone always sees it. That's the same thing I tell a sixth grader who thinks the lie worked because no adult caught it. In the chapter, the saying follows "which part of me needs there to be one," and I said it felt like a second ending to The way out. In the second file, The way out ends on that question, which is where it ended for me anyway. So I'd move it back. The cost is that Common knowledge in the second file already runs long, and the saying makes one more ending there too. I still think it earns that spot more than the other.

**What the chapter was right to leave out or change.**

The remorse-in-reverse question. The second file has the bad person "wracked with guilt for having gone against his evil nature." The chapter says "wracked with remorse." You'd just told me that guilt at breaking a rule I was raised on is not remorse. So in the second file, I could answer "yes, I can imagine it: a man raised in a cruel house could feel guilty for breaking its rules." In the chapter I couldn't. The chapter's word is the one that makes the test work.

"Remorse is what turning back feels like: you turn toward someone you've harmed and see them properly." Only the chapter has it. It's what let me find the fourth grader's mother's face in my memory, and it prepares "turned toward" and the stranger's face at the end. The chapter was right to add it.

"If darkness were an essence, I would be right to be afraid of it, and ashamed, and I'd have to keep proving it's in others and not in me. But if it's something I *do*, then I can stop." Only the chapter has this, and it caught me harder than anything around it, because of the three weeks I didn't report the rumor. Without it the bad-essence passage in the second file is Augustine and nothing more. With it, it's about me.

"That's what I looked like to my enemy." The chapter ends the essence paragraph on this. The second file stops at "rather than the absence it actually is." The chapter's last line turns the doctrine back into the scene in Father Keane's office. Keep it.

"*That wasn't the real me* may sound like an excuse, and it can be used as one." Only the chapter has this paragraph. I believed it, and I said so. "You'd take that bet at any odds. But on what evidence? You'd never met him. You knew nothing about him." This made the common-knowledge argument land in a way the chapter's version didn't. In the chapter I followed the argument and didn't feel it. Here I felt it first. Of course I'd bet the stranger does the same thing he's accusing me of. I'd bet it on Greg without a second thought. And how would I know, unless I know what that look comes from?

"And if you had to guess: is *this* the kind of person who sneaks around trying to get away with stuff?" Short, and it lands. Dolores, no. Greg, yes, and I'd have said so before I could stop myself.

"He was judging you *from* the very state he was judging you *for*." The chapter says this in many more words. This sentence does it in one.

"Why does he so desperately need you to be bad—unless *some part of him suspects you're not?*" This is a better answer to my Greg question than the one I found myself. In the chapter, I worked out that Greg needs the clipboard voice because he'd see the girl otherwise. This puts it in one line. It pushes a little, but it's the right push.

"*Our conscience is the only thing that warns us when we've strayed from it.*" I don't know if this is true. I keep thinking about it, which is something.

"Malice is frightening. Contempt is malice that believes it's righteous, and that's what makes it **maddening**." This is the clearest thing in either version about why kids explode in hearings. They're not reacting to the punishment. They're reacting to the righteousness. I've seen a kid sit calmly through a three-day suspension and then throw a chair because the AP said "I'm doing this for your own good."

"Which is why it never mattered how small the thing you did was. It only had to be _evidence_." The dress code. The freshman's outfit was nothing. To Greg it was evidence. I'd want this in the chapter, maybe near "So do the certainty and urgency, the sense that I must not let this go."

From "When You Send It": "It isn't hard because of what you did, which may have been nothing much. It's hard because you don't want to believe you have that in you." This is the most useful sentence in either file for me personally. My sigh at Joon over the dishwasher is nothing much. That's exactly why I can't look at it. And "Check whether, from here, looking at the flash without the reasons, you still endorse it." That's a test I can actually run. I ran it on the scallions at Eun-hee's, and no, I don't endorse it. The chapter's version, "Now look back at it with their face in view," is close, but this one strips off the reasons, which is where I hide.

"Notice how even the smallest flicker can feel dangerous to admit—out of proportion to its size." Yes. True, and the chapter doesn't have it.

"What you showed them, for a second, was that you'd stopped seeing them. That they'd been moved out of view—and you let them watch you do it." Good, and plainer than the chapter.

"*My darkness isn't real darkness—it's the kind all good people must harbor to keep bad people in check. They just take it the wrong way, because of course they would.*" This is me with Greg in one sentence. I'd want it in the chapter more than almost anything else here. The chapter's "Fighting evil is the place where my own darkness is most likely to come out" says the same thing from outside. This says it in my own voice, and I recognized it.

"If you decide to be less charitable, it won't feel "wrong." It will just feel like what you must do to protect yourself in this rotten world." The chapter has this, but in the second file it comes early and it's about me, the one who got the contempt. It made the spread feel like something that happened to me, not just to his enemy.

**What the chapter was right to leave out or change.**

The Taunt. The long italic speech, ending "*I know you can hear this, but you can't prove it. Good luck.*" It's too much. It made the darkness sound like a movie villain, which is the thing the book says not to do with people. The chapter's one-line version, "*Yes, we ultimately cause harm through the same mechanism—but I'm going to get away with mine, by making you rot for yours.*", is enough.

The Agent. "It behaves like an agent, so I'll refer to it as one." Then "his "dark" part," "**infection**," "**seducing**," "The plan is Machiavellian." By then I was thinking about demons, which the chapter avoided. The chapter's "I think it's trying to protect me, and the getting away is a side effect" is what made the part believable to me. The second file never says it's afraid. It just says it's dark. My therapist would say a protector that's only described as an enemy will dig in. That's the book's own point, turned on itself.

"**The only reason we ever turn away is to enable harm, and we cannot intend harm without turning away**." I refused it. "The only reason" is too big. I turn away from things because they hurt to look at, sometimes, and no one gets harmed. The chapter didn't make that claim, and I trusted it more for it.

The Loop. I skimmed it. The chapter's "My stories kept changing; my conclusion never did" does it in one line.

"Why that should be, I'll come to later." That's you talking to yourself about the book.

"Every road funnels into the same conclusion." Then the second file does the same thing to me that it describes. "Of course not." "Harder, almost certainly." The chapter used the same list of four ("Deny it, and you're the kind of person who does...") but framed it as "If you've been on his end, you know how it closes around you." That's an invitation. The second file's version is a closed door.

The "we all know" everywhere. "We all know this is how it works. We have to, or else it wouldn't work." Maybe. But I want to find it first.

**Order and length.** The second file starts on the memory with no preamble. I missed the chapter's "Or maybe not," which is what got me to sit down. But the second file's order, receiving end first and then sending, makes a kind of sense. You learn the look by getting it, then you find out you give it. For someone who doesn't think she does contempt, that might be the better door. For me, the chapter's order worked: I was already the one with the look, so it made sense to start me there.

Both are long. The second file feels longer, because it has so many short sections, each one restarting. The chapter's Corruption section and the second file's Taunt and Loop are the same kind of drag: the mechanism explained past where I'd already got it.

The comparison also showed me the chapter's ending problem more clearly. The second file ends once, on the thing that's been quietly knowing all along. If the chapter ended on something like that, I wouldn't have felt it end three times.

But I'd copy out three sentences from the second file and tape them inside the front cover for her: "Both people may have stopped your misbehavior, and so both will leave confident that what they did "worked."", "Malice is frightening. Contempt is malice that believes it's righteous, and that's what makes it **maddening**.", and "It isn't hard because of what you did, which may have been nothing much.
