## Anna (49, hospice social worker in Minneapolis; Karma Kagyu, ngöndro done; raised Polish Catholic)

### Stage 1 (preface, introduction, Chapter 1)

Dear Aditya,

I read it steadily, not devoured. My attention went slack in two places: the introduction, before you'd shown me anything, and the middle of "The Corruption," where it starts to feel like an argument being closed around me. Chapter 1 itself held me most of the way, and one part of it is still with me tonight.

**What happened, in order**

The preface I took at face value. "Plant medicines" made me blink, I won't pretend otherwise, but you said it plainly and moved on, and I liked that you admitted you aren't living it. The warning paragraph ("Go slowly, and stop when you need to. Nothing here is a verdict on you.") read a little like the handout I give families. I don't mind it, but I noticed it.

The introduction is where I first stiffened. "The mind is intrinsically compassionate. Evil is nothing but a mind turned away from itself." Fine. I've heard my lama say something close to the first, and the nuns said something close to the second when I was nine (Augustine, evil as the absence of good; you'll hear about that from every Catholic you hand this to). But then: *it means we are being "evil" in some sense.* I've sat with eighteen years of families at the worst moment of their lives, many of them hating somebody. If I said that word to them it would end the conversation. You say right away it's not an attack, and that you'll earn it. I decided to let you try. That was a tentative buy, not a refusal.

Then the Devil and the conspiracies, in the introduction. My uncle in Chicago still believes Smolensk was an assassination. That paragraph nearly cost you me before Chapter 1 started. "You don't need to believe that to read on" saved it, barely. I'd move it much later, or out of the introduction entirely. It is a promise of something strange before you've given me anything ordinary.

Chapter 1 opened and I relaxed, because it asked me questions instead of telling me things.

**The checks, honestly**

The wrong I knew at the time. The one that came up, without my choosing it, was the year of the divorce. Zosia was nine. After every weekend at her father's I asked her questions in the car. What did you eat, who was there, was Kristen there, what did Kristen say about Mom. I told myself I needed to know she was safe. I knew while I was doing it that I was using my daughter as a listening device. Did the knowledge feel new when I finally owned it, in therapy, two years later? No. It felt like something I had been standing next to the whole time. Your word "limbo" is right for it.

"Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" I sat with that one. It's a good question and I couldn't answer it any other way than you want me to. The part that knew never made a case. The part that asked questions in the car had a dozen.

"Could you have?" (done it without justifications). No. I'm fairly sure no.

Now my first real refusal: *We like to call such knowledge "unconscious," but it isn't. It was right there to see*. For this wrong, yes. But I've done six years of therapy. There are things about my father's drinking I truly did not see, and seeing them took years and a professional. You're right about the car rides and I don't think you're right in general. I could follow after that because you were talking about wrongs I did, and for those, I'd grant it. But the sentence is broader than what it needs.

The remorse check. "Have you ever felt it over something you did out of decency?" Here I have a lot of evidence, and it doesn't sit as neatly as your answer. I watch daughters feel something exactly like remorse over taking their mother off the feeding tube. It was the decent thing. They go against themselves, they say. Your next sentence anticipates this, that it turns out to be "regret that it cost me, anger at being taken advantage of, guilt at breaking a rule I was raised on, or feeling foolish." Maybe. Guilt at breaking a rule, often, the rule being "you don't let your mother starve." But from where I sit, the difference between that and remorse is not as easy to see as you make it sound, and the list felt a little convenient, as if it were built to catch anything that doesn't fit. I'd grant it in my own case. I don't feel remorse over the decent things I've done. But I'd be careful telling a grieving daughter it's "something else."

The two strangers. Bought, completely. The first stranger is every person who has ever explained to me what kind of mother I am. The second is my lama, three years ago, in a short interview, who said I was using my practice to stay angry at my ex-husband in a more refined way. In front of the translator. It stung more than anything Kristen has ever said, and I didn't dig in for a second. "Whatever he was standing up for wasn't *his*." Yes. That's exactly why it didn't hurt in the wrong place.

The encounter I read with Dr. Brekke in mind, our medical director, who rushes families through the goals-of-care conversation so he can get to the next one. Last month he told a son, more or less, that his father was "actively dying, so there's not a lot to discuss," and walked out. In the team meeting the next day I didn't say much. I didn't have to. I looked at him. Your narration of the look is accurate to the ounce: "*I'm a better kind of thing than you.*" And "it's the *good* kind of darkness—the kind that fights the real kind." I've said nearly that sentence to a colleague about Brekke. "Someone has to hold him to account." And then, as you say, he got more defensive, and I took that as proof.

The two buttons. I didn't answer the way I expected. With Kristen, I'd press the pill. But when I stayed with it, I wanted the pill to work and then for her to wake up and remember everything she'd said to Zosia about me, and feel it. The pill as a delivery system for the punishment. I laughed out loud at myself, alone in the kitchen. That's yours; the exercise did that.

**What I'll carry out of the room**

"**Love and contempt are addressed to the same place.** One says *I know you're in there.* The other says *there's nobody in there*, and they both say it to the somebody who is."

I stopped reading there for a while. In my work I watch families speak to people who can no longer answer. "Mom, I know you're in there." I say it myself. I have never once thought of contempt as addressed to the same place. When Kristen posts about "blended family harmony" and then tells my daughter I'm "intense," she is talking to someone in me, and it lands precisely there, on the part that knows I'm a decent mother. If there were nobody in there it couldn't hurt. That's not an argument for me. It's a thing I felt in my chest.

And then the next paragraphs: *What I've been hiding isn't that I could do what the "bad people" do. It's that I'm already doing it.* I didn't need you to say it. I'd already got there by myself, about the car. Kristen talks about me to my daughter. I pumped my daughter for information about Kristen. I have spent six years being sure these are different. They are different in degree. I'm not sure they're different in kind. I don't enjoy writing that.

**From my seat as a practitioner**

Said better than my tradition usually manages: contempt as a transaction between two people, carried by gaze and tone, "unquotable." My teachers talk about ignorance and the kleshas inside one mind. They rarely talk about what my anger does in your mind, and teaches you to do. "The signal is the pretense that there is no signal" I'll remember. So is "Badness is the result of turning away, not its cause."

Thin or overclaimed: *What you hid it from is what you call "I" when nothing is being hidden.* And then: *That's why I call it myself.* In Mahamudra I've been taught very carefully not to find a self that's home. You hedge it ("I'm not claiming there's a thing in there you could point to"), and I accept the hedge. But for a book "for friends who sit," calling the nature of mind "myself" will bother anyone in my sangha who has had pointing-out instruction. It bothered me.

"Pure evil" as this error at maximum, and the hostage at the window. I have seen that once, honestly. A man in his eighties who had beaten his children, dying, and for about a minute something in his face looked out of it. Once. Most of the time I've seen nothing at all. I'd believe the hostage is there; I wouldn't say I've seen it reliably, and I wouldn't want you to say it more firmly than you do.

"This was intentional, and done in limbo." I buy it tentatively. With Brekke, yes, I steered. But I would want to go slower with that word, "intentional," before I gave it to a family.

**Where I was handled**

Not often. But a few times. "I think that's what the second stranger gave you." He gave me something, but you're telling me what. "Did it feel like an accident?" is asked in a way that has only one answer. "If your answers were mine" at the start of "The Way Out" is fine, it leaves me room. The section "The Way Out" in general is where I felt most argued at: "So what I turn from is the baseline, and the turning is a departure from it." That's a conclusion presented as if I'd just reached it. I hadn't. I was still in the kitchen with the pill.

The middle of "The Corruption" (from "Notice how my stories kept changing" through "How could this trap work unless") is where I slackened. It was true and I'd already got it.

**What I would cut**

From the introduction: the Devil-and-conspiracies paragraph; the game-theory footnote (I skipped it); "I realize these are all very lofty ideas. Let me now pull them into the realm of our everyday experience." From Chapter 1: some of "The Corruption." "My enemy sees I'm turned away, even though I don't. *Because* I don't." and the paragraph after it, and the "how it closes around you" paragraph, say the same thing three ways. And "In brief" could go; the section already said it.

I wouldn't cut a word of the two strangers, the buttons, or "addressed to the same place."

The last line of the chapter, about remembering the ground, is close to something Rinpoche says. I didn't mind hearing it again.

Anna

### Stage 2 (Chapters 2 to 4, "The Picture I Use")

Dear Aditya,

Steady, with one stretch I devoured and two where I nearly put it down. I devoured the middle of Chapter 4, the checks and the sentences with and without "the extra." I skimmed the factory farms, the thousand-year tree, the meaning crisis and the paragraph on judging politicians. I would have put the book down in "What I'm claiming," at Hitler, and again at the end of "The Picture I Use." I didn't, because you'd asked me, and because Chapter 3 had already given me something by then.

**Did it pick up where Chapter 1 left off?**

Chapter 2 opened on the face, and that felt like a continuation, not a new start. I did the exercise twice. The first time with a patient's wife I sat with on Thursday, and it was easy, because that's my job; I've looked at a thousand faces until they became a place somebody looks out of. The second time with Kristen. You wrote: "For me the story is there before the face is." For me the story is there before the face is, too. I actually saw her profile picture before I saw her, the one with the three kids and the dog on the dock. That's the turning away, live, as you say, and I'll grant it.

Then Chapter 2 started telling me things I'd already been given. The paragraph beginning "You can check that from the receiving end, and it's subtle, so go slowly" is the Chapter 1 passage almost word for word, down to "One says *I know you're in there.* The other says *there's nobody in there*, and they both say it to the somebody who is." The first time, that sentence stopped me for twenty minutes. The second time I noticed I was being shown it again, and it lost something. Leave it in one place.

Chapter 3 opens on "why do they turn away in the first place?" Chapter 2 didn't really leave me with that question; it left me with a list. But Chapter 3 earned its opening anyway. Chapter 4 opens on Chapter 2 ("Chapter 2 claimed there is one guide") rather than on where Chapter 3 ended, "what to do from inside it." It does answer that, but it took me a page to see that it was answering it.

I got lost in the chapter numbers several times. "Chapter 4 says why there's no chooser to find" (I think that was Chapter 3). "Now you're doing what you did in your own case in Chapter 2." "This is the executive from Chapter 2 again." I went back to look for an executive and there isn't one. "The interlude asked you to check that in your own memory": I haven't read an interlude. "for a reason Chapter 5 spells out": there's no Chapter 5 in what you sent me. In Chapter 4 there's "the parasite's line from Chapter 4 again" and "the fear from Chapter 4." I'm not editing you; I'm telling you that each time it happened I stopped trusting that I'd read carefully, and that's a bad feeling to give a reader who's already working hard.

**What I'm claiming**

This is where it turned into a lecture. You say "I'm not asking you to take them from me," and then I was given ten bolded claims. Some I'd sign: "Compassion is not wisdom," though in my tradition that sentence will confuse people, because we're taught compassion and wisdom as the two wings, inseparable, and "wisdom" means prajna, not knowledge and skill. You mean something more like competence. Say competence, and my sangha won't argue with you over a word.

"There is one kind of evil, and this is it." I didn't buy it. Maybe it's true. But I've been asked to check it "against the cases you'd already use the word for," and I have one case I'd use the word for, the man with the children I mentioned last time, and I can't run that check in a paragraph. The Hitler paragraph I read as an argument won by definition. That's where I would have closed the book, if it hadn't been for you asking.

"In an important sense, nobody's there." Be careful with that sentence around people like me. "Nobody's there" is what families say about their mother with late-stage dementia, and it's the thing I spend my working life gently disagreeing with. I know you mean something else. But it jarred, and "It's incoherent" right after it sounded like the part that proves, not the part that knows.

**What landed**

Chapter 3, almost all of it. "The view goes, then the reasons arrive, and then it feels like wanting." I tried the experiment: hold the eyes and decide to stop seeing. I couldn't find the moment either. "The want was innocent; the label was borrowed." That one I'll keep.

The parasite. I expected to hate it and didn't. "A groove in attention so old that its shape looks like intent" is close to how I was taught about habitual tendencies, and the paragraph on the word's drawbacks was honest enough that I let you have it. "The exception is the ego. The parasite is the ego defending it" is clean Lojong. Most of my sangha would recognize it as self-cherishing and be glad of the new angle.

Then this, which is the one that changes my week: "you can only be good *relative* to someone worse." I've needed Kristen to be worse. If she's a decent stepmother, what am I? The one who left, the "intense" one. And the Chapter 4 check put a number on it: "if the harm simply stopped tomorrow, with nobody punished and nobody exposed, would you feel relief, or robbed?" If Kristen stopped talking about me to Zosia tomorrow, and Zosia never found out what she'd said, I would feel robbed. I want Zosia to see it. I want to be vindicated in my daughter's eyes. That isn't protecting my daughter. It's the car rides again.

And "It's consumed." I hate-read Kristen's Instagram. Two or three times a week, late, in bed. "Blended family harmony." The hot little thing you name with a word I won't repeat to my mother is exactly what I feel when I scroll. I hadn't counted that as contempt, because she never sees me do it. I'm going to stop, or at least notice when I open the app. That's a real change, and it came from your book.

The pairs in Chapter 4 I'm taking into Tuesday's team meeting. "*I don't think you understood what that did to her.* With the extra: *I don't think you're capable of understanding what that did to her.*" That is exactly the sentence I've been composing in my head for Dr. Brekke since last month. I'll say the first one. I don't know if I can keep the pause out of it.

And pity: "pity, which passes for compassion and is contempt with a soft face: it needs them to stay below me." My profession runs on that danger, and we talk about it badly. My tradition says it better, honestly ("idiot compassion," and the teachings on pity as compassion's near enemy), but your version is sharper because it says what pity needs.

The most practice-true thing in the book is one short paragraph near the end of Chapter 4: "look at the contempt itself, not the person. Where is it? What's it made of?" That's Mahamudra. It's how I was taught to work with anger: look at its nature, not its object. I'd have liked it to be much bigger. For a book "for people who practice," it's tucked away after forty pages of argument.

**A note for people who were hurt**

I read this as I'd read anything I might hand a client. The first paragraph is good. "Staying turned toward someone never means staying near them" is good, and so is putting distance first. I'd give a survivor those two paragraphs. By the fourth paragraph it had become a lecture to someone who'd been hurt, about what their own darkness wants. "You, of all people, deserve to know that" is the line where a client of mine would stop listening. They would hear: even your rage is serving the abuser. You say you don't mean that, and I believe you. It's too long to keep from meaning it.

**"The Picture I Use"**

I held it at arm's length and read it through. I'm Polish; I know 2 Corinthians and I know Mara, and that part I didn't mind. "it isn't real, and it must be fought" I'd sign. Then the institutions and the conspiracy. *This is the truth behind the "evil global conspiracy."* The italic note said this was a picture with no check. That sentence doesn't talk like a picture; it talks like a finding. And the parenthesis ("part of us knows it, and we need it to be that way") I refused. My uncle would read this page and hear that he was right all along, only about a different conspirator. It didn't spoil anything before it, because you'd told me to hold it loosely. But it made me trust the next chapter a little less.

**Told again**

The receiving-end paragraph in Chapter 2 (as above). The far end in Chapter 3, the hostage at the window, which Chapter 1 had already given me in two sentences that worked better. "Watch any two sides go to war: both grow," in Chapter 3, and again in "The Picture I Use," nearly verbatim. The parasite frame's failure modes in Chapter 3 and again in Chapter 4 ("the parasite frame can fail in the opposite direction"). In Chapter 4, "contempt for the people who still have contempt," after Chapter 1's "people who haven't seen through this trap yet." And "contempt with a soft face" twice in Chapter 4.

The mushrooms footnote: you said it honestly, and it shouldn't count as evidence, as you say. But it's the second time drugs have been mentioned, and half my sangha will stop there. Rinpoche would not hand this to them.

**Where it ended**

For me it ended at "only the light, turned away." That's the right sentence. The last paragraph, "The battle is real, and it was always the light's alone," I read three times and didn't understand "the light's alone." The final clause, "it's empty and tell you to give your life to it in the same breath," I liked very much, as a practitioner; that's emptiness and bodhicitta in one line. But it came after an ending, as an encore.

**If they had to lose a third**

"What I'm claiming," cut to three or four claims, or gone. The repeated receiving-end paragraph in Chapter 2. The far-end paragraph and the footnote in Chapter 3. The world-and-institutions stretch at the end of Chapter 3 (factory farms, rainforests, the tree, the arch). The meaning crisis and the paragraph on whom we hand power. The last two paragraphs of the note for people who were hurt. The second half of "The Picture I Use." Keep every check in Chapter 4, and make the paragraph on looking at the contempt itself twice as long.

Anna

### Stage 3 (the appendices)

Dear Aditya,

Dutifully, mostly. Appendix 3 I read eagerly, the way I read anything about my own tradition written by someone outside it, half to learn and half to check. Appendix 2 I read steadily and some of it with pleasure. Appendix 1 was a drag from "Why it had to be this way" on, and I skimmed "For the lawyers among you" and the reading list entirely. Would I have read them if you hadn't asked? Appendix 3, yes. Appendix 2, the first page, and then I'd have been glad to stop. Appendix 1, no.

The first thing I read was the italic line at the top: "*The three appendices were written with Claude's help.*" I'll tell you what that did, since you asked what happened in me. It made me read the Mahamudra section with a pencil. I went looking for the places where someone had smoothed my tradition into something tidier than it is. I found fewer than I expected. But I was reading as an auditor rather than as a friend, and I don't think that's what you want from people like me. I also found myself wondering, for a moment, about the chapters. I put that down. I mention it because others will pick it up.

**Appendix 1**

The first section held me longer than I thought it would. The list of what the look delivers ("The turn," "The intent," "The wanting," and so on) is what I felt at that team meeting, laid out in order, and "It's why malice is frightening and contempt is maddening" is a sentence I'll steal for a training I give on difficult family meetings. Brekke has never frightened me. He maddens me.

Then the chain, and "Not one of those is a separate fact," and "why it isn't a metaphor," and I felt the book become a proof. I've been taught all my life, in two traditions, that the argument for these things is not where they live. You say so yourself, in the italic note at the top: "I didn't arrive at any of this analytically." I believed that note more than the chain.

Two places I'd stop you, from my work. The first is again the remorse that "never runs backward." Appendix 1 leans on it hard ("remorse never runs backward," "None of those happens, in any case I've found"). I told you about the daughters and the feeding tubes. I'm still not sure what they feel is remorse, but a book whose central link depends on that sentence should look harder at the people who'd test it, and those people are in my building every day.

The second is "Nobody is surprised by their own wrong when they finally see it." I've sat with a man at the end who said, of his son, "I didn't know I'd done that to him." I believe he knew the act. I'm not sure he knew the size of it. Maybe that's your distinction between knowing and feeling the weight. But "Nobody" is a big word for a hospice.

The test in "What I think is really happening" I took personally, in the good sense: "Take the people others bring their worst to, because they see what's there. They can look at the worst person, see everything you see, and no verdict forms." That's supposed to be my profession. It's true of the best of us. It's true of a Franciscan sister I worked under for six years, and of Rinpoche. It isn't true of me on a Tuesday with Dr. Brekke. Which I suppose is your point: the verdict tracks me, not him.

*A Course in Miracles*, and the sentence about none of it unfolding in time: I held it at arm's length. It's marked, which helped. Half of my sangha would put the book down at the Course. The other half have it on their nightstand.

**Appendix 2**

This was the one I most enjoyed and least needed. "Most of what this book concludes is old. I want to say that plainly" disarmed me, and I wish the introduction had said it.

Projective identification I know from my own therapy. I was waiting for it from Jung onward, and when it came, "So that claim is false, and I withdraw it" made me laugh. I liked you for it. It also reads as a draft talking to itself. Either say it up front, or rewrite the section so the five claims stand without the withdrawal.

Murdoch's mother-in-law I hadn't read. I will. A woman who changes how she sees her son's wife without the wife doing anything differently. You can imagine why that one stayed with me.

The passage I most wanted, and it's one clause: "I only got to the other thing because it was done to me and I had, for once, no way of not noticing." What was done to you? That's the story the book is missing. Chapter 1 has you as the first stranger. It never has you as the target, and I think that's where you'd stop sounding like an argument and start sounding like a man.

**Appendix 3**

From my seat: mostly right, and in places better than I expected. Marigpa as non-recognition, the obscurations as adventitious, "the nature of thought is dharmakaya," Shantideva's stick and the hand. These are fair. The Aspiration of Samantabhadra I know less well than a Dzogchen student would, but the blankness with no motive matches what I was taught: the first not-recognizing isn't a sin. Coming from a Catholic childhood, that was the single most freeing thing I learned in eighteen years. You put it well. And I like that you keep the fear account and the blank account in order rather than pretending they're one.

The pointing-out paragraph is careful, and I'm grateful for the care ("I don't mean the two are the same thing"). My lama would still wince at the comparison. I didn't, because of what he said to me in that interview.

Eckhart I loved: "When the will turns from the One, it becomes its own devil." My grandmother would have had me say a rosary for reading the Gospel of Philip, but Eckhart she might have let pass.

What I wanted less: the DiLullo quotation is long and says one thing, and the film *Revolver* pulled me straight out of the tradition and into a Guy Ritchie movie. "The Christians gave that trick to Satan. The Buddhists gave it to the self. I think they were describing one thing." I'd want to argue with that over tea, not in an appendix. My teachers don't say the self hides; they say there isn't one and we cling to it anyway. Close, maybe. Not one thing.

When you write that "none of them claim that non-recognition coordinates across minds," collective karma might want a word. I don't think it says what you say. But someone in my sangha will raise it, and the appendix should know it.

"What would count against it." I read the three marks of fully seeing twice. I haven't met the case either. I've met people where I couldn't tell, and I don't think I could run your three marks on them. They're good marks for a philosopher, not for someone standing at a bedside.

**What belongs in the book, and the reverse**

The caution at the very end belongs in the book itself, near the end of Chapter 4: "The easiest way to misread non-fabrication is as *nothing to do*." That is the most common failure in Western sanghas, mine included, and the book never names it where the reader of the book will meet it. Shantideva's stick and hand belongs in Chapter 3, next to the parasite; it would do in one line what the parasite takes a page to do. And so does your own story, from Appendix 2.

The reverse: "What I'm claiming" belongs back here, and so does the second half of "The Picture I Use." So does the passage on the far end and the mushrooms footnote. The appendices say nobody has to read them; that's the right place for what I didn't have to believe.

**Would I give it to my sangha?**

Not as it stands, to everyone. But yes, to some.

To Margaret, who leads our Thursday sitting and works as a public defender. She has the hottest contempt of anyone I know, for prosecutors, and the most practice behind it, and she'll argue with every page, which is what she likes. I'd give her the whole thing. To my friend Deb in the sangha, who is in the middle of her own divorce: Chapter 1, Chapter 3 and the checks in Chapter 4, and nothing else yet. To my therapist, honestly, for the car rides.

Not to the newer people, who would come away with "parasite" and use it on each other within a month; you say so yourself in Chapter 3. Not to the older students who've had pointing-out instruction: the "myself" in Chapter 1, the mushrooms and the *Course* would close it for them before Chapter 2. Not to Kasia, who was hurt badly as a child, unless I'd first taken out the note for people who were hurt, cut it to its first and third paragraphs, and handed that to her on its own. And not to Rinpoche, until it's finished; you asked for practitioners with better communication skills than yours, and he'd want to see the drafts after they've passed through a few more of us.

And Zosia, someday, the two strangers. Not yet.

Anna

## David (61, retired mathematician in Boulder; Drikung Kagyu, three-year retreat; raised Swedish Lutheran)

### Stage 1 (preface, introduction, Chapter 1)

Aditya,

Steadily through the preface and introduction, which I'll admit I read with my arms crossed. Then I devoured Chapter 1 from "The Encounter" to the end of "The Corruption." My attention went slack in the preface's long paragraph about what this version leaves out, and again for a stretch in "The Spread."

**What happened, in order.**

The preface I took as a letter from a friend, which it is. "Plant medicines" made me raise an eyebrow and then lower it; I've sat with enough people to know insight arrives by strange doors. The paragraph beginning "So this leaves out a great deal" is a paragraph about the book rather than the book, and it lost me.

The introduction is where I first refused, and it was early. "The mind is intrinsically compassionate. Evil is nothing but a mind turned away from itself." I can sit with the first sentence; in my lineage we would say emptiness and compassion are not two, and I have tasted that, not only read it. The second sentence I refused, or rather I refused the word. I was raised Swedish Lutheran, and "evil" still arrives in my body with a pastor attached. Then came the claim that contempt "is only coherent if both sender and receiver already know" that the nature of mind is compassion and that this is common knowledge. That is a theorem stated before its proof, and I am a mathematician. I did what Chapter 1 later asks and held it as an unplaced piece. Then I read the footnote. Common knowledge "in the game-theoretic sense, approximately" made me laugh out loud, because common knowledge is the most fragile object in that whole field. One lost message and the infinite tower collapses; Rubinstein's email game is the standard example. Approximate common knowledge is a real notion, but it is a different animal with weaker consequences. I wanted to see what you meant before I granted it, and I was able to follow on after refusing, because the introduction told me "You don't need to believe that to read on" and seemed to mean it.

### Stage 2 (Chapters 2 to 4, "The Picture I Use")

Aditya,

Steady, with two stretches where I devoured it and one where I skimmed. I devoured Chapter 3 from the choice paragraph through "A note for people who were hurt," and Chapter 4 from "There are two kinds of judgment" to the end. Told again.

There's a mechanical problem too, and I'm a lawyer, so I'll tell you, though it's not the kind of thing you asked for. Your chapter references don't match this book. What landed.

Chapter 3, the choice. "Try it: hold someone's eyes and decide, while holding them, to stop seeing them. See if you can find the moment." I tried it on Denise, at dinner, during a small argument about her brother. I couldn't find the moment either. What I found was "I've had a long day," which is close to the exact label you give. "The want was innocent; the label was borrowed." That's good. My lineage would call it the gap between the thought arising and the grasping, and I've never heard it put so plainly.

"So all evil is innocent, in one exact sense." My brother-in-law would throw the book across the room at that sentence, and I think it's true. What saves it is the next part: responsibility "lives in how fast you catch the blindness, in what you repair when sight returns, and in what you've done to give the blindness less room next time." I have argued versions of that in sentencing memos for thirty years without being able to say it. You said it. That paragraph alone is worth handing to some judges I know.

"A note for people who were hurt." I read it as Terrence would, and then as myself, the man who's been carrying Terrence's case. It's careful and it doesn't flinch. "Staying turned toward someone never means staying near them." That's right, and plenty of preachers I grew up with never managed it. "It is also much harder than contempt, which is why contempt gets sold to you as the strong option." My grandmother marched in Oakland. That's what she would have said about the people who told her nonviolence was weakness.

Chapter 4, the line that changes something this week. Your examples of the extra: "*You lied to me.* With the extra: *Of course you lied to me.*" I say that to clients. I have said those words, with that "of course," to a nineteen-year-old in a holding cell who had lied to me about where he was that night. I told myself it was me being straight with him. It wasn't. It was the verdict, and I watched it land, and I pretended I hadn't. I have a client meeting Thursday. I'll be listening for the "of course."

The relief-or-robbed check. If the prosecutor were simply never exposed, Terrence whole, nobody punished? Robbed. I'll say it plainly. It's the honest answer and I don't like it. What I refused.

"The test is simple: does it tell you what to do to the person in front of you before you've looked at them?" I stopped hard here. Lady Justice wears a blindfold on purpose. A rule that tells a judge what to do before she has looked at my client is sometimes the only thing that protects a Black kid from what she'd see if she looked. Mandatory minimums are a disaster, yes. But blind application of the rules is also what equal protection was built for, and my people bled for it. You come back with "a contract, a precept, a grievance procedure exist because people who were sure they had us fully in view didn't." That's the Brady rule itself, and it's the best sentence in the section. But the test right before it cuts against it, and I don't think you've noticed.

"Every decision made while the people it touches are held out of view belongs to it, and I haven't found a second kind." That's a broad claim, made flat. Under it, a juror who never meets the victim's family is doing evil. I know what you mean, and the turning-away is real. But as stated it convicts too many people.

The Hitler paragraph. "*Thought.*" That's a debater's trick. It may be right, but it reads like a closing argument that's pleased with itself.

"The Picture I Use."

I held it at arm's length, as invited, and through the first half that worked. Second Corinthians landed on the Baptist in me. "Masquerades as an angel of light" is the verse my pastor used on the men who quoted scripture at the Panthers. And "it isn't real, and it must be fought" is as good a gloss on emptiness and compassion as I've read.

Then the institutions. "At some level, we need them to remain." Then the parenthesis: concrete conspiracies "feed on our collective contempt, and part of us knows it, and we need it to be that way." I'm a Black man. COINTELPRO was a concrete conspiracy. Tuskegee was a concrete conspiracy. The men who ran them needed nothing from my grandmother's contempt, and she didn't need them to remain. You said the piece had no check, and I accept that. But "we" and "all of us" are claims about everyone, and they land on people who were the targets. That's where I walked around the block. Where it ended.

For me it ended at "only the light, turned away." That's the sentence where Chapter 1's limbo and Chapter 4's looking became one thing, and I stopped. The last paragraph, "it was always the light's alone," is lovely, and a practitioner will hear the Heart Sutra in it. But it's a flourish after the verdict's already in. It survives as tight shoulders all the way to the courthouse.
