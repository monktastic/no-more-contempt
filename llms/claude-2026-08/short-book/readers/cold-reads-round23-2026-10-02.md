The chapter never uses this, and for a reader raised on paperwork it's the most persuasive thread in the second file.

"And the thing is, it doesn't feel like a loss at all. It feels like you've gotten a bit wiser. A little more realistic about how this world operates." That is the retired officer's disease, precisely. It's Bill, and on bad days it's me. The chapter's "I'd be the chump" gets the mechanism. This gets how it feels from inside, which is wisdom, and that's why no one fights it. Keep both.

"That's what your conscience is: the part of you that keeps other people in view." And "Demote it, and you see a little less of everyone." The chapter never says what conscience is in plain terms. This does, in a sentence I could repeat to my rector. It's better than "better nature," which always sounds to me a little like a greeting card.

"*Our conscience is the only thing that warns us when we've strayed from it.*" Worth keeping. I'd have to think about whether it's true, and that's to its credit.

"We confuse "fierce" with "dark" not because we truly don't know the difference, but because we *need* to not know it." This is the honest answer to my relieved-for-cause objection, put more sharply than the chapter puts it.

"You'd take that bet at any odds. But on what evidence? You'd never met him." A navigator's question, and a good one. It's the hypocrisy argument made concrete. I'd trade some of the chapter's "Common knowledge" abstraction for it.

"Check whether, from here, looking at the flash without the reasons, you still endorse it." This is a better check than several in the chapter, because it lets me find the answer myself. I tried it with Thanksgiving. Without the reasons I don't endorse "That's an interesting view." With them I do. That gap is the whole matter, and the check showed it to me without anyone telling me.

"Would the big ones be easier to recall, or harder?" And "If all you could hear was your own conscience—no justifications to hide behind—could you have even done what you did?" Both are fair questions that leave room. The first sent me back to the Pruitt report. I'd remembered it in ten seconds, but I realized I've never once, in thirty years, said Ellis's name aloud to anyone. That's the "harder."

"It shows you a man with nobody home." Short and exact.

The ending. "It's still there in you, quietly knowing what it knows, patiently waiting to be remembered." / "Just as it is in him." The chapter ends on me needing to be seen, which is right. This ends on the enemy, and it turned me back toward Pruitt and toward Jordan with something other than a verdict. I'd consider whether the chapter's last paragraph could close with that last line.

**What the chapter was right to leave out or change.**

"then **fuck it**. Game on." The chapter's "why the hell shouldn't I?" is enough, and "I'd be the chump" is better than either. The obscenity cost the second file more with me than the line earned.

"Every road funnels into the same conclusion." The irony is that the second file then funnels me. The chapter's list of the four responses, followed by "The stories keep changing; the conclusion never does," lands, and it lands because you'd shown it from your side first.

The whole of "The Taunt" monologue, ending "*I know you can hear this, but you can't prove it. Good luck.*" I skimmed it. It's a villain's speech, and it made the thing theatrical. The chapter's "The signal is the pretense that there is no signal" does it in one line.

"The Loop," "The Righteous Appearance," and "The plan is Machiavellian." I skimmed these too. They read like the author working the thing out on paper. The chapter compressed them and lost nothing I missed.

"If he knew it fully, then he sounds like a mustache-twirling villain." The chapter's "When I'm sure someone was fully aware of what they were doing, I've made them a villain. When I'm sure they had no idea, I've made them a machine." is the same point, and better, since villain and machine are a true pair.

"It is an **infection**, and it just spread itself to you." The chapter's "corruption" is the right word. Corruption is a moral word with a long history, and infection is a medical one.

"Try this: go out in public, find a stranger, and shoot them a dehumanizing gaze. It's hard to do, isn't it?" I refused this one. For me it's not hard at all. I've done a correct version of it for decades; it's called a courteous nod. The chapter's cashier exercise, which asked whether either act needed a reason, found me where this one failed. The question with room in it worked, and the question with the answer attached didn't.

"**The only reason we ever turn away is to enable harm, and we cannot intend harm without turning away**." Bold type and "ever," about all of us. I couldn't check that. The chapter's remorse asymmetry carries the same weight in a form I could check in my own case.

"It's the thing we call **evil**." Set against the chapter's "It has the same flavor as what we call evil." The chapter's is quieter, and I came round to it. The second file's I'd have refused outright.

"At the same time, describing it as "you" risks creating shame." Therapy language, and it closed me up. The same goes for "Seeing this is the only thing that makes full responsibility safe enough to bear." The chapter puts it as duty, "It's my mind, and I answer for what it does," and that's the language I trust.

"And it's what this book exists to provide you." And "Why that should be, I'll come to later." The first is a sales line and the second is bookkeeping. The chapter keeps its mouth shut about itself, and that's to its credit.

What the second file lacks and the chapter must keep: the cold version; "Love and contempt are addressed to the same place"; the integrity saying and "But someone always was: me"; the cashier; the remorse asymmetry; the worst-enemy pill with your own admission; "The stupid, the slow and the weak get the same verdict, with better cover". Those are the passages that reached me personally.

**Order and length.** The second file is considerably longer and feels longer still. Its middle, from "The Righteous Appearance" through "The Taunt," is where I'd cut first. The comparison showed me two things about order. First, putting the reader on the receiving end before asking him to see himself as the sender is the right sequence. I'd want the chapter's general-and-Tolliver moment to come before the author's encounter, or in place of its abstraction. Second, the chapter's habit of saying the better-nature claim three times is worse after reading the second file. The second file says it once, as "the part of you that keeps other people in view," and lets it work. And "When You Send It" comes late in the second file and at about the right place. Asking me for my own flash after I've been the target is much harder to refuse than asking for it cold.

If I could have one chapter, it would be yours, the first, with the two strangers as its frame, the sweeter-than-justice sentence, the certainty-and-urgency paragraph, and the record thread carried over. And with not one more page.
