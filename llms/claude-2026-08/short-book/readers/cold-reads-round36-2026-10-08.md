## Martha (67, retired school librarian; Quaker, hospice volunteer)

### Stage 1

Dear Aditya,

I read your chapter at the kitchen table on a Tuesday, with a pencil, the way I used to read anything a publisher's rep left on my desk. I did your exercises as I came to them, with real people. Some of them I'd rather not have met again. Here it is in order.

**The opening.** You asked me to "Hold them tentatively, until you see the picture they assemble." I've had forty years of people telling me what I'll find in myself, so I was braced. Then you wrote "Maybe you'll recognize something of yourself in it. Or maybe not." That loosened my shoulders. I kept reading because of that "Or maybe not."

**The memory.** It came to me at once, which tells you something. In 1997 a board member's wife complained about a novel in my middle-school collection. I did not challenge it. I weeded it. I wrote "poor condition" on the withdrawal slip, and the book was in fine condition. Two girls had checked it out three times each that year. I spent the next several years telling myself that budgets were tight and that I'd picked my battles. When I finally admitted it, to my husband Walt, about five years later, it was not new information. It was like opening a drawer I had been carefully not opening. So yes, your first claim matched. "If that doesn't match anything in your memory, the rest of this won't make sense, and I'd rather you know that now." That sentence did two things to me. It was honest, and I respected it. It was also a gate, and I felt the gate.

Did I deflect when people came close? Yes. A younger librarian asked me once, at an ALA regional, whether I'd ever had a challenge. I said "nothing serious" and changed the subject to her shoes. That was not passive. I chose the shoes.

"Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" I sat with that a long time. The side that argued had a whole speech: budgets, the board, the long game. The side that knew just had the girls' names on the circulation card. It didn't argue. It didn't have to. I bought this one outright.

**The cashier.** Here is where something happened I didn't expect. The kindness went with no reason at all. The cruelty wouldn't go. I tried to sneer at the young man at the Kroger on Third Street who bags so slowly, and nothing came. Then, without meaning to, I pictured the man in the quilted vest who was ahead of me in that line in August and snapped at that same boy for putting the eggs under the cans. And the sneer came straight away, for the man in the vest. So: "Did either one need a reason before it would go?" Yes. My cruelty needed a reason, and I had one ready, filed under rudeness to service workers. I will come back to him. He turned out to be my enemy for the rest of the chapter.

**Remorse.** "But never remorse: that sickening sense of having gone against myself." I tested it. I lent my brother-in-law four thousand dollars in 2009 and never saw it again. I've been angry about it. I've felt like a fool. Never sick at myself. Bought. The bad man lying awake over his secret kindness: I tried to imagine it and the closest I got was a man ashamed of going soft, and you'd already called that something other than remorse. "Maybe some cannot access it, but I doubt it's ever reversed." I bought it tentatively. I liked that you said "I doubt" and not "it never is."

**The Encounter.** It was believable as what a mind does, and I'm sorry to say I could have narrated it myself. In the Kroger line I said to the man in the vest, "He's a person, you know." Pleasantly. The pleasantness was the weapon. I wanted him to know exactly what I thought he was, and I wanted the woman behind me to see me do it. "I'm only holding him to account, and if he reacts poorly, well, it only proves my point." He did react poorly. He said something about old church ladies. And I remember the small warm feeling of having been proven right, which I would have called sadness if you'd asked.

Where I skimmed: the run from "It's not like I'm actually threatening him" through the bit about the invitation. I got it the first time. I didn't skim "Who cares if I give him a little nudge to help him reveal what he truly is?" That one I read twice because it was mine.

**The cold version.** "a politeness with nothing behind it." That is my native tongue. I'm a Quaker and a librarian; we don't do hot. I write people off with great courtesy. I was glad you put it in. Without it I think a lot of quiet people would have decided this chapter was about somebody louder.

**Integrity.** It came at the right time. I had just finished being pleased with myself in the Kroger line and the objection was forming in me: well, what was I supposed to do, let him talk to the boy like that? And there it was. You voiced it, as an objection, in a voice that wasn't quite mine, and then you didn't argue with me. You asked for a memory.

The person who corrected me with integrity was Ruth Coffin, an elder in our meeting, a farmer's widow and a lifelong Republican. In the spring of 2017 I stood up in meeting for worship and gave what I told myself was ministry. It was a political speech with the word "Light" in it. Afterward Ruth took my arm in the parking lot and said, "Martha, that wasn't ministry, and you know it wasn't." She was fiercer than anyone had been with me in years. And I did not dig in, because there was no verdict in her face. She wasn't telling me what I was. She was telling me what I'd done, and assuming I knew better. "Not more gently. Just that their anger was directed at what you did, and not what you are." Yes. Exactly that.

Your teacher story did one thing for me that an argument couldn't: the word *responsive*. He was "searching my face for something." Ruth did that. I had forgotten she did that until you said it. And then you wrote "I don't want to make too much of that afternoon. It worked because there was something in me for it to reach, and I was young and easy to reach. It doesn't always work." I trusted you more for that than for anything before it. A writer who tells me where his story stops working is a writer I'll follow a while longer.

"What is it that two people on opposite sides can both be serving?" I didn't have to think. Friends have a phrase for it, *that of God in every one*. Ruth and I voted opposite ways for fifty years and served the same thing that day in the parking lot. I noticed you didn't say God. I didn't mind. I supplied it, and you left room for me to.

**The Corruption.** This is where I slowed down and had to reread. "In brief: I am in limbo about provoking him, and showing him that I'm in limbo is itself the provocation." Twice through, and I got it the second time. "The signal is the pretense that there is no signal." I read that three times. I think it's true and I think it's the hardest sentence in the chapter. But then this, which needed no rereading at all: "Deny it, and you're the kind of person who does. Get angry, and that's proof too. Stay calm, and you don't even care. Apologize, and it's too convenient." I have been on that end. My son Tom has been on that end, from me. I'll come to Tom.

"From the inside, being proven right and being right are the same experience. The only difference is who produced the evidence." That was insight, not lecture. The man in the vest produced some evidence, and I produced the rest.

**The two buttons.** I want to be honest about this one because it was the most useful minute of the evening. My worst enemy, if I'm truthful, is a principal named Dale Hutchins who eliminated my aide's position in 2003 and then corrected her grammar in front of a room of sixth graders before she'd even been told. I told myself at once I'd push the pill. Then I noticed what I actually wanted: the pill, but slow. Slow enough that he'd have a few years of knowing exactly what he'd been. "Is some part of you itching to mash that first button?" Not the first button. I'd dressed the first button up as the second. That is a Quaker way of mashing it. I wouldn't have found that without the question, and I didn't feel pushed toward it. You told me what was in you and left me alone with mine.

(I winced at "mash." It's the only word in the chapter that sounded younger than you.)

**Whose intention.** "In that sense, it is not "my" intention." I bristled. It sounded like an alibi, and I've heard every alibi a twelve-year-old can make. Then "It acts as if it has goals of its own, and it pursues them intentionally," and I heard old George Fox, frankly, and the seventeenth-century Friends who were not shy about naming the adversary. I didn't refuse it. Then "Don't mistake the grammar for the claim" and "None of this lets me off the hook. It's my mind, and I answer for what it does." That came in time. One paragraph later and I'd have put the book down for the evening.

"When I'm sure someone was fully aware of what they were doing, I've made them a villain. When I'm sure they had no idea, I've made them a machine." That caught me. I do the machine version with Dale Hutchins: *he simply has no inner life*. It's tidier than villainy and it lets me feel charitable.

**The first place I refused.** "And I only do that because, at a level I cannot let myself see, I fear I might be one of them." No. Not for me, not about the man in the vest. My contempt for people who are rude to service workers comes from my mother, who cleaned houses on the north side of Indianapolis, and from the women who called her by her first name and never learned her last. It is loyalty, not fear. I sat with this a long while and I still think so. Then "If you can find even one memory where it's plainly true of you, that's the one to look at." I felt pushed there. It was a polite way of telling me to keep looking until I found what you'd said. I could follow everything after it, though. I just held that one sentence at arm's length. (I'll admit, two days later, that I am less sure about the loyalty being all of it. But I am not going to revise what I felt at the table.)

The "Only a chump" passage landed, and yes, I connected it. "I'd be a chump," in the voice of the man who's been on the receiving end, and then later "Only a chump believes otherwise" as the lesson I taught him. I liked finding that on my own.

**The way out.** "What does "yourself" mean here?" and "I'm not asking a philosophical question." I tried. I went back to the withdrawal slip. What was I hiding it from? My first honest answer was something else than what you point at: I was hiding it from the librarian who stood up at a school-board meeting in 1994 and defended *Bridge to Terabithia*. A picture of myself, in other words, a reputation I kept in my own head. I was a little pleased to have found something you hadn't predicted. Then I sat with it, the way I would in meeting, and I wasn't sure the picture and the thing you mean were so far apart. The picture was what I'd made of it. The thing itself was what made the girls' names on that card hurt. So: something else first, and then, faintly, what you point at. I won't claim more than faintly.

"Here's what I think: our better nature isn't one part among others. It's a description of what we *are* in the absence of all self-deception." Granted as your claim, and I recognized it from my own tradition, so it didn't sound grandiose to me. Friends have been saying something like it since 1652. It might sound grandiose to my sister. "Not because it's nicer. Because it's the one that's home." That I found in myself. That's the line I underlined twice.

**The dehumanizing gaze.** I have to tell you about Tom now. Thanksgiving 2024, at his house in Carmel, I said something about the election and he looked at me, not angrily, just finished. As if I were a type he'd read about. "Can you feel the sense in which the other person was absent, or somehow "turned away" from himself?" Yes. He wasn't there. "Why would he have to turn away at all, if what he is, is essentially bad?" I tried to answer it as Tom. And what I came up with is that he couldn't look at his own mother and think what he was thinking about me. He had to stop seeing me first. A bad man wouldn't have needed to. Tom needed to. That answer frightened me more than I can say, because the next question was "Is it possible that you look a tiny bit like this when treating him with contempt?" and the answer is that I've looked at him that way for two years and told myself it was his doing. I've said to friends at meeting that the distance is his. I don't think I'll say it again.

So the bad-essence passages did catch me, but mostly through that question, not through the explaining around it. "It's convenient to ascribe the worst behavior to some kind of "bad essence."" and what follows read a bit like you heading off a reader who hadn't spoken yet. The question did the work. The paragraphs after it were saying what the question had already done.

**The worst thing.** "Could they have done it with kindness in their eyes?" I want to push on this one because I'm a Friend. Quakers ran Indian boarding schools in the last century and the one before. The people who cut those children's hair thought they were being kind. My first answer was: yes, they did it with kindness in their eyes. Then I thought about what they were looking at. They weren't looking at the child. They were looking at an idea of what the child ought to be, and at a verdict on what the child was. So my second answer was no, and I came by it honestly, but it took me a while and I'd like you to know a careful reader might stop there and not come back.

**"The same thing."** By the time you wrote "Part of me doesn't want to believe that my enemy and I answer to the same thing," I already knew what I took it to be. That of God in every one, the Inner Light, what Ruth was serving in the parking lot. "I treat it like an advisor offering an opinion, which I can weigh against other practical concerns and set aside if the price is right." That caught me hard. It is exactly how I weeded that book. "I'm just another person who knew what was right, decided it was negotiable, and then looked for someone worse than me to prove I wasn't so bad after all." Dale Hutchins has been my someone worse for twenty years.

**Common knowledge.** I followed it, mostly. The hall of mirrors matched Tom, exactly: we both know, it can't be named without sounding crazy, and to his wife it's all deniable. From his look to "Contempt only works because we already know we're the same kind of thing": I followed it. It felt like the opening claim come back, earned this time. The hypocrisy step I followed too. "Neither of us had to be told what my look meant, or what turning away is." That's true of me and the man in the vest. He knew. He called me a church lady because he knew.

"Try it now: imagine the same act without turning away." I tried, with the withdrawal slip, picturing the two girls' names in my own hand as I wrote "poor condition." I couldn't have done it. Then "Does it surprise you that a stranger could count on that about you?" That one I didn't like. The first question left me free. The second told me how to feel about my answer. It was the one place in that section where I felt managed.

What surprised me: "Not your pride: an insult to your pride is easier to shrug off." Then "Recall the last time someone's contempt really got under your skin." That was Tom again, a different day: "Mom, you don't care about people, you care about looking like you care." It didn't reach my pride. It reached the place that knows I count, and that I'd have to turn away from to hurt someone. And then "One says *I know you're in there.* The other says *there's nobody in there*, and they both say it to the somebody who is." I put the pencil down. I had never thought of love and contempt as addressed to the same place, and I've been sitting with dying people for nine years. It is true at the hospice. You speak to whoever is still in there, even when they can't answer.

"Think of the last time you sent that look yourself." Tom, again, on the phone in September, a sigh. "Did it feel like justice, just a little sweeter than justice needs to be?" Yes.

"I've laid this out as an argument, but that isn't how I came to it." I believed you, mostly because of the paragraph before it about the horror. That paragraph doesn't sound like a man arguing. It sounds like a man who saw something.

**Evil.** I flinched a little at "be *nice* to evil" until I saw it was the objection talking. "That's what I'd normally call "evil"—cruelty for its own sake" I accepted. "It's why I don't think "pure evil" is a different kind of thing, but the same thing at full strength." I accept that as a Friend. I'm not sure I'd accept it if I'd been one of those boarding-school children, and I don't think it's mine to accept on their behalf.

**From "my allies are the entire rest of society" to the end of Common knowledge.** It followed. The comment section after a murder on the news, yes, I've read those and felt the warmth of the crowd. "It's possible for the fierceness to stay while the poison goes. What's left is fierce kindness." I already knew this, from Ruth, but I'd never had words for it, and now I do. The saying about integrity: "But someone always is: me." I've sat in silent meeting for forty years, and that is what the silence is for. It landed. It's also one of three places where the chapter seemed to end and didn't.

**The places it seemed to end.** "But someone always is: me." Then "If it does end up reminding them, I can't take credit for it." Then "Whether that number ever goes to zero is something you can discover for yourself." Each of those is a good last line. By the third I was tired.

**The spread.** The kid who watches. "the next stranger he won't give the benefit of the doubt, the kid who watches him do it." I thought of the boy bagging groceries watching me and the man in the vest. What did he learn from the church lady? Probably the same thing he learned from the man.

Then "I send it to people I love." I wasn't ready for that paragraph. "The sigh that tells them what I really think of them, dressed up to make me seem like the reasonable one." That's the September phone call with Tom, word for word. That paragraph is the one I'll remember. It's buried late in a long chapter, though, and I nearly missed it from fatigue.

"Sometimes it looks like me writing this book *at* people, instead of *for* them." I appreciated that. I had been watching for it all along, and you named it before I could.

**The last exercise.** I did it. Not with a stranger in my mind but with the bagger at Kroger, whose name tag says DeShawn. I stayed with his face until it became a place someone was looking out of. That took longer than I'd have guessed. "There's someone here." Yes. Wishing him harm wouldn't go. Then I put the man in the vest there, and the story was there before his face, just as you said: *the kind of man who*. And then I put Tom there, which you didn't ask me to, and the story came before his face so fast I didn't see it come. "For me the story is there before the face is. That's the turning away, live." It was live. It gathered the chapter up for me. It was not one ending too many. It was the right ending and the three before it were the ones too many.

The line about "the hood, the number instead of the name, the word for vermin" was strong and also felt like a reach toward a scale the exercise didn't need. I was at a grocery store and it took me to a death camp. I'd have stayed at the grocery store.

**Length, and what's said twice.** It is long. If I had to cut a fifth: the second half of the Corruption, after "My stories kept changing; my conclusion never did," which drags; some of Whose intention, especially the paragraph on calling it unconscious; the advisor passage is too good to cut but the paragraph right after it, "So part of me already knows the difference," says what was just said; and "Most contempt is quiet," which is the cold version again.

Said twice: "Nobody ever looked honestly at something in themselves while being told they were worthless for having it" near the start, and "If seeing this makes you want to hide, notice that's the same verdict pointed inward" near the end. They're the same thought; the first is better. The phrase "Part of me knows" and its cousins appear so often that by the end I stopped hearing them. "I've found" too. And "we answer to the same thing" gets said in bold, and then a whole section opens on it again.

**Words.** "Limbo" is a Catholic word and I kept hearing purgatory in it, which isn't quite your meaning. "Shit" and "dammit" in the Encounter I didn't mind; they're the narrated mind. "crucially" in the same place didn't bother me either, it was the mind talking. "mash" bothered me. "rot" in "by making you rot for yours" I found strong and fair.

Where I felt free: "Or maybe not." "If not, I have no argument to offer here." The buttons, where you told me yours and left me with mine. "Whether that number ever goes to zero is something you can discover for yourself." Where I felt pushed: "If you can find even one memory where it's plainly true of you, that's the one to look at." "Does it surprise you that a stranger could count on that about you?" And a little, the "I've discovered I do, and I don't think it's a coincidence" after the gaze question. The question had already done it; you didn't need to tell me what you'd discovered.

**Last.** Was any of it obvious? Some of it I already knew, as a Friend: that of God in everyone, the fierce eldering that doesn't condemn. What was new is that my contempt is addressed to the very thing I say I honor in people. I have told myself for two years that the distance from Tom is his doing. This week I'm going to call him and not sigh. I don't know what I'll say. I'll try to look for the person looking out instead of the story.

Would I pass this to a friend? Yes, to Ruth's daughter Ellen, who is in our meeting and stopped speaking to her brother over the same election. Not to my sister, who would feel cornered by the gaze section and put it down. I'd pass it to Ellen with a note: do the buttons honestly.

With thanks, and some discomfort, which I suppose is the point,

Martha Ellison
Bloomington

### Stage 2

Dear Aditya,

I read the second file on First Day, after meeting, a few days after the first, and I went more slowly than I expected to, because most of it is the same and I kept having to check whether a sentence was new or whether I was only seeing it fresh. Here is what the comparison showed me.

**Which did more, and which treated me better.** They did nearly the same thing to me. Tom, Ruth, the man in the vest and the withdrawal slip all came back on cue, and the last exercise did the same work. But the second file treated me better, and in one place it did a little more.

It treated me better because the sentence I felt pushed by is gone. In the chapter, right after "I fear I might be one of them," you wrote "If you can find even one memory where it's plainly true of you, that's the one to look at." The second file ends that paragraph at "Every enemy this part of myself helps me make gives that fear somewhere to point other than at me." and stops. I still refused the claim about fear, for the same reason (my mother, and the women who never learned her last name). But this time I refused it and was left alone, and being left alone, I came back to it on my own, the way I did two days after the chapter. The chapter got me there too, but it had a hand on my back. The second file didn't.

It also stops sooner in two places. The chapter has "If it does end up reminding them, I can't take credit for it." after "especially when I've been given every reason to abandon it." The second file ends that section on "every reason to abandon it," and that is the better last line. The extra sentence in the chapter was one of the false endings I complained about.

**What the second file has that the chapter should recover.**

First, the question. In the second file, under "No one watching":

"Can you remember a time when you corrected someone in a nasty way, but then later, with more years of wisdom, took an entirely different approach to the same problem? One that worked better?"

The chapter never asked me to stand on the corrector's side with my own past and compare. I did it. My first year at Fairview Elementary, 1979, a boy named Kevin Doyle tore pages out of *Where the Red Fern Grows*. I held the book up in front of his whole class and said, "This is what Kevin thinks of books." He never came back to the library willingly. Fifteen years later a girl wrote all over the margins of a library copy of *Hatchet*. I sat down with her and a roll of book tape and asked what she'd been trying to say in the margins. She'd been arguing with the boy in the story. We mended it together and she became a shelver the next year.

So it worked, and it gave me a case of my own for "fierce kindness" from the giving end, which the chapter only gave me from the receiving end (Ruth). That matters. It's one thing to have been corrected well and another to know you once did it badly and later did it better.

But I have to tell you what else happened. When I asked myself whether the second time was *fierce*, I'm not sure it was. It may have been gentle. I was forty-five and tired and fond of that girl. The Integrity section says "Not more gently." The question as written, "One that worked better?", invites exactly the gentler memory, and lets a reader like me count softness as the cure. I'd keep the question, but I noticed that it pulled me toward the thing your Integrity section warns against.

Second, the placement of the integrity saying. In the chapter, "They say integrity is what we do when no one is watching" closes Common knowledge and pretends to be the end of the chapter. In the second file it has its own short section, "No one watching," before Common knowledge, and it doesn't pose as an ending. "But someone always is: me." landed just as hard both times. In the second file it didn't make me think I was finished when I wasn't. Common knowledge in the second file ends on "And I think goodness spreads the same way—not through the visible acts, but what we all know they carry, whether or not we can prove it." That's a quieter close, and it didn't trick me.

I'll say the move has a cost. The second file goes from "When I feel a fundamental difference between me and my worst enemy, I ask which part of me needs there to be one." straight to "Contempt can sometimes stop people's misbehavior." That was a jump; I felt the gear change. In the chapter that material came after "I get to pass off my contempt as righteous anger," where it followed naturally. So the second file has the better place for the saying, and the chapter has the better place for "Contempt can sometimes stop."

Third, in the second file "Fighting evil is the place where my own darkness is most likely to come out" runs straight on into "I might fool people watching from afar." That joins two thoughts that belong together. In the chapter, two paragraphs about contempt stopping misbehavior sat between them and I lost the thread.

**What the chapter was right to leave out or change.**

"my allies are the entire rest of society." The chapter's lead-in is "when someone accused of a heinous crime is on the news, and the comment section fills up." The second file has "like when my target has been accused of a heinous crime." The chapter is right. I had read those comment sections. "The comment section fills up" put me in front of my own screen. "My target" put me back in your argument.

"I've laid this out as an argument, but that isn't how I came to it. I found it the same way you found that the knowledge of your own wrongdoing didn't feel like new information" (the chapter) against "I don't mean any of this as an inference. I mean it in the same way you found that the knowledge of your own wrongdoing didn't feel like new information" (the second file). The chapter's is better. "I found it" is a man telling me what happened to him, and I believed him. "I don't mean any of this as an inference" sounds like a man correcting a referee. I'd have believed him less.

Near the end of the spread, the chapter has "that only happens as I increasingly see mine" and the second file has "that only happens as I increasingly contact mine." "See" is right. "Contact" made me stop and wonder what you meant, which "see" did not.

The chapter has a sentence the second file lacks, in "Most contempt is quiet": "It isn't always moral, but it's always a sense of being more deserving." I didn't mark it the first time, but reading the second file I missed it. It's the sentence that reaches my contempt for the man in the vest, which I'd filed as moral. *More deserving* is closer to the truth of it than I'd like. Keep it.

The chapter also has "So part of me already knows the difference between the "good" I've invented to excuse myself and the kind we all recognize. I just tell myself I don't." The second file goes without it. I said in my first letter it repeated what came just before, and reading the second file I didn't miss it. The second file was right to leave it out.

**Order and length.** The second file is a little shorter and I felt it. Not much, but the ending came when I expected it. What the comparison showed me is that the chapter's length problem was mostly false endings, and the second file has fewer of them: no "I can't take credit," and no integrity saying at the close of Common knowledge. "Whether that number ever goes to zero is something you can discover for yourself." is still there in both, and it still sounds like a last line. In both, the passage right after the dehumanizing-gaze question still explains what the question already did, and the second half of The Corruption still drags. The comparison didn't change my mind on any of that.

**If I could pass only one.** The second file, to Ellen Coffin, who stopped speaking to her brother over the same election that I let come between me and Tom. I'd pass the second file because it asks her to remember a time she corrected someone badly and then better, which she has (she taught high-school chemistry for thirty years), and because it doesn't put a hand on her back. I'd change three things in it before I handed it over, if I were allowed: bring back the comment section, bring back "I found it," and bring back "more deserving." Then I'd write in the margin next to the corrected-badly question: *was the better way fiercer, or only softer?* She'd know what I meant. Her mother did it to me in a parking lot.

I did call Tom. I didn't sigh. We talked about his daughter's soccer for twenty minutes and nothing else, and I let it be nothing else. That's as far as I've got.

Yours,

Martha Ellison
Bloomington

## Dev (44, emergency physician; raised Hindu, lapsed Vipassana)

### Stage 1

Aditya,

I read it after a run of four nights, which is probably the worst condition to read it in and also the most honest one. I'll go in order. I think in differentials, so when something fits everything I get suspicious, and you'll see that come up. I'll try to say where it came up because the thing was wrong and where it came up because I was dodging.

**The memory at the start.**

You asked for one and I didn't have to look. Four years ago, a woman named Tamika, twenty-six, sickle cell, came in at four in the morning in crisis.
