## The protocol the readers were given

You are reading as a person, not reviewing as a model. The point of this exercise is to catch what a real reader would trip on. Readers like you have, in the past, sailed past obvious jumps in wording and logic, because they read fast and charitably and filled the gaps themselves. Don't.

### The rule

Read one file at a time, in order, with the Read tool, **at most 30 lines per call** (use offset and limit). Each paragraph is one line, so that's about a dozen paragraphs. Never read ahead. Never read a whole file in one call.

After every chunk, **before you read the next one**, append an entry to your log file with Bash (`cat >> LOGFILE <<'EOF2'` ... `EOF2`). Your log is your memory of the book: what you knew, believed and expected at each point. Each entry:

- **where**: file and line range.
- **state**: one or two sentences: what you now think the author is claiming, and what you think he's building toward.
- **prediction check**: what your last entry predicted, and whether this chunk did it.
- **snags**: every place you stopped, each with the words quoted exactly. A clean chunk gets "none". The kinds:
  - a jump: a "so", "that's why", "which means" that doesn't follow; a step left out; a conclusion before its evidence;
  - a word or idea used before it was set up, or used in a new sense without saying so;
  - a sentence you had to read twice, or still don't understand;
  - a reference back ("as I said", "the check from Chapter 1", "that") to something you can't find or don't remember;
  - a claim you didn't buy, and why;
  - anything that sounded written by a machine or a committee rather than a person: stock phrases, a tidy "not X, but Y" or "It isn't A. It's B." rhythm, announcing what's coming, abstraction where a person and an act should be, words like "crucially" or "fundamentally";
  - being handled, or told what you'd find or feel before you looked;
  - repetition: something already said (say where you first met it).
- **energy**: 1 to 5, and why if it moved.
- **next**: one line predicting what comes next.

### Two rules about snags

1. **Don't fill gaps for the author.** If a sentence only works because you can guess what he must have meant, that's a snag: quote it and say what you guessed.
2. **Don't invent snags to look thorough.** Every snag needs a quote. A clean chunk is a finding too.

### The exercises

When the book asks you to remember something or try something, do it, with a real case from your own life, and write in your log what came up, including when nothing did or when it felt rigged.

### Letters

At the end of each version (or each stage you're told about), write a letter to the friend who gave you the manuscript, to your letters file, **before opening the next stage**. Say what happened in you, not a review. Don't be polite at the expense of being true. Don't revise an earlier letter after reading further; add to the next one instead.

Warn yourself in advance: the opening of the book is the heaviest part, and relief comes later.

## Letters from Mara Lindqvist

### Letter 1 (after the preface and P)

Dear Anne,

You asked what it did to me, not what I thought of it, so I'll try to keep to that, though you know I can't read a sentence without marking it.

The preface I'll give a paragraph. It's earnest and it makes promises. "The book earns it" is a sentence I'd strike in any workshop, because a book either earns something or it doesn't, and announcing it is a loan against the future. But the second sentence, "It is not one that I can claim to be fully living myself," bought my patience for a long while. A man who starts by saying he doesn't live it is a man I'll listen to. I'm less fond of "flawlessly confident" (confidence isn't performed flawlessly; it's just had) and of the epigraph's being better written than anything near it.

What happened. The first pages are questions, and the first question went straight to a letter I wrote four years ago for a former student who had called my workshop a hazing ritual in print. I wrote her a positive letter. I chose "solid" and "conscientious" and "dependable" with a jeweler's care, knowing exactly what those words do to an application. The book asked whether the knowledge that it was wrong felt like new information when I finally admitted it, or like something I had always known and hadn't let myself know. It felt like a coat that had been lying on a chair in my own study for four years. I'd been walking past it. That opening worked on me, and it worked because it asked and then got out of the way.

Then it lost me for a stretch, and you should know why, because it's not the author's fault exactly. The contempt the first chapter dramatizes is hot: a man staring down someone who was cruel, eyes doing the damage, a gaze, a tone. That isn't my kind. I don't stare at Erik. I stopped looking at him years ago. I make dry jokes about him at dinner and everybody laughs, Erik included. For forty pages I kept thinking, this is a good book about somebody else. Then, almost at the end of the chapter, one paragraph: "I just write the person off: a quiet filing-away, a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them. It's the same verdict, delivered by withdrawal, and it's meant to be felt: by them, or by whoever's watching." *Or by whoever's watching.* You know what my daughter said to me at Christmas. I read that clause and put the pages face down on the table.

The sentence I'll carry out of the room is this one: "Love and contempt are addressed to the same place. One says *I know you're in there.* The other says *there's nobody in there*, and they both say it to the somebody who is." He sets it in bold, which it doesn't need; it would hit harder in roman. Right after it comes a line I don't think he knows the weight of: "A child can't refuse it; he believes it, and carries it for years." He goes on to the next thought. I couldn't.

But the recognition that actually changed something came in Chapter 4, which is short, and it's the chapter I would hand you if you only read one. It's about contempt for people who haven't harmed anyone, the stupid and the credulous and "the relative who believes something ridiculous," where the cover is better because you tell yourself you're only being accurate. And then the "shadow space": two people tearing into someone absent, each a little bolder, and "a warmth between them that had nothing to do with the person they were discussing." That's Karin and me on the phone every other Sunday, starting with "I'm worried about Erik." It's the only time my sister and I are close. And it's Christmas, where the shadow space has the target sitting in it, laughing, and a nineteen-year-old at the end of the table learning what happens to people who disappoint Mom. The book asked, "What would have happened if someone had said, quietly, *I don't think that's fair*?" Nobody ever has. My daughter didn't. She drew her own conclusion instead.

Two more things went in. The question in Chapter 5, which he calls the daily form of an earlier one: "if the harm simply stopped tomorrow, with nobody punished and nobody exposed, would you feel relief, or robbed?" I'd feel both. I'd be robbed of the jokes, and of being the one who saw through him. Beside it: "something in you would be disappointed if they got better." Yes. If Erik went back to teaching high school biology I would have to look at the boy who built model rockets with me in our father's garage, and I don't know how. And the examples of "the extra" in the same chapter, which are the most useful thing in the book for a person who works in sentences: *You lied to me* against *Of course you lied to me.* "Each time the difference is one word or one pause, and each time you know, on the way out, which one you sent." My version is "Erik is selling a new supplement" against "Erik has discovered a new toxin." I always know which one I sent. Always. And I always send the second, because it gets the laugh.

The other thing that stays, oddly, is in the Epilogue, the part he says you can only hear, not check: the words he heard from the people who'd decided he was the bad one. "You will be humanity's sacrifice. The dumping ground for our collective shadow. I'm sorry to have to do this to you, but it's the only way to keep our hands clean. Good luck." The "Good luck" is what got me. Erik is the family's dumping ground. All of us keep in him what we've disowned in ourselves: the credulity, the salesmanship, the wanting to be adored. He knows it. There's nothing he can do. That's in the least checkable chapter, and it's the truest thing I read about my brother.

Where I skimmed: the Buddhist terms. *Marigpa*, Dzogchen, Mahamudra, lojong, the Mahayana, "self-cherishing," the Aspiration of Samantabhadra. I know these only from the posters at my yoga studio, and he drops them without stopping. The worst is "if you practice in a tradition that calls things empty, notice that this has exactly the kind of existence you grant everything else." I don't, and I didn't follow. That sentence is written to somebody else. So is "lojong's *drive all blames into one*," which is never explained. And I skimmed the institution-and-country paragraphs of The Picture I Use, apart from one line I loved, which I'll come to.

Where I was lost. "What I've been hiding isn't that I *could* do what the 'bad people' do. It's that I'm *already doing it*, by using them as a place to hide the fact that I am." I read it three times. "The fact that I am" what? "And how better to make him carry it than to show him that I was doing it, while hiding it from myself?" I had to build the logic myself. "That's why evil benefits from being seen as a presence, in either direction, and why calling it a parasite isn't a comfort. It's what makes looking possible." I still don't know how the last sentence follows from the one before. "It isn't following rules; it is what sees myself in others" is simply ungrammatical. And Chapter 2's paragraph about the knowledge being "common," where he gives "common" a technical meaning mid-sentence in a chapter called "Common Ground," lost me on the first pass.

Handled, or told what I'd find. Not often, to his credit; mostly he asks. But: "We know the difference; at a deeper level it's obvious." "You recognized it instantly." "We all know the distinction. We just don't trust that we know it." "I think it was in your eyes too, the last time you held your enemy in contempt." "That isn't said to frighten you." "That isn't cause for alarm." "(You may be wondering about the people who seem to be born this way...)" I wasn't wondering, yet. And in Chapter 4 the check about which scenes gave you contempt and which made you wince comes with its answer already attached: "Those are the shadows you can judge safely... The ones that made you wince are closer to home." It happened to be right about me. That's not the same as letting me find it.

Then the machine sentences, or the sentences written by nobody, which I'm afraid there are a lot of. The worst offender is a rhythm, not a phrase: "It feels like accountability. It isn't. It's a verdict." "It isn't following rules; it is..." "That's not an accident; it's part of the trap." "It isn't the same thing at all. It's the parasite." "It isn't a fact about them... It's a stance." "It isn't the darkness subjugated. It's the darkness looked at." I stopped counting at around twenty. A few of them are earned ("I wasn't *exposing* his darkness, like I told myself. I was creating more of it"), but in this density it reads as a habit, and the habit sounds like software. Then the committee phrases: "where 'othering' has reached its zenith, and feels maximally righteous"; "a uniquely insidious expression of evil"; "the great lie we tell ourselves"; "feeling forsaken at a level we rarely cognize"; "the kind of world most of us long for." The greeting-card ones: "I think we're all trying to find our way home"; "It was always there, just patiently waiting to be remembered"; "the light in me is reminding the light in him that it has a partner in the fight." And in the Epilogue, the capitals: "There was only a pure singularity: blinding white Light; infinite Love. It was self-evidently the Source of creation, and I was That." Then, a paragraph later, "It's Home." I believe he saw something. But that's the language of every retreat brochure in Minnesota, and I closed the manuscript.

Something you couldn't know, and he couldn't either. My brother's best-selling product is a parasite cleanse. His copy is about the invisible thing living inside you that makes you tired and wrong. When this book said "For all these reasons, I started calling it a parasite," I had a genuinely ugly moment of: am I being sold a cleanse? He does warn against picturing a creature, and his own gloss is better than the word: "a groove in attention so old that its shape looks like intent." But then come "a low-grade infection," "a mind-virus," and "the way you can only choose junk food once you've forgotten real food," and the book's whole vocabulary for evil turns out to be my brother's vocabulary. A reader who has spent time near the wellness world will hear it. And when the Epilogue reaches for *A Course in Miracles*, which I know only as what Erik quotes in his captions, and says that what's buried in us "manifests" in the physical world, I could feel the floor go soft. He puts in a careful parenthesis that nobody's illness is a sign of what they did. That he needs it tells me where the idea lives.

There's also a joke at my expense he didn't intend: in the list of names the traditions gave the adversary, the first is "Mara." I had to look it up. I laughed, and then for a page every "it" was partly me.

Lines I'd keep if I were his editor, and fight for: "There's no bottom to what can be done to someone who doesn't count." "Fierce integrity and contempt can be made to look alike from the outside, so that when I look back later, even I won't be sure which one I did." "When I'm sure someone was fully aware of what they were doing, I've made them a villain. When I'm sure they had no idea, I've made them a machine." "The torturer's turn is the one I make, smaller, to feel okay cheating on a test." "I've found them guilty of being what they are." "Pity, which passes for compassion and is contempt with a soft face." "Two rooms differ in everything the light shows; the dark in one is the same as the dark in the other." And, about institutions: "The one who decides doesn't look, the one who looks doesn't decide, and the harm happens in the gap between them." That's my university's tenure committee in a sentence.

What I'll do differently this week. Two things, small. I'm not going to make the Erik joke on Sunday when Karin calls. I'll see if I can say "Erik is selling a new supplement" and stop there, and notice what it costs. And I'm going to write to my daughter. I don't know what I'll say yet. Something closer to "you've seen me do something at the table that I'm ashamed of, and it was never about whether people disappoint me" than to an apology she'd have to manage. The book's line was "often the repair is no more than letting them see that you see them now." I'll try that.

Was any of it obvious? Yes, a good deal. That contempt is projection, that we put our disowned selves in other people: that's Jung, and eight years of therapy have said it to me in four different voices. Evil as an absence rather than a thing is Augustine and Weil. "Darkness cannot drive out darkness" is King, and he says so. Self-contempt making the wrong dig in is something every decent therapist knows. I thought "I already knew this" more than once.

But some things I knew became newly consequential, and that's the part that matters. I knew evil was a privation; I'd never been made to feel it as something that "tracks, it aims, it fights to stay hidden," and that it uses my accurate perception to recruit me. I'm right about Erik's products. The book let me keep that ("you may well be on the side that's right") and then said the contempt is "a tug to be recruited into the thing you're looking at, and it uses your accurate perception to do it." My being right is the bait. I knew shadow projection; I didn't know that "you can only be good *relative* to someone worse," and so I need Erik not to get better. And I knew a joke can carry cruelty. I hadn't seen that a dinner table where everyone laughs is a room that teaches, and that the person learning was never the target.

It's too long and it repeats itself (the "innocent at the root and fully responsible" idea comes round at least three times, and the caveat for people who were hurt is restated in three chapters). Its prose is at its best when a person is talking to himself, like "he's **bad**, dammit," and "*Oh, boo hoo, he was abused as a child*," and at its worst when it explains. The opening chapter is the heaviest thing I've read in a year, and it still has only one scene in it, the teacher who caught him stealing, three sentences long. I want that room. I want to know what he stole.

But it did something to me, and I'm going to have to write a letter to my daughter, so there.

Love,
Mara

### Letter 2 (after Q)

Dear Anne,

I've read the second version. You warned me most of the sentences were the same, and they are; something like nine in ten of them. What's different is the order, a handful of new paragraphs, and some chapter titles. It's a strange experience, reading a book you've just read in a different order. It's like walking through your own house after someone has moved the furniture in the night. You keep reaching for a light switch that's now on the other wall.

What happened in me this time was quieter, which is partly the order and partly that I'd already been through it once. The first chapter is the same up to the man's monologue and the "same place" passage, and those hit me again, nearly as hard. But then Q does something I'd wished for in my first letter: it gives my kind of contempt its own heading, "The Quiet Kind," right after the hot monologue, before any of the metaphysics. "A politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them... meant to be felt: by them, or by whoever's watching." In P I nearly missed that paragraph; it was buried in a section about social media, after five pages on the nature of evil. In Q it stands at the top of its own section, as the other half of the man's monologue, and I didn't spend forty pages thinking the book was about somebody else. If I'd read Q first I think I'd have come to the dinner table a chapter sooner.

The second chapter is where Q is a better book. In P, the second chapter opens with an instruction (look into a stranger's face) and arrives at a definition of evil at the end. In Q it opens with a question: "So what is evil, if it isn't a kind of person?" Then come cases I'd already use the word for (a politician lying a country into a war, executives selling a drug they know is killing people) and two that rule things out: "A hurricane can kill thousands, and nobody calls it evil," and murder against accident. Then the line-cutting and the birthday, which in P came after the definition as illustrations, come here as evidence, before it. I liked being asked to supply the evidence before he named the thing. Then: "Cheating and contempt are one move from two ends: *this doesn't apply to me*, and *you're not really one of us*." That sentence put my letter for my former student and my jokes about Erik in one frame, and I hadn't had that in P. Then "Not every advantage I take is an exception. I can take the last seat on the train with the man behind me fully in mind... I can't cut in front of him in line that way. Why not? Start with a face." So when the face exercise comes I need it. In P it was a request from nowhere.

The new lines I'd keep, from that chapter and after it: "Hunger doesn't have to look away from the bread." That's the shortest argument in either version for why care is where we start, and it's lovely. "It's an absence, not of the faculties, which are all still at work, but of the facing." And in the list of what evil looks like — "The padded expense, the lie to the insurer, the joke that makes someone smaller, the slur, the order to clear a village" — there I was, third item. It didn't make a fuss of me. It just put me in the line, which was right. Later, a new paragraph: "The message that denies the sameness is made of the sameness, and nothing else could receive it." That's the clearest thing either version says about why contempt needs what it denies.

Chapter 3 in Q ("How Far It Reaches") asked me a question P never did: "When the world gets darker, what happens in me? When I read about a politician's lie or a company's scam, do I feel more justified in my contempt, or less? More free to cut my own small corners, or less?" More justified, every time. The month that piece about supplement fraud ran with Erik's company in the sidebar, I passed a thesis I didn't believe in because I didn't want the fight. I don't know that those two things are connected. But I'd never have put them side by side without that question. Q also gave me a sentence I couldn't parse in P and made it parse by moving it: "What I've been hiding isn't that I *could* do what the 'bad people' do. It's that I'm *already doing it*, by using them as a place to hide the fact that I am." In P it came out of nowhere. In Q it follows "Corrupt leaders meet a hidden need in all of us... *At least I'm not like them*," and suddenly I could see it: the bad people are where I park my own wrongs. And the loop paragraph after it is new and plain: "The need makes the darkness, and the darkness feeds the need."

Two more placements are better in Q. The villain/machine line ("When I'm sure someone was fully aware of what they were doing, I've made them a villain. When I'm sure they had no idea, I've made them a machine") now sits in a chapter called "The Worst People," with the Clancy case, which is its proper home; in P it interrupted the first chapter's way out. And the exercise for the far end now comes right after the chapter it serves, instead of two sections away.

Now what went wrong, because a good deal did, and some of it a first-time reader would trip on harder than I did.

The terms come before their introductions. "Parasite" appears in Q's third chapter, five or six times, as if we'd agreed on it ("My parasite is constantly sensing who it's safe to treat this way"; "That's the parasite, watching"; "all their parasites come out and play"), and only in Chapter 4 does the author build up to it and say "For all these reasons, I started calling it a parasite." In P that sentence was a naming; in Q it's a footnote to a word I've been using for a chapter. Same with "the background hum," used in Chapter 2 ("It doesn't take monsters. It takes the background hum") and only explained in Chapter 3, and "the low-grade infection," used ten lines before "Call it a low-grade infection." I only knew what they meant because I'd read P. Worse, Chapter 3 ends on a beautiful question, "Something in this loop behaves as if it wants the world darker, in me and in everyone at once. What is it?", and the answer has already been handed to me by name. The reveal is spent before it's asked.

One reference is broken: "The license from Chapter 1 comes at the scale of a team too." The license was set up in Chapter 4, a few pages earlier, not Chapter 1. P had it right.

The picture he holds without proof, the Devil and the conspiracy and the one coordinated thing, comes in the middle of Chapter 4 in Q, under the same italic label as in P: "Up to here, almost everything came with a check you could run. What follows doesn't." In P I trusted that label; I liked being told the tier of what I was reading. In Q the label is on the wrong shelf. Some of what P kept under it has already walked into the body of the book unlabeled: Chapter 2 says "my contempt for a neighbor makes an atrocity on another continent a little more likely, by no line I could ever trace," and Chapter 3 says "Corrupt leaders meet a hidden need in all of us." And nothing marks where the unchecked part ends; the next chapter just starts again with "Here's a check that needs no metaphysics," right after a page about the Devil and the Cree word *wetiko*. Then there's an order problem with the Devil itself. In Chapter 4 the darkness acts "like a single thing with a single, monstrous aim." In Chapter 5, a new paragraph: "So nothing in the encounter belonged to a separate thing called the darkness." Then in the Epilogue: "That's why I see one Devil and not many." I know he means all three to fit (no being of its own, but behaves like one). In P the plain claim came first and the Devil was laid over it as a picture. In Q the Devil comes first, gets walked back, and then comes back. It reads as a man changing his mind twice.

There's some visible shuffling. Chapter 5 opens "What is all of this turned away from? Start with why anyone turns at all," and gives the answer. Chapter 6 opens "But even after we recognize it, the question remains: why do they turn away in the first place?" as if the question were new. There are now two loops (Chapter 3's need-and-darkness loop and Chapter 5's suspicion-and-home loop) and I couldn't tell if they were one loop or two. Chapter 5 says "Now widen it. Put the face of someone you love there. Then the stranger again," and "there" and "again" point back three chapters to the face exercise; the same "Now widen it" already opened a paragraph in Chapter 2 about something else. In Chapter 1, "But it did show me a new way to relate to others" is followed immediately by a new heading, and the new way never comes. And "*That wasn't the real me* can be used as an excuse, but I also think it's true," which bothered me in P, bothers me more in Q, because the passage that justifies it ("What you hid it from is what you call 'I' when nothing is being hidden") has moved four chapters later.

The biggest cost, for me, is where the practical chapter sits. In P, the chapter that ended at my dinner table (shadow spaces, contempt for the credulous) was followed directly by the chapter that gave me something to do: relief or robbed, the extra, catching it faster. Recognition, then a tool. That adjacency is why I put the manuscript down and then did something. In Q the dinner table is Chapter 3 and the tools are Chapter 7, with the cosmology, the worst people and the far-end exercise in between. By the time Q handed me "*You lied to me*" against "*Of course you lied to me*," my own case had been offstage for a hundred pages. And the author's line "It's the thing I most wanted this book to deliver," which in P came near the end like a culmination, comes in the middle of Q's Chapter 4 of 7, and made me wonder what the last three chapters were for.

On the questions you asked last time. The recognition I'll carry out of Q that I didn't have from P: "Cheating and contempt are one move from two ends." And the turn-around question, about whether a darker world makes me freer to cut my own corners. Where I skimmed: the same Buddhist terms, and the second reading of the Picture, which in Q sat in the middle and felt like a sag. Where I was lost: in Q, honestly, less than in P in the argument and more in the bookkeeping; the common-knowledge paragraph still loses me ("If that's right, the knowledge is exactly common; if not, it's close enough for the trap to work"); and "one faculty, in two bodies, turned against itself in one of them" — which one? Handled: the same places as before, since they're the same sentences ("I think it was in your eyes too"; "We all know the distinction. We just don't trust that we know it"; "That isn't said to frighten you"). Machine sentences: the same. The isn't/is cadence is identical, and Q's new paragraphs mostly avoid it, which I noticed. Q's new prose is a little plainer than the old.

What I did this week, since you'll ask. Karin called on Sunday. I said "Erik's selling a new supplement" and stopped. There was a silence on the line. She filled it with her own joke. I didn't laugh. That's all; it was small, and it was the hardest thing I did all week. I haven't written to my daughter yet. I have a draft.

Obvious? The same things as before were obvious to me (Jung, Augustine, King). What Q made newly consequential that P didn't: the pairing of cheating and contempt, which I'd have said I understood, and didn't, until I saw my letter and my jokes as one move.

What Q has that P should keep: the opening of Chapter 2 as a question with cases ("So what is evil, if it isn't a kind of person?... A hurricane can kill thousands, and nobody calls it evil"); "Hunger doesn't have to look away from the bread"; "Cheating and contempt are one move from two ends"; the last seat on the train; "the joke that makes someone smaller" in the list; "The Quiet Kind" as a heading straight after the monologue; "Now turn it around. When the world gets darker, what happens in me?"; the loop paragraph; "The message that denies the sameness is made of the sameness"; villain/machine under "The Worst People"; the far-end exercise right after its chapter; chapter endings that ask the next chapter's question.

What P has that Q should keep: the order of terms, which never once made me guess; the Picture quarantined after the practice, with a label that tells the truth and a title that ends it; the face exercise flowing straight into its widening (the loved one, the animal, the tree, what's doing the looking) without a three-chapter gap; recognition followed directly by practice; "and to the kid who watches him do it" in the first chapter, the first time the book brushed my daughter; and the Devil arriving after the plain claim, so it reads as a picture and not as a doctrine walked back.

Love,
Mara

### Letter 3 (comparing P and Q)

Dear Anne,

You'll want a verdict, so here's the short one: P changed me more, and Q thinks better. I'm not sure the author can have both in one book, but I think he could have more of both than either version gives him.

Which went deeper. In the sense of showing me something I didn't have, Q, by a little. Its second chapter does the best thinking in either version: it asks what evil is as a real question, rules out size and death with a hurricane and an accident, and arrives at the exception — "*this doesn't apply to me*" and "*you're not really one of us*" as one move from two ends. Love,
Mara

### Letter 1 (after the preface and Q)

Dear Paul,

You asked what it did to me, and then, because it's me, where it breaks. I'll do the first part first, since that's the part I'd rather skip.

The preface: a man who saw something on mushrooms and promises to check it in his own case and mark what he can't. He promises the book will "earn" the word evil. I wrote that down as a debt and kept the receipt. The Gospel of Philip epigraph is good; "it is powerful because we have not recognized it" is "you're only as sick as your secrets" in older clothes, and that told me what kind of book it would be.

What happened in me. Glenn walked in on page three, uninvited, and stayed. You know the story; you've heard it at dinner, which is the problem. The book got to me three times, and none of them were where I expected.

The first was the monologue in Chapter 1, the author narrating himself being the man who condemns. "I'll have produced the result I needed: proof that my enemy is beyond help, because look how he reacted." That's Marcus. When Marcus came home that summer and said Glenn had been decent to him, I didn't want it to be true, and I called my son naive, and he didn't speak to me for a year. I needed Glenn to stay bad. I've known that in some back room for a while. I'd never said it in words before.

The second was smaller and did more damage. "Try and get a feel for what being met that way passes on, and whether that's likely to stop with them either." I tried, and it went off like a flashbulb. In the lawyer's office on Walnut Street, Glenn said to me, "You were never a businessman, Tom. You're a guy who likes books." Naive, in other words. A year later I said "naive" to my son about Glenn. Glenn's verdict on me became my verdict on Marcus. I've told that story at thirty dinners and never once heard it.

The third was this: "**Love and contempt are addressed to the same place.** One says *I know you're in there.* The other says *there's nobody in there*, and they both say it to the somebody who is." That's the sentence I'll carry out of the room. It put my father-in-law's Sunday-dinner face next to Helen Marsh's, the editor who caught me tidying a quote in '87 and never once called me a liar, and it showed me they were aiming at the same spot in me. Then it showed me where I'd aimed "naive."

And one exercise did real work: put your enemy's face in front of you and try for the same recognition you got with a stranger. "For me the story is there before the face is." I couldn't see Glenn's face. I saw the March 2019 transfer on the bank statement. Same here, exactly, and I'd never have noticed without being asked.

Later on, two more that I'll use. "Seeing clearly what they are capable of is not the verdict." I can know Glenn is capable of draining an account and not say that's all he is. I've been treating those as one thing for six years, and treating anyone who separated them as a fool. And the daily check: if the harm stopped tomorrow, nobody punished, nobody exposed, "would you feel relief, or robbed?" Robbed. Instantly. Glenn's harm stopped six years ago. What I'd feel robbed of is the punishment.

Now where it breaks, since you asked.

The first two chapters are a chain of real findings, each one I could check in my own life: the knowing side never argues; you can't harm someone fully in view without a story; contempt changes its reasons and never its conclusion. Then comes the central claim of Chapter 1, and the step to it is thinner than the steps before. On one side: "Something tracked his reactions and steered around them... It was intentional, but done in limbo." Good; I'll grant it; editors see that pattern in every motivated manuscript. On the other: "This trap wouldn't work unless some part of me recognized our similarity—that he has a better nature to be turned away from." To steer around a man's reactions, I need to model his reactions. I don't need to know he has a better nature. The content of the knowing is bigger than the evidence needs, and it's the opening thesis of the book.

The second gap is in Chapter 2. "If awareness were neutral between care and harm, why would harming someone require turning away from it?" A fair question. Then: "My own direct experience is that care belongs to awareness itself, and that's also the simplest explanation I can find." There's a plainer explanation he doesn't name: we evolved to feel for creatures we see as our own kind, so harming them means suppressing that. It explains the hood and the word for vermin just as well, and it doesn't need "awareness itself." What I noticed, though, is that his best argument survives the objection: "Hunger doesn't have to look away from the bread." Even if care is evolved, it's still the baseline in us, and harm still needs the turn. So the practical book stands on its own feet. The metaphysical book is a separate building, and he keeps walking between them as if they were one. He answers the evolution point honestly, but only in the Epilogue, as a "what if." That's the right tone in the wrong place.

The third gap is in Chapter 4, in the section he honestly marks as having no check. "The darkness has no substance of its own; a turning away has no qualities... Together they act like a single thing with a single, monstrous aim." Two holes aren't one hole because holes have no substance. And in the Epilogue I found out the one-Devil claim doesn't rest on that argument at all: "my mind isn't separate from the world... That's why I see one Devil and not many." So Chapter 4 offered an argument for something whose real ground is a vision. Better to say so there.

Where I was lost: the Buddhist words (marigpa, Dzogchen, Mahamudra, lojong, tonglen, "only conventionally so"). The glosses usually rescued me. "Self-cherishing: the mind that counts itself as the one exception" I got, and it tied back to Chapter 2's "exception" well. And I was lost for a page in Chapter 3, because a "parasite" shows up three times ("My parasite is constantly sensing who it's safe to treat this way") a full chapter before the book says "I started calling it a parasite." He was right in my case, which is why it annoyed me: I can't tell whether I found it or was steered. Again and again a question is asked and then answered for me: "feel for *where* it was trying to reach... This reaches further in, for the *you* that knows you count." Each is fine alone; together they're a tic. A few flat ones: "I'll stop looking at exactly the moment it's crucial to look more closely." "We have projects and agendas that are motivated by the self-serving part of us, and they create apparent good but hollow out the caring in everything they touch." That last one reads like a committee.

What I'll do this week. I'm going to call Marcus on Thursday. Not to talk about Glenn; to let him see that I see him now, which is the book's phrase for the repair and the Big Book's phrase for the ninth step. And I'm going to do the thing I've told a dozen sponsees to do and never done myself: pray for Glenn every day for two weeks, ask for him everything I'd want for me. I noticed while reading that I'd never once done it. I didn't like noticing.

Walt, my sponsor, was the second stranger for six years. So yes, I already knew it.

It's a better book than I expected and a less careful one than it thinks. The first chapter is the real thing. Now I'm told there's a second version.

## The developmental editor (Q only): the vision it read for, and its letters

### The vision (from brief.md, engine.md, CLAUDE.md), in the order the reader should find it

V1. **Limbo.** When I do wrong, part of me knows. Recalled, it isn't new information; I was turned away, not ignorant. The side that knew never argued; the other side supplied reasons. (Reader checks it on their own wrong, first, short.)
V2. **The double standard.** I call my wrongs unconscious and my enemy's conscious, because he has a bad essence and I don't. Contempt is that verdict, "you're a different kind of thing", and it's the same turning away from commonality that any harm needs (lie, cheat, steal, atrocity).

V8. **Evil is the same road, further along.** A difference in condition, not kind. The emotional punchline: we do this because we're afraid we're like them, so we make them worse, so we're part of it. Contempt is the last evil we still praise.
V9. **The message it carries.** Not "goodness isn't real" but "goodness is whatever you decide", so there's no North Star to have left. The part that proves has been handed the job of the part that knows. Good = what the better nature senses toward, and it's shared between any two people beneath their self-deception.
V10. **The cure is remembering, and only in you.** Turn back each time you catch it; you catch it faster with practice; oriented, you give the other nothing to fight and something to recognize. Shame is the same verdict pointed inward.

**The peak (meant):** the preface's question (does what's in my skull matter?) and Chapter 1's (what is this thing?) converge: *it reaches everywhere because of what it is.* Nothing is confined to your skull; every act counts, witnessed or not. Delivered at full volume only at the end, marked as belief, without cornering the skeptic. The "deeper discovery" (we've been making them worse out of fear we're like them) lands late, not in Chapter 1.

**The page test:** the reader supplies the evidence before the name; predicts before being told; "of course, I've always known it, I must have." Tells of cornering: telling the reader what they'll find, "if you're honest," objection-as-mechanism, volume knobs, the book naming itself, presumptive questions, every road funnels.

##### Letter after Chapter 1 (to the friend who gave me the manuscript)
The chapter got under my guard twice: the bet against the first stranger ("I'd bet on it at any odds. But on what evidence?") and the sigh at someone you love. Those I'll remember. The encounter monologue is the best thing in it; I believed him because he made fun of himself. Where it lost me was the middle, "The How," where a lot of large claims arrived with "that's why" in front of them and I stopped checking and started nodding. And then it ended on the answer. I closed the chapter feeling the book had told me what it was: contempt works because we're the same, the good is an orientation, remember it. I wasn't sure what was left to find. The opening two sentences had already told me the trick, which made the middle feel like a proof of something I'd been handed rather than a thing I was finding.

##### Letter after Chapter 2
This chapter is where I started to trust the book's sense of evil: line-cutting and the cutting remark did more than any argument, and "the story is there before the face is" caught me. But it also taught me, earlier than it meant to, that the book will tell me the big things. The background hum and the atrocity on another continent came as sentences, not as something I saw. So when the chapter ended by asking whether my contempt stays in my skull, I'd already been told it doesn't. I'm a little tired of hearing "the turn." I'm curious, though: the claim that evil "tracks, aims, fights to stay hidden" is strange and I want to know what he means by it.

##### Letter after Chapter 3
This is the chapter I'd hand someone. The coworker who doesn't get it the third time, the toddler who can't add, FAFO, Lisa, the driver: I recognized all of it and didn't feel accused, because he was in every one first. The line "contempt is the one we still praise as goodness itself" stayed with me. Two things bothered me. "Parasite" just showed up, possessive and all, and I went back to look for where I'd missed it; I hadn't. And the chapter's biggest moment, "I'm already doing it," came in a sentence I had to read three times. It ends asking what the thing is, but I've been told what it is twice.

##### Letter after Chapter 4
This is where the book got big, and I went with it further than I expected, because the dance between him and me was the encounter I'd already lived through, reread. "My enemy and I are fighting. Our parasites aren't" was the first time the book showed me something I hadn't seen coming. After the handshake, though, it turned into a sermon, a good one, and I stopped being able to tell which lines were the point. He told me which one was ("the thing I most wanted this book to deliver"), and I believed him, and then wondered what the remaining chapters were for. The line about not believing someone who acts with plain integrity, that there must be an angle, caught me harder than the Devil did.

##### Letter after Chapter 5
"The message that denies the sameness is made of the sameness" is the line I'll quote to people. It did what the first page of Chapter 1 tried to do and couldn't, because now I had the encounter, the face, and the dance to check it against. I didn't need the Tibetan word or Augustine at the top; they made me feel I'd wandered into a seminar right when I wanted the answer. And "home" came from nowhere. The chapter ended on kindness being the harshest thing in the room, which I liked, but I don't know what I'm waiting for next. It feels like the book is done and there are still three files.

##### Letter after Chapter 7
The last two pages are good. "Somebody you'll never meet is about to give up on the part of them that knows" made me think of a particular person, which no line in the Devil chapter did. And the very last sentence, "only the light, turned away," closed something I'd been carrying since the first chapter. But I had a feeling of déjà vu at "None of it stays inside you," because I'd heard it in Chapter 2 with the same "can't trace" shrug. Chapter 7 itself is the most useful chapter in the book: "Of course you lied to me" is something I'll catch myself saying this week. I wish the book had told me sooner that "who's to say what's good? You get to decide" was the thing it was most worried about; it arrived as an aside.

##### Letter after the Epilogue
I didn't expect to be moved by the part about someone he'll never meet holding him when he was about to break, and I was. The Epilogue is where the book finally said the thing I think it's been about: that his mind was never separate from the world, so it was never only reaching the man he was contemptuous of. If that had been the hinge of Chapter 7 instead of a line in a vision, I think I'd have closed the book with the "of course" he wants. As it is, I closed it with a lot of strong sentences and a sense that the big reveals had come early and the reason for them late. The best thing in it is still the first stranger, the second stranger, and the sigh at someone you love. I'd read it again. I'd skip parts of Chapter 4.
