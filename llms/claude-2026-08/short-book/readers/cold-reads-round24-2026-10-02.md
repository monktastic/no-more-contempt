Dear Aditya,

You asked for what happened, in order, so this is that. I will try not to grade you. By the end you'll see why that's a joke at my own expense.

**The opening.** Your first sentence is sixty-odd words with three "how" clauses stacked on each other, and I nearly put my pen down before I'd started. Then: "Maybe you'll recognize something of yourself in it. Or maybe not." Those two rescued it. "Or maybe not" was the first sign you weren't going to herd me, and I relaxed a little.

"Contempt is the claim that you're a different kind of thing than me, but for it to land, you must be the same kind of thing." I didn't buy that yet. I didn't understand it yet. I filed it as something to watch for, which is what you asked. (The schoolteacher wants "from me." I lost that battle with Americans in 1990.)

**The memory.** I have one, and I didn't enjoy finding it so quickly. When I learned Sipho had borrowed against Mama's house in Pimville, I telephoned my cousin Nomvula in Johannesburg, "just to ask if she'd known." I told it in a way I knew she would carry to every aunt before Sunday. I knew that while I was dialling. I told myself I was asking a question.

Did it feel like new information when I finally admitted it? No. "Or like something you had always known, but somehow hadn't *let* yourself know?" Yes. Bought, on my own evidence.

"Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" That's a good question, and it left me room. My arguing side had a whole brief: Nomvula has a right to know, it's family property, secrets rot families. The other side didn't argue. It just said, quietly, *you want them to think less of him.* I noticed I could hear that second voice only now, reading you, and I'm not sure I could ever have heard it at the time. But it was there.

"If seeing that makes you want to call yourself names, hold off." I was about to. Thank you.

**Better nature, the cashier, remorse.** "In fact, I *can't*, when I can clearly see myself in them." I wanted to argue. I've been unkind to people I could see perfectly well. But perhaps "clearly" is doing the work, and I let it pass. Tentatively bought.

The cashier was the best check in the chapter, because it worked on me and not just on the argument. The young man at the Publix on Ponce, Devontae on his badge. The smile went at once; it wanted nothing. The sneer wouldn't come. Then, within two seconds, I'd supplied a reason: he calls everyone "sweetie," including me. So yes, the sneer needed a reason, and I discovered I'm a very quick supplier of reasons. That was not comfortable and it was useful.

Remorse for decency: I lent Sipho money in 2003 and regretted it. Regret, not remorse. You drew the line where I would have drawn it. Bought. "Can you imagine it even in the worst person?" I tried with the worst people I've known, and I grew up in Johannesburg under the old government, so I had candidates. I couldn't imagine one of them sick at heart for having been kind. Bought, with a chill.

"It means that under the right circumstances, I'm capable of the worst acts humans have committed." You needn't persuade a South African of my age. I watched church-going neighbours do it. Bought outright.

**The encounter.** The hot version isn't me. I don't confront; I go quiet. So I read it as someone else's mind, and mostly believed it as a mind. I skimmed the "invitation" paragraphs; I've read that rationalising voice in a lot of student fiction and I could predict it. "Ha! Classic projection." rang false: too much the cartoon villain. But this is the best line in the scene, and it's exactly how a mind does it; I have watched mine do it: "He can't prove I'm doing this, can he? No. And crucially, *neither can I*. So I know it's not on purpose."

Then the four sentences after it, and I had to put the pages down. "I just write the person off: a quiet filing-away, a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them." That is me. That is me at Christmas when Sipho walks in and I say, "How lovely that you could come." My daughter Thandi once told me I "grade people," and I was offended for a week. That paragraph is the most important one in the chapter for me, and you've written it as an aside between two sections. I did as you said and read the rest with the cold version in mind. It mostly worked, though the Corruption section is still written for a shouting match.

**The corruption.** "If this is the kind of world I live in—one where people can lie to themselves and get away with it—then why the hell shouldn't I?" I thought of Sipho, and that his not bringing the children might not be sulking. It might be something he learned at my table. Tentatively bought, and it stung.

"So I didn't look—and that *was* my role in causing it" took me two readings and was worth both.

**The two checks.** Is there something satisfying in seeing evil violently punished? Yes. I watched the Truth and Reconciliation hearings from Atlanta, and there were men I wanted hanged, and I sang in church that same week. The pill: I tried Sipho, because he's not my worst enemy but he's the one with weight. I didn't want vengeance. I wanted the pill. Then I watched what I imagined the pill doing, and it was Sipho arriving at my door to tell me I had been right all along. The pill I wanted was not a change of heart. It was a confession addressed to me. So "craves punishment" didn't fit; "craves being proved right" did. It may be the same animal in a cardigan.

What opened me there was "I'll be honest: there is in me." You went first. I listen to people who go first.

"Did it feel like losing something of yourself, or like getting something back?" I've never owned up to Nomvula. I owned up to myself, an hour ago, reading you. It felt like getting something back. A small thing, but not nothing.

**The corrector with integrity.** This is where the chapter worked best. My first department head in DeKalb County, Ruth Abernathy, 1991. She sat at the back of my class while I made a boy reread a passage three times because he'd said "Pharaoh" wrong, and the class laughed. I've never forgotten it, and I never did it again. "*you're better than this*": that is very nearly what she said. Fully bought.

The time I corrected someone badly: my first years, I wrote "Did you read the book?" in red across the top of essays. Later I wrote one question in the margin and nothing else, and more of them read the book. With Thandi I haven't got to the later approach yet.

"It's a little darkness I sprinkle into otherwise-good behavior" — "sprinkle" is too cute for poison. I winced.

**Common knowledge.** I followed the move from hypocrisy to a shared standard. I've taught La Rochefoucauld, that hypocrisy is the homage vice pays to virtue. What you added, and what surprised me, is that the homage is paid inside the sneer itself: the look can only sting because it appeals to the standard it's breaking. I hadn't seen that. "And it only reads *as* a signal (and not a slip) if each knows that the other knows that the other knows." I read that four times and couldn't make the third "knows" do anything.

Then my first outright refusal: "Not your pride: an insult to your pride is easier to shrug off." Maybe what you mean is under pride and I can't tell where one stops and the other starts. But as written, I refused it. I could still follow what came after.

The last time I sent the look: last Thursday. The new director announced a praise chorus with a key change, and I turned to Margaret beside me in the altos and raised my eyebrows. Did I know he'd read it? Yes. Did I mean him to? Yes. Did I give myself an out? I'd have said I was looking at my music. I answered yes to all three of your questions, and that was horrible. "In the opening, were you surprised that I knew you needed limbo to cause harm? If not, why not?" Here I felt pushed. You didn't tell me that in the opening; you asked me. And the question has only one answer it will accept.

"It's hard to describe the excruciating pain I experienced" set off my sentimentality alarm. "Excruciating" is telling me what to feel about your pain, and it's not needed; the sentence before had already done it. But "Maybe I'm wrong, and this isn't how it works in others. But this is how things appear to me." was honest, and I trusted you more for it.

The gaze: in 1986 a policeman at Park Station checked my papers and looked at me as though I were furniture. Could I feel him absent, turned away from himself? Yes, actually. I've never put it that way, but yes: he wasn't quite there. Then "Is it possible that you look a little bit like this when treating others with contempt?" ## Josh (27, software engineer, secular Jewish, reads rationalist blogs; cold, the precise reply)

### Stage 1

Aditya,

I read it start to finish and did the exercises properly, with real people, so this letter has names in it. This is the order things happened in.

**The opening.** "Contempt is the claim that you're a different kind of thing than me, but for it to land, you must be the same kind of thing." My first move was to test it. You can't hold a rock in contempt; you can't really hold a dog in contempt either. You can be disgusted by a dog, but you don't sneer at it, because there's nothing in there to receive the sneer. So the claim survived its first counterexample, and that's when I sat up a bit. It's a mechanism stated sharply enough to be wrong, which is about the fastest way to get me to keep reading. Bought tentatively.

"Hold them tentatively, until you see the picture they assemble." Fine. I'd rather be told this than not.

**The memory.** I went with the obvious one, which is a little embarrassing. I write replies online to people I think are bad thinkers. They're polite, precise, sourced, and constructed so the other person looks like an idiot in front of everyone. For years I told myself I was correcting misinformation. Then my friend Sam said I "win arguments and lose people," and I knew right away that the humiliation had been the point the whole time. It didn't feel like new information. It felt like he'd read it off a screen I'd been keeping face-down.

So "it was right there to see, fully available to consciousness, but you were *turned away* from it" matched. My philosophy-of-mind reflex wanted to know what "fully available to consciousness" means operationally, and I'd still like to know. But the experience matched, and I respected this a lot: "If that doesn't match anything in your memory, the rest of this won't make sense, and I'd rather you know that now." That's a falsification condition on page one. Most books like this never give you one.

"Did you find a way to deflect? If you did, did that happen passively?" No. When someone replied "you're just trying to dunk on me," I had a ready answer every time: I'm engaging with the substance. That wasn't passive. I had it loaded.

"Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" Good question, and it stayed open in me, which I liked.

**The cashier.** I used the guy at the coffee place by my office, Luis. The smile and thank-you just went out of me. When I tried the sneer, my mind started building a reason before I'd even pictured it: he's slow, he got my order wrong last week. He did get it wrong once, months ago, and my brain fetched that like it had been waiting. I caught my own reasoning running in real time. That was the first place the chapter did something to me rather than telling me something.

My caveat is that I'm a polite, conditioned person, and the asymmetry could be training rather than nature. I'll grant you the observation, not yet the explanation.

**The remorse question.** I looked hard for a counterexample, because that's what I do. The nearest one is the times I've conceded to Derek, a coworker who takes credit for things, just to keep a meeting moving. Afterward I felt something close to self-betrayal. But when I looked at it, it was anger at being weak, not remorse; it didn't feel like going against myself. So no counterexample survived. I'll flag that you define remorse as "the feeling of having gone against myself," and that definition does a lot of the work. Someone could say you've rigged it. I don't think you have, but I noticed the hinge.

**The encounter.** The hot version isn't me. I don't do "he's **bad**, dammit," and "I would defy a message like that too, and violently" is not my temperament. I skimmed the eye-contact lines a bit. Then this stopped me:

"He can't prove I'm doing this, can he? No. And crucially, *neither can I*. So I know it's not on purpose."

That is my exact inference. I've run it in so many words: there's no proof of intent, so there was no intent. As an engineer I'd call it a type error, treating absence of evidence I control as evidence of absence. Seeing it written as a thought someone has, rather than as an argument, was uncomfortable in a useful way.

"Ha! Classic projection." I say this. Not out loud, but I think it.

The cold-version paragraph rescued the section for me: "a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them." That's Derek. I route around him. I'm unfailingly courteous to him. And there is a small satisfaction. I'd been calling it efficiency.

**The Corruption.** "I immediately sense his hardening, and feel a brief flicker of satisfaction, vindication, even relief at being proven right about him—just before I relabel it disgust or dismay." Yes. When someone online doubles down after my reply, I feel a little click of having been right. Then I say something like "sad, really." The relabeling is real and I hadn't noticed it before. The quoted line from his side ("why the hell shouldn't I?") I took as your guess about his mind. Plausible, not shown.

**The two checks.** Is punishing evil viscerally satisfying? Yes, obviously. I don't think anyone honest says no.

The worst enemy. I tried a couple and ended on Derek, because he's real and I see him every day. Vengeance or the pill. My stated answer is the pill; I'm a utilitarian when I'm writing things down. But when I actually pictured it, I noticed I wanted the pill to work in a particular way. I wanted Derek to have the change of heart *and know I'd been right about him*. I wanted him to come to me. A plain change of heart, where he just quietly starts crediting people and never thinks of me, felt unsatisfying. That's the craving wearing a nicer outfit, and I wouldn't have seen it without the setup. "I tell myself it's innocuous as long as I don't act on it" is also true of me.

**"I'll go ahead and name what's happening."** This is the best part of the chapter for me, and it's insight, not lecture, because it's a mechanism. "And neither half alone would get me what I need: his reaction, and my innocence." That's a two-constraint optimization: an accident gets pity, a visible intention gets blame, so only the hidden-but-visible version gets both. It reminded me of Trivers on self-deception and of The Elephant in the Brain, but you put it in the interpersonal loop, and I hadn't seen it there.

I read "The signal is the pretense that there is no signal" twice. The second time it clicked.

"Conscious yet unaware: that's the limbo state." I'd already got this from the opening and the paragraph before; it felt like a restatement.

The integrity paragraph did land, though: "But someone always was: me." Simple and true.

**"Genuine darkness" through the corrector.** "my better nature will simply never fall for it. That's not something I can choose to override." That's strong, and stated as fact. I let it pass because the corrector section came next and pulled me in.

**The corrector.** Priya, my first tech lead. I was writing code review comments that were technically correct and quietly brutal, and a junior engineer was thinking about quitting.
