# Short book cold reads, round 5 (September 18, 2026)

*Two readers, no preface or intro. Stage 1: the new Chapters 1 and 2 (the split). Stage 2: the earlier single Chapter 1 (commit 86516c1), compared. Stage 3: the rest of the book. Letters verbatim. Note: the Chapter 2 they read had Claude's draft opening and Claude's fill of the four blanks in the "evil" paragraph.*

## Maren: 52, palliative-care nurse, fourteen years of Kagyu Mahamudra, six years of therapy

### Stage 1

Dear J.,

I read the two chapters over three evenings, after shifts, which is probably the right state to read them in: too tired to perform for the page. You asked me to say what happened rather than what I think of it, so I'll try to keep those apart, and I'll answer his questions where they come up.

**The cases I brought.** For the opening I didn't need to imagine a stranger. There was a son, two winters ago, who had been skimming his mother's morphine for months while she lay at home in pain, and who looked at me across her bed as if I were the intruder. I put his face there. For Chapter 2 I brought Anders, my ex-husband, because that is the contempt I spent six years in a therapist's office learning to see. And for the cold form I brought a woman in our sangha, whom I'll call R., who comes to retreats for the company and talks through the breaks about her kitchen renovation.

**Where I was with him.** The first page works. I do that first looking every day at work; faces become places someone is looking out of, that is simply true and I have not seen it said that way. When I ran it again with the son, the recognition did not go out like a light. It went behind something. And "This is all you are" is the right sentence. I felt my face do it.

But already on that first page he started telling me what was in me. "Something in you wants this verdict to land; to register deep inside. Can you feel that?" With the son, honestly, no. What I wanted was for him to be out of the room and for her to have her medicine. The wanting-it-to-land I know very well, but from Anders, not from the son. So I had to swap cases in the middle of the exercise to keep up with the text. "For the rest of us: notice that instead of seeing someone whose wellbeing matters, now there is only something rotten" does the same thing. He is describing his own finding in the grammar of mine. Whenever he stays in "I" I lean in. Whenever he says "notice that" followed by the answer, I lean back. That is the pattern of my whole reading, so I'll say it once here and not keep repeating it.

**The monologue.** He asks if it's believable and whether I recognised my mind in it. The first two thirds, yes, uncomfortably. "It's really more like an invitation." "Fighting evil genuinely requires being fierce. Isn't that all I'm doing? Surely that's all I'm doing." "There's only one answer: they are projecting. Classic." That last one made me laugh out loud, alone in my kitchen, because I used that exact word about Anders for years, with a therapist's vocabulary to make it sound clean. And "But there is nothing inviting about my tone or gaze" is a good, plain catch.

The last three lines I did not believe. "I'm going to enjoy making sure everyone sees it" and "It's gone on for far too long in this world, and it ends now" are a man in a film. My mind never says that. My contempt does not announce that it will enjoy itself; if it did I'd catch it. It says "I'm just being honest" and "someone has to be the adult." The monologue is truest where it is petty and least true where it is grand.

One more thing about it. The opening has me looking into someone's eyes. Then suddenly there is an exchange going on: he "just won't let it go", he accuses, I double down. What scene are we in? An argument? A confrontation in a room? I had to build one myself. And the person goes from "they" to "he" without notice.

**The passage that confused me.** The section from "Now for the strange part" through the "stack" and the "burning question". I read it three times. I follow this far: he sees my hypocrisy, that maddens him, his fury becomes my evidence. I know that; I used a calm voice on Anders precisely so that he would shout and I would be the reasonable one. But then the text moves from "I rely on his reaction" to "I must be revealing to him that I am deceiving myself" and "I *need him* to see this", as though showing him my self-deception were the aim. In my case the aim was for him to feel it and not be able to point to it. That is what Chapter 2 says, much more clearly, under "How you sent it". In Chapter 1 it is wound into a knot and then handed to me as a "burning question" that, I'm sorry, was not burning for me. It was his question. I hadn't formed it.

Same with "There's something curious embedded in that bottom layer, which we'll come back to", and then later "Earlier I said we'd come back to something curious". When he does come back, the payoff is "the bottom-most thing I was hiding from myself was nothing but the fact that I was hiding", and just before it, "There is nothing to turning away except knowing and not-knowing." I can't tell you whether that is true. It went by as a clever thing. It may be the deepest sentence in the chapter, and it is close to how my teachers talk about ma-rigpa, the not-recognising that arises together with the knowing. But I was told it, I did not find it, and the cheating example that follows is too thin to carry it.

**Limbo.** This is the best thing in the chapter and it is where the real work happened in me. "Did it feel like new information? Or like something you had always known, but somehow hadn't let yourself know?" He asks and then stops, and because he stopped, I answered. The day in my therapist's office when I finally said "I despised him, and I made sure he knew" was not news. It was like admitting the colour of my own front door. "Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" is also a real question, left open, and I am still turning it over.

I did the exercise with something small: taking the last good shift swap before my colleague Birgit saw it was posted. With her face really in front of me, it would not go. The moment I let her blur a little, a reason arrived on its own: she has no one at home to get back to. That was instructive. I watched the reason arrive.

Then he closes it: "If you look carefully, you'll find that limbo contains knowledge of itself, in limbo." Perhaps I would have. He got there before me and told me what was in my pocket.

The "little grace" passage: the content is true and I know it from the inside. "Nobody ever looked honestly at something in themselves while being told they were worthless for having it" is what six years of therapy taught me at considerable expense. But "let's give ourselves a little grace" is the voice of a workshop facilitator, and it came before I had felt any need of it. Likewise in Chapter 2: "If you need to stop here for a while, stop. This is the heaviest part, and it doesn't get heavier." I sit with dying people for a living. I will know when I need to stop. I can see that another reader might need that hand on the shoulder. I felt managed.

**What produced a real recognition.** Three things, and I can name them.

First, the cold paragraph: "a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them." That is R. That is exactly what I do to R., and she feels it; I have seen her stop mid-sentence when I put on my pleasant face. I thought I had finished with this in therapy. I had finished with the hot kind. The small satisfaction is what got me. I hadn't admitted the satisfaction.

Second, in Chapter 2: "Present enough to plant the evidence, absent enough not to recognize it as planted." That is the most exact description of what I did in my marriage that I have read anywhere, including in the books my therapist gave me.

Third, and this is the one I will carry out of the room: "The look on their face that tells us it landed, which we'll pretend not to have seen." My daughter was sixteen during the worst of the divorce. I know that look on her face. I have never once let myself know that I saw it, and I saw it. I put the manuscript down there for the night, and not because he gave me permission.

**Is Chapter 2 necessary?** Partly. It says it will ask for my case, and "every sentence is an offer" is a good contract. But he doesn't keep it. "If it is, then you *know* they're redeemable, and you're blocking their way back." "This was all there to see, if you had looked." "Some part of you already knows that it cannot be anyone else's, either." Those aren't offers. And a great deal of it is Chapter 1 said again with "you" where "I" was: working hard to keep him trapped, so part of me thought he could leave (already in Chapter 1); the darkness can't survive being looked at (already there); no good kind of darkness (now a third and fourth time); he'll pass it along; the single move; the malice/contempt definitions. By "Why" I was reading a summary of what I'd read.

If it were mine I would cut it by half. I would keep: "How you sent it" with the second-conversation blockquote; "we only treat people as irredeemable because part of us knows they're not", which is a real turn; the saving grace, especially "the thing we're turned away *from* is also the thing that warns us when we *are* turned away from it"; the conscious/unconscious paradox with nice against kind; the people we love; the executive; and the last paragraph. I would cut most of "What it is" and most of "Why". The "humanity's sacrifice, the dumping ground for our collective shadow" message was put in my mouth. My contempt for Anders never said anything so large. It said: I am the grown-up and you are not. A theory of scapegoating may be right, but it is not what I found when I looked, and he presents it as what "we're actually sending".

One matter of order. "Can they do it with kindness in their eyes?" I balked at that in Chapter 1, because I have watched families insist on brutal, futile treatment for a dying parent with what looks very like tenderness. Only in Chapter 2 does he say that out of view doesn't mean out of sight, and then it resolves: those families are looking at their own fear, not at her. That parenthesis is needed where the claim is first made.

**As a practitioner.** Where it is thin or overclaimed:

"My darkness cannot survive my looking directly at it, by definition." Not by definition. By looking, and not always. I have looked straight at contempt on the cushion and had it look straight back for a week. What cannot survive the looking is its righteousness. That is a smaller claim and I think the true one.

"The horror is evidence that the darkness is not your nature." Earlier he said "hint", and hint is right. Horror can just as well be shame in a better coat. In my tradition nobody establishes the nature of mind by inference from a feeling, and I distrust being reassured about my nature by someone who hasn't met me. "It's a tremendous relief when that sinks in" is him telling me how I'll feel. I didn't feel relief. I felt tired and rather sad. He does say some people find tiredness or grief instead of horror, and at that line I felt accurately seen, so credit there.

"The darkness" as something that "takes control" and "commandeers" and "finds a way in". He says it isn't a literal entity and that us/not-us are both true, and I respect the care. But we are taught that Mara is one's own mind, and the reason is practical: the moment the klesha is a someone, I have a new place to put the blame. A few paragraphs later he quotes Paul on Satan, which arrives with no preparation and tilts it further. I'm not offended, I'm wary. "Nobody, underneath, believes their contempt is righteous" and "everybody knows it" outrun anything he or I could check. And "Everything I can accomplish with the darkness can be accomplished better without it" is asserted. I believe it, from the hardest family meetings I've had to run. He hasn't shown it yet.

Where it is better than my tradition usually manages: we talk about ignorance as though it were a fog that settled on us. He shows it as something I am doing, moment by moment, with a payoff, and with some part of me watching. Limbo is a better word for that than anything I was given in fourteen years. And "when what we should be is *kind*—fiercely kind, in a way that stops the harm without cutting off their potential to return" is what wrathful compassion means, said so that my colleagues could hear it. Most dharma talk about compassion gets heard as "be nice", and he refuses that.

**So: what works and what doesn't.** What works is every place he tells me what he found and then asks a question and stops. What doesn't is every place he tells me what I will find, every knot of "know they know we know", and the volume of repetition in Chapter 2. It is heavy, and I don't mind heavy. I mind being steered while it's heavy.

Tell him the sigh paragraph was worth the whole read.

With love,
Maren


### Stage 2

Dear J.,

I've now read the earlier single chapter. My short answer will annoy him: the new one has the better shape, and the earlier one has the better first third. If I had to hand one of them to someone in the sangha tomorrow, I would hand over the earlier one, with some reluctance, because it says everything once. But the version I'd want to exist is the new structure with several pieces of the old prose put back.

**What the earlier one did better**

*The monologue.* I told you the new monologue goes false at the end, where he is going to "enjoy making sure everyone sees it" and evil "ends now". The earlier one never leaves the petty register, and that register is where my mind lives. "Is this a bit disingenuous? Maybe a little." "They're **bad**, dammit, and this proves it." "They think *I'm* the bad guy. *Ha!*" That "Ha!" is more truthful than the whole closing speech of the new version. And it ends on "Yet for some reason, this only makes them even *more* defiant..." which is dry and lets me see the blindness without being told about it. It is also half the length. The new one has better individual lines ("But there is **nothing** inviting about my tone or gaze" is new and I'd keep it, and "Surely that's all I'm doing"). But the earlier one is a mind, and the new one becomes a demonstration. One thing the new one does add, the look in the other man's eyes saying "Don't pretend you're doing something noble here", helps the scene. The earlier version has the same staging problem I complained about: where are we, and what is the exchange?

*The naming.* This is the big one. In the earlier version, after the monologue, he says: "A dark part of my mind—a part that *needs* them to be bad—is *revealing* itself to them, knowing how they'll receive it. But at the same time, this part is *hiding* itself from me, so that I can (in all sincerity) hold their reaction against them." I understood that on first reading. It is the same content as the new version's "strange part", "stack", and "burning question", which I read three times and still hold loosely. The new version tried to derive it layer by layer, and the derivation is harder to follow than the thing itself.

And directly after it come two sentences the new version has lost, and they are the ones I'd most want back: "I don't mean that as a standing fact about me, or about you. It's true while I'm in it, and close to invisible when I'm not, which is why looking back rarely finds it. If you can find even one memory where it's plainly true, that's the one to look at." That is how a practice instruction ought to sound. It doesn't tell me what I'll find. It tells me where to look and admits that I mostly won't find it, which is true, and which made me trust him. When I read that, I went straight to one particular dinner with Anders, and it was plainly true there.

*Why the contempt is maddening.* Also lost: "flashing someone your inner darkness can be frightening or off-putting, but it usually isn't *maddening* in a way that produces defiance. For that, we must also telegraph the fact that we think it's *righteous.* That way, they know they can't appeal to our better nature—because we're convinced this *is* our better nature." I stopped on that. It explains something I have watched many times across a hospital bed and never understood: why the sanctimonious nurse gets more fury from a family than the merely rude one. There is nobody home to appeal to. The new version has the malice/contempt distinction, but as two "levels of hiding", which is a taxonomy. The old sentence gives me the other person's experience, and the taxonomy never did. "Which means we must *genuinely believe it* ourselves. I wasn't putting on an act" also matters, because without it the account sounds like hypocrisy, and hypocrisy is too easy a thing to disown.

*The way into Limbo.* In the earlier one, he has just shown that the hidden part "actively evades detection" and yet he sincerely can't see it, and he says: "There's something strange going on here—and the easiest way to show it is by example." So Limbo arrives because I need it. In the new one it arrives to answer "How do I show him something I don't (and *cannot*) know about myself, and moreover, *that* I don't know it?", which as I said was his question and not mine.

*Two small repairs that went the wrong way.* The earlier Limbo ends: "did part of you know you were, even as you hid it? If so, limbo contains knowledge of itself." The new one: "If you look carefully, you'll find that limbo contains knowledge of itself, in limbo." The old "If so" left me free. The new one tells me what's in my pocket, which is the very thing I objected to in my first letter before I knew there had been a better version. And the self-contempt passage used to begin "One more thing about that memory." Now it begins "Before we move on, let's give ourselves a little grace." The old opening is a man continuing his thought, and the new one is a facilitator. The paragraph is otherwise the same, so the change of heading is the only thing that moved.

*Saying it once.* In the earlier version "present enough to plant the evidence", the trapped/redeemable turn, the darkness that can't bear being seen, the ripple outward, each come once. In the new pair most of them come twice, once in his case and once in "mine". I understand now why Chapter 2 felt like a summary: it *is* the back half of the old chapter, nearly word for word, with headings put in, while the new Chapter 1 had already spent a good part of it.

**What the earlier one did worse**

The switch to "you" is worse there, and heavier. "From here I'll say 'you,' on the assumption that you found at least some of this in yourself" and then about three thousand words of "you're holding them", "you *know* they're redeemable", "you made sure they saw it", with no break. By the time I got to humanity's sacrifice I was braced, and a braced reader isn't looking. The new arrangement, where he stays in "I" for a whole chapter and only then asks for my case, is kinder to the reader in the proper sense: it gives me a chance to see. The new Chapter 2's "every sentence is an offer. Hold it against your case and keep what fits" is a better contract than the old "on the assumption that", even though he then breaks it in the places I named. One real improvement inside it: "I was working hard to keep him there, which only makes sense if part of me thought he could leave. Is that true in the case you picked?" is better than the old "You wouldn't need to keep them trapped there unless you thought they could leave. So you *know*..." He should do that conversion everywhere and finish the job.

The hall of mirrors comes too early in the old one, on the second page, before I have anything to hang it on: "we know they feel it, and they *know* we know they feel it... and we know they know that, too... etc." The new one moves it late and adds "You don't have to trace the logic. You might have felt it before." That is right. I have felt it, and I did not need to trace it.

The word "evil" lands in the old one with no warning: "It is the condition we all recognize as **evil**." I flinched and half left. In the new one he tells me why he uses it: "So that I never again believe that my mistake is a different kind of thing than what my enemies do." It is a reason in his own voice, about himself, and I could accept the word on those terms.

The new Chapter 1 has things the old one simply lacked, and they're good. The "kindness in their eyes" check. The honest concession that contempt can stop people: "Not that it can't stop people. My behavior might make him think twice... But does he feel remorseful?" That answered a doubt I did have. "My justifications, on the other hand, are believable only to me (and my allies)" is sharp. And the ending. The old chapter ends on a statement of what compassion means. The new Chapter 1 ends: "Which makes me wonder: might this be what my enemies are doing, too?" and then "I might even have an inkling of what that way is. But as long as this problem continues to exist in me, I'm liable to keep doing this." That's a man still inside it, and it is the most trustworthy moment in either version. In the new order that question also comes as a discovery, where in the old one "they had to turn away from their victim in exactly the same way" is just stated on page two.

**Would a sangha friend feel improvement or dilution?**

Both, and I think they'd say it in that order. For the first half of the new Chapter 1 they'd miss the old monologue and the old naming paragraph, and feel that something direct had been made complicated. From Limbo to the end of Chapter 1 they'd feel it had deepened. Then in Chapter 2 they would recognise the old text with the furniture moved, realise they were being walked through the same house twice, and call that dilution. Thubten, who read the earlier one and told me about it at Losar, quoted the "plant the evidence" line to me from memory. That line sits in a stronger place in the old version, right after Limbo, as the first thing Limbo explains. In the new one it is tucked under a heading and introduced with "In my case the aim was the giveaway," as if it were a recap.

**What I would do**, if he wants it that plainly: keep the two-chapter shape and the new ending of Chapter 1. Put back the old monologue, grafting in the two or three best new lines. Put back the old naming paragraph with its "one memory" sentences and the "maddening / righteous" paragraph, and let them replace the stack and the burning question. Put back "There's something strange going on here" as the door into Limbo, "If so" at the end of it, and "One more thing about that memory." Then cut Chapter 2 to what Chapter 1 has not already said, and hold it to its own contract.

With love,
Maren


### Stage 3

Dear J.,

I've finished. The first thing to tell him is that the rest of the book is better than its opening, and treats me better. From Chapter 3 on he mostly does what I praised in the first letter: he says what he found, hands me a check, and steps back. "If you find none of this, good; I have no way to argue with that and won't try." "If you look at such a person and find only a monster, I can't argue you out of that from here. I can only say what I found." "See what you find." A reader who is spoken to like that stays in the room. I wish the man who wrote those sentences would go back and revise Chapter 2.

**What happened in me**

The Interlude is the best thing in the manuscript. I gave the pill to the morphine son. I expected to find nothing, since as I told you what I mostly wanted was him out of the room. I was wrong. What I wanted was for him to have to sit by that bed and hear her, and the pill would give him that, and still something in me was disappointed that it wouldn't *hurt* enough. So there is the flicker. Then the small sizes: "the small wait, after you've said it, to watch it land." I do that wait. I did it to a junior doctor last month.

And the shrug. His is "well, who can really say?" Mine, I discovered, is dharma-flavoured. About R. in our sangha I say "she has her own path." It sounds spacious, and it is the filing-away.

The second test worked completely. My first person was a consultant, years ago. My second was the ward sister in my training who caught me being short with a dying man's wife, took me into the corridor and took me apart, and I walked back in a better nurse and have loved her ever since. "Almost the same words. Opposite outcomes." I didn't need that argued to me. In Chapter 1 he asserted that "Everything I can accomplish *with* the darkness can be accomplished better without it", and I wrote to you that he hadn't shown it. Here he shows it, out of my own memory. I'll say something he may not like: I would have borne Chapter 2 much better if I had already met the second person. For two chapters the only light in the book is one look at a stranger on the first page, and everything else is what I do wrong. I understand he wants the weight first. But the second person is the proof that there is somewhere to stand while looking, and it arrives late.

In Chapter 3, the check from the receiving end, "feel for *where* it was trying to reach. Not your pride", is superb. I used my former mother-in-law. It's true: an insult reaches my pride, and her contempt reached for something further in. "Contempt and love are addressed to the same place. One says *I know you're in there.* The other says *there's nobody in there*, and they both say it to the somebody who is." That does all the work that the know-they-know-we-know paragraphs in the opening were straining at, and I didn't have to trace anything.

Chapter 4: "There is one choice, and it's real. While you can still feel both pulls... you're still looking, and you can keep looking or stop... It came labeled *I've had a long day*." That is the most exact description of the moment that I've read, in dharma books or out of them. It is the shift swap with Birgit from my first letter. The account of responsibility that follows ("how fast you catch the blindness, in what you repair when sight returns") is better than what my tradition usually offers lay people, which is either karma, or nothing.

Chapter 5: "if the harm simply stopped tomorrow, with nobody punished and nobody exposed, would you feel relief, or robbed?" I asked it of Anders. If he woke up tomorrow a decent, happy man and never once understood what those years were: robbed. After six years of therapy, still robbed. I'm not ashamed of it, I'm interested, and being interested instead of ashamed is, I think, what he is after. Then the three pairs of sentences: "*You lied to me.* With the extra: *Of course you lied to me.*" I will use those with the juniors on my ward. And the paragraph that was written for our sangha, whether he knows it or not: "The friend who 'isn't there yet.' The beginner's question, heard with a patient smile. And pity, which passes for compassion and is contempt with a soft face... would I be glad if they got there before I did?" Would I be glad if R. got there before me? No. I put the pages down again.

**Does the opening set it up? Does the rest deliver?**

What the later chapters actually use from the opening is a short list: turned toward and turned away; *this is all you are*; limbo; the part that knows and the part that proves; that the look is read by the other; "no good kind"; the sigh to the people we love; the executive. Nothing in Chapters 3 to 5 depends on the stack, the "bottom layer", or the "burning question". Nothing depends on "humanity's sacrifice" either. Chapter 4's loop is the grown-up version of that: "you can only be good *relative* to someone worse. So as long as you don't feel at home, you can't afford to let anyone else get there." I recognised that; I did not recognise the sacrifice speech. So the rest of the book confirms, for me, what to cut from the opening. The "Why" section of Chapter 2 is a weaker early draft of something Chapter 4 does properly. Chapter 2 could end on the question and let Chapter 4 answer it.

What the opening promised and the rest delivers: the "inkling" of a better way at the end of Chapter 1 (the Interlude and Chapter 5 pay it in full); "fiercely kind" (paid, with examples); the audience (the locker room, which is good); the horror as a hint about my nature (Chapter 3 opens by paying it, and more modestly than Chapter 2 claimed it: "The simplest explanation is that care belongs to awareness itself." Chapter 2 should come down to that register. "Hint" and "simplest explanation" I can live with; "evidence" I can't.) Also paid, and this corrects a complaint in my first letter: Chapter 1 says darkness cannot survive being looked at "by definition". Chapter 5 says "In my experience it doesn't survive that... It comes back, and you look again; the first several times you may find only a tight chest and the story." The second is true to the cushion. He should make the first agree with it.

Where it doesn't deliver: I was told the relief was in the rest of the book. There is relief in the Interlude and in parts of 5. But Chapter 4 is as heavy as anything in the opening, and its last third, the institutions, "the truth behind the 'evil global conspiracy'", "every system of extraction and destruction on Earth rests on that", is the one stretch where I left. It is asserted at a great height, in a book whose whole authority comes from *check it in your own case*. "At some level, we need them to remain" is him telling me again. The tree and the stone arch I could check, and I felt it, and that paragraph I'd keep. The start of Chapter 5 (ideology, the meaning crisis, "what I think is eating the world") has the same trouble; the chapter really begins at "Here is how to tell them apart in yourself."

The ten claims "stated flat" in Chapter 3: I respect the honesty of "I believe all of them". But ten bold sentences in a row, with "one kind of evil", "only one guide", read as a creed, and Hitler arrives to answer an objection I hadn't raised. "Compassion is not wisdom" I was glad to see. Those are the two wings, and anyone in my job has watched loving people make a mess.

**References back that no longer fit**

- The Interlude opens: "You may have come through the last three chapters thinking that a hypothetical stranger proves nothing about you... Bring to mind someone whose harm was real." But the new Chapter 2 already had me bring a real case and work it for a whole chapter. That sentence belongs to the older book.
- Chapter 4: "Look at what you watched it do in Chapter 1. It aimed the verdict, anticipated their reaction, and prepared to use it." That is the wording of the *earlier* opening ("making sure the verdict lands, anticipating their likely reaction, and preparing to use it against them"). In the new one the aiming is in Chapter 2, and Chapter 1 was *his* case, so "what you watched it do" in me isn't quite right either. "A darker world" is also Chapter 2 now.
- Chapter 5: "This is the move you watched in Chapter 1, done to everyone at once: the part that knows made to look naive." The demoting of the part that knows, with the "why the fuck shouldn't I", is in Chapter 2 now.
- Chapter 4, "the way the stranger's did through nothing but a look," and Chapter 5, "The pressure on them is the kind you felt from the stranger." Which stranger? The only stranger I have is the one I looked at on page one, and he put no pressure on me. I think he means the first person in the Interlude, who is never called a stranger. It reads like a name from a version I haven't seen.
- The kindness-in-the-eyes check now comes three times: Chapter 1, the saving grace in Chapter 2, and the opening of Chapter 3. In Chapter 3 it's doing real work. One of the other two can go.
- The self-contempt paragraph ("*you are what you did.* It feels like accountability") appears nearly word for word in Chapter 1 and again at the end of the note for people who were hurt, with a reminder in Chapter 2 between.
- In Chapter 5, "What is yours is how fast you catch it afterward" starts a section as though answering something said just before it. Nothing was. Something has been cut above it.
- "Look at who is relieved when you hold it." I stumbled: hold what? The verdict, I worked out, but "hold" had just been used for holding rage.

**As a practitioner, before it goes round the sangha**

He names that danger himself when he lists the parasite picture's drawbacks, and I trusted him more for it.

*Going home.* "As long as you don't feel at home, you can't afford to let anyone else get there." That's the elder brother standing outside the party in Luke 15. I've preached that story to myself for a decade and never saw that he *can't* go in.

*Contempt for the merely slow.* "I've found them guilty of being what they are." I teach. Enough said. And the flat-Earther joke is the one time the book is funny about its own trap.

*"At some level, we need them to remain."* My wife asked me once who I'd be without that board to fight. I gave her the look the book is about.

*The note for people who were hurt* is careful and right, and "Staying turned toward someone never means staying near them" is a sentence Al-Anon could use.

*Chapter 5's checks,* above all the sentences with and without "the extra." "*You lied to me.* With the extra: *Of course you lied to me.*" Most useful page in the book. I could hand that page to a first-year teacher. And "would I be glad if they got there before I did?" goes straight to my men's group.

**What didn't**

The argument that turning away can't be chosen because choosing it "would be a wrong done while still looking" felt like winning by definition. So does the Hitler paragraph. If anybody who's certain and wrong turns out not to have been following "the part that knows," then the part that knows can never be caught out, and a history teacher gets nervous. He patches it honestly with "Compassion is not wisdom," and I give him that. But "There is only one guide you can trust" is a hard sentence to hand a union rep. Contracts exist because people who were sure they had us "fully in view" didn't. His test for an ideology is "does it tell you what to do to the person in front of you before you've looked at them?" A grievance procedure does exactly that, on purpose, and thank God for it. Rules get one grudging clause ("can serve it"). They've earned a paragraph.

The most-lost person "carrying *more* of that knowledge": he marks it as an extrapolation and owns the mushrooms, which I respected. I can't check it, and the hostage at the window is a picture I'll keep anyway. The "evil global conspiracy" paragraph: who asked? "What I think is eating the world": too big for the page it's on.

One place I felt handled. "*Wait, are you saying I shouldn't correct bad behavior?*... The objection is fair... But notice when it shows up. It never comes while you're correcting. It comes the moment you're caught adding the verdict, and it's how we slip out." That was my honest question through two chapters, and it didn't come from being caught. It came from having real opponents. "Never" closed a door on me there.

And the paragraph about self-contempt ("*you are what you did.* It feels like accountability") has now appeared three times, in nearly the same words each time.

**What would have to change for me to stay willingly, not just dutifully**

1. People. In five chapters and an interlude, the only thing I learn about the author's actual life is that he once took mushrooms. There's no enemy with a name, no second person who caught *him*, no fight he fought clean and what it cost him. He keeps saying "in me," and I believe him, but I never see him in a room. He asks me for my father. I'd like one afternoon of his.
2. One worked case of fierce-without-the-verdict in the world. He says it's "among the most effective forces in the history of people facing power they couldn't match" and names nobody. Give me Birmingham. King's letter from that jail is about as harsh as English gets, and there is no verdict in it anywhere. I'd teach that page.
3. More way through. The book is called a way through, and the method is about two paragraphs long: catch it faster, and look at the contempt itself, "Where is it? What's it made of?" The checks are good. But after five chapters of diagnosis I wanted more than "it comes back, and you look again" and a pointer to an appendix and a teacher I don't have.
4. Keep saying *this is my belief* at the spot where it's a belief: Chapter 2 and the fourth paragraph of Chapter 3, not only the top of Chapter 4.

**References back that don't fit what I read**

- The interlude opens: "You may have come through the last three chapters thinking that a hypothetical stranger proves nothing about you." But Chapter 2 had me bring a real case. By then it was my father, not a stranger.
- Chapter 4: "Look at what you watched it do in Chapter 1. It aimed the verdict, anticipated their reaction, and prepared to use it." In the version I read first, the aiming and planting are in Chapter 2's "How you sent it," and the "darker world" goal is Chapter 2 also. Chapter 1 was *his* monologue, not something I watched "it" do. That sentence matches the earlier single-chapter version.
- Chapter 5: "This is the move you watched in Chapter 1... the part that knows made to look naive." Demoting the part that knows is in Chapter 2's "Where it goes."
- Chapter 4: "the way the stranger's did through nothing but a look," and Chapter 5: "The pressure on them is the kind you felt from the stranger." Which stranger? In the opening, *I* was the one looking at the stranger, and the look was mine. The stranger never did anything to me. If he means the first person in the interlude, that man is never called a stranger. It reads like a leftover from some other draft.
- Chapter 3 mentions "the trap" as though it were a named thing. The opening says "keeping him trapped" and once "the entire trap," but I hadn't registered it as a term.
- The rest checked out: limbo in degrees, the executive, King's line, the second person, the end of Chapter 2.

Tell him thank you. Tell him that the question "relief, or robbed?" is going on an index card in my wallet for the next board meeting, and that I intend to be every bit as fierce as before. I'll let you know whether I manage it without the half-second pause.
