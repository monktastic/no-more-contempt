Dear Aditya,

I'm a hospice chaplain. I've sat in Mahamudra for twenty years in a Kagyu sangha, and most of my working life has been spent with the dying and with people who were badly hurt by someone. **What happened when I did the exercises**

The face. I used a woman from our unit last spring, eighty-nine, who kept apologizing for taking up my time. The recognition was immediate, and her wellbeing registered before I decided anything. Then I put up an enemy. Not anyone who hurt me badly; the hospital vice-president who cut our overnight chaplain coverage, so people now die at 3 a.m. with nobody there. You're right. The widening. The dog, then a crow: fainter, but it didn't stop. Your thousand-year tree landed in my body near where a violation of a person lands. The stone arch didn't. The decision to stop seeing. Here I have to push back. "Try it: hold someone's eyes and decide, while holding them, to stop seeing them. See if you can find the moment. I can't." I can, sometimes. The toddler and the grandparent. This one is true in my work, and painfully. Families don't feel contempt for a parent with dementia until someone says "she's doing it on purpose." Then it arrives instantly. Your check found it exactly.

Smallest wrong and biggest. The biggest took me years, and I knew it more, not less. Confirmed.

Which scenes gave me contempt. The locker room, right away. And the "boo hoo" voice made me wince, because that one is mine. I was hurt young and I have said, more than once, that it made me a better person. Your sentence about taking credit for the people who held me hit home. That check works.

**4. Contradictions**

"Turning toward" has a target, then doesn't. Chapter 2: "Call this being *turned toward* them." Chapter 4: "That's what I've been calling the light: the thing that, when someone is aimed at it, you know it, and when they're aimed away from it, you know that too, and you can't define either." Then, a page later in Chapter 4: "Turning toward has no target." I can make them agree, with a Mahamudra teaching in hand. A reader without one sees three different claims.

"Life" means two things. Chapter 2: "Whatever is looking out of your eyes right now is alive, and it's the same kind of thing that looked out of the stranger's." and "If what's looking out of us has nothing to differ by, then to know this in yourself is already to know it in them." So life, at the level of what looks, has nothing to differ by. The Picture I Use: "Two people full of life differ in a thousand ways; two perfectly dark rooms look the same from inside." Now life is what makes people differ, and only darkness is uniform. The contrast the Picture needs undoes the one Chapter 2 built. Also, Chapter 2 grounds the light in awareness ("The simplest explanation I can find is that care belongs to awareness itself.") and then, a few paragraphs on, in life ("When I look most deeply into what I am, I find life."). Is it awareness or life? My tradition would say awareness. Pick one, or say why they're the same.

The Devil is a character after all. Chapter 3 warns: "A name for an affliction easily turns into a character, and then the character has to be fought, and fighting something in your own mind is mostly a way of not looking at it." The Picture: "Treat it as an essence and you get contempt." Then the Epilogue: "I like to tell myself that every time we do this, we give the Devil a middle finger. He's the one who actually deserves it." "He," "deserves," and the finger. In Chapter 3, the finger is your own example of low-grade contempt: "I give the finger to the driver who cuts me off, and ten minutes later I cut someone off myself and don't even register it, because I had somewhere to be." I smiled at the Epilogue line. Then I thought: he's just handed the verdict to the darkness, the one thing he spent two chapters saying has no one there to receive it. A careful reader will catch it, and it comes three pages from the end, where trust matters most.

Physical shape against the disclaimer. The Epilogue: "knowledge of my wrongdoing always takes physical shape in my "external" world," and "Ideas that live in our collective shadow manifest "externally," taking shape in our physical reality." Then: "None of this is a reading of anyone's illness or loss, or of what someone else did to them; it's about the harm I do." I'm glad the disclaimer is there. But "always" plus "physical" is wider than the disclaimer. If all of a dying woman's wrongdoing takes physical shape in her world, her tumor is in her world. The disclaimer says what you intend. The claim says something else.

"Shape," twice. The Picture: "It is right about the shape and wrong about where it lives." The Epilogue: "will never quite take the shape we desperately want." In the first, shape is the structure of coordination, and the archetype gets it right. In the second, shape is people in charge whom we can punish, which is the misplacement. Small, but I noticed.

The parasite's goal. Chapter 3 grants it one goal: "What it wants is what Chapter 1 found: to stay hidden from the one it lives in," and "So it's headed somewhere: toward a world in which everyone is fundamentally *other*, because that's the only world in which it's safe." The Picture: "The darkness only has to convince us the light was never there, and you heard it say so in Chapter 3, in its own voice: genuine goodness is a myth." I can join them: staying hidden needs others to point at, and that needs us to forget the light. The page never joins them, so it reads as two goals.

The choosing moment. Chapter 3: "See if you can find the moment. I can't." then "There is one choice, and it's real; it just never comes under that name." You explain the join ("under that name"), but it cost me a reread.

**5. The background hum**

The thread is clear, and I could follow it. Chapter 3: "Call it a low-grade infection: no fever, nothing anyone would notice, and running the whole time." The Picture: "Under the nudges runs the low-grade infection from Chapter 3, in billions of people at once and in every feed, each turn too small to notice: a background hum of darkness." Then institutions: "An institution does what all those small turns add up to, and nobody in it feels responsible." Then the conspiracy: "What's being sensed underneath them is the background hum, which supplies those rooms with people willing to sit in them, and supplies the rest of us with the certainty that we'd never be among them." Then the Devil: "And in this picture it is the coordinator: not a plan, not anyone, but the background hum seen as one thing, the same interest in every mind, building the same cover, until the one orientation every one of us steers by is the one thing none of us believes is there." Then the biosphere: "It doesn't take monsters. It takes the background hum." Then the Epilogue picks it up by name. One phrase, carried all the way. That's good work.

Where it felt earned: the institution step. I've watched it in my own hospital. Nobody decided the dying should die alone at night. A thousand small budget turns, each defensible, each with a face kept out of view, added up to it. I've watched it, so I believed it. The conspiracy step also felt earned, and "that misplacement has filled graves" is the sentence that made me trust you with it. The biosphere step worked for me because the tree worked for me. A reader for whom the tree didn't land will take that step as your report, which is what you said they should do.

Where it felt asserted: two places.

First, the hum gets credit for something the book only showed about contempt. Chapter 3 says the low-grade turning "has no enemy in it at all." But the Picture's argument that all the darkness is one ("they all face the same way") rests on the verdict passing between people: "Chapter 3 said that when you send the verdict to the "bad person," the darkness in him gains and so does yours, each better hidden from its host." That's contempt, not the hum. The step from my own quiet, self-serving corner-cutting to "the same interest in every mind, building the same cover" is never shown. I think it can be shown in a sentence or two: the hum needs the bad people as cover, so it feeds the contempt that makes them. The corrupt-leaders paragraph almost says this. But that paragraph is itself asserted. "Corrupt leaders meet a hidden need in all of us" is a big claim, and "Every time I claim they're a different kind of thing, part of me is helping keep them there" doesn't say how. By voting? By not looking? I believe it. I can't check it.

Second, the Devil step. Calling it "the background hum seen as one thing" is honest. It's a way of seeing, and the opening note says this whole section is a picture. But Mara isn't quite your coordinator. In my tradition Mara is four, the four maras, and the teaching is finally that they're your own grasping. That supports you, actually. You could say so in a line, and a Buddhist reader would trust the paragraph more.

So: earned through the institutions and the conspiracy, asserted at the join from hum to coordination. The opening note makes that acceptable, but it's the one place a skeptical reader will get off.

**The majors, in full (from the first line)**

Chapter 3, the note for the hurt. The note is the best thing of its kind I've read in a book like this. "It is possible to leave, cut contact, and never see them again with no verdict in it at all." I'd read that line aloud to people. Then: "That darkness wants you to take the license, because nothing proves its case better than a good person who was wronged and turned dark." Many people I sit with who were badly hurt did turn dark for a while. They drank, they hit, they left their kids, they said unforgivable things. They will read "turned dark" as a description of themselves and stop there. It is a verdict phrase, in the one note written to spare them one. "Took the license" or "used the hurt to license harm" names the act, not the person, and loses nothing.

The Epilogue's "physical shape." I spend my days with people asking "what did I do to deserve this?" Some have been told by well-meaning relatives that they manifested their cancer. The sentence those relatives would underline is this one: "knowledge of my wrongdoing always takes physical shape in my "external" world." The disclaimer is right but comes afterward, after a laugh, and reads as a hedge. Put it first, or narrow the claim itself: the harm I do comes back through other people and the world we share, not through my body as punishment. If you mean something stronger, say plainly that it doesn't apply to illness, and put that sentence before the claim.

The Epilogue's middle finger. Above, under contradictions. Cut "He's the one who actually deserves it" at least. The finger alone is a joke. The "deserves" is the verdict.

One more for the Epilogue, not a major. "It was self-evidently the Source of creation, and I was That." Any Kagyu teacher reading this will think *nyam*: an experience of clarity and bliss, real and empty, not to be grasped and not to be taken as realization. Your note that it was empty helps. A line saying you know your teachers would call it an experience, not realization, would keep the sangha with you. Without it some will quietly file the whole Epilogue as a trip report. I don't. But they will.

**6. Would I give it away?**

Through the Epilogue: yes, to my sangha friends now, and to my colleagues in chaplaincy, with a word about the Epilogue. Chapter 1 and the note for the hurt are worth the whole thing. The second stranger, the torturer who watches the face, the toddler and the grandparent, pity as "contempt with a soft face": those are true, and I'll use them.

Not yet to a dying patient, and not yet to the most badly hurt people I sit with. For them, three changes first: "turned dark" in the note, the "physical shape" claim narrowed, or its disclaimer put before it, and the Devil's middle finger. They're all small changes. With warmth, and thanks for the work,
