**2. READY?**

**Preface and Introduction: READY.**

One thing is close to major, and it depends on what goes out with this file. The preface says "The full book earns each of those slowly, from the reader's own memory; here I lean on them, which is what lets me be brief." Then the text points three times to an appendix the reader may not have:

**Chapter 1: CLOSE.**

This is the strongest thing in the book. One major objection, from the bench.

The chapter's mechanism assumes that the target defies. "I would defy a message like that too, and violently." "My claim that I'm a *better kind of thing* is maddening precisely because we both know it's false." In family court I saw the other outcome more often: the child who heard *this is all you are* from a parent and did not defy it. He believed it. Your mechanism covers him (the verdict reaches for the somebody who is there, and in him it succeeds), but the chapter never says so. A reader who was that child will read "maddening" and think: it wasn't maddening, it was true for me for thirty years. That reader is precisely the one you most want to keep. One or two sentences in "The Encounter" or "The Corruption" would do it: some targets dig in, some believe it, and both are the verdict landing. Without them, a badly hurt reader concludes the book wasn't written about people like her.

Nothing else in Chapter 1 is major. The buttons exercise already excuses the abuser's face: "If the one who comes to mind is someone who abused you, pick a different enemy for this; that face can wait, or never come." Good. Keep that exemption in force later (see Chapter 3).

**Chapter 2: CLOSE.**

Two major objections.

First, it contradicts Chapter 3 on choice, and a lawyer will catch it on first reading. In "What I'm claiming": "So the judgement that *someone in there is choosing evil* is aimed at a chooser who was never there." And: "Whether stopping looking counts as a choice in the usual sense is a question I don't think has an answer; what I can see of it is in Chapter 3." Then Chapter 3 says: "There is one choice, and it's real." And: "That's why, looking back, it feels like you chose it knowing better, and you did, and that isn't shame's shape, *I am it*."

So the chooser was never there, and the question has no answer, and there is one choice and it's real, and you did choose. Chapter 3's account is the right one; it is the best thing in that chapter. Fix Chapter 2 to match it: the judgement is aimed at someone choosing evil *as evil*, and what he chose was to stop looking. Or cut the claim from the list, since Chapter 3 makes it properly.

Second, the arch breaks the conclusion it's used for. "The tree has no conscience and the arch has no inside." Then, if the reader felt it: "whatever is looking out of your eyes right now is alive, and it's the same kind of thing that looked out of the stranger's." The tree is alive, so it supports the life claim. The arch is not, so if my reaction to the arch landed where a person's violation lands, that is evidence *against* life being what I'm registering. A careful reader will see the step fail. Either drop the arch, or say what the arch shows that the tree doesn't. Your later sentence, "to things I'd once have called stuff", suggests you mean something wider than life. If so, say that rather than "alive."

**Chapter 3: NOT READY.** It's close in substance; it's the order and one contradiction that hold it back.

## 2. Ready?

### Preface and Introduction: READY

I have no major objections. The plant-medicine line will cost you a little with the precept-keepers in a Kagyu group. It's honest, though, and I'd keep it.

### Chapter 1: READY

I have no major objections. The chapter repeats itself internally (see section 3), but nothing in it would embarrass you, and it's the strongest thing in the book.

### Chapter 2: CLOSE

1. **The face exercise loses Chapter 1's safeguard.** Chapter 1 gives the reader a way out: "If the one who comes to mind is someone who abused you, pick a different enemy for this; that face can wait, or never come." Chapter 2 then says "Now put your enemy's face there instead, and try for the same recognition." and doesn't repeat it. A reader who took the out in Chapter 1 may hear "your enemy" as the very person they set aside. One clause would fix this.

2. **"Life" slides between two meanings.** The line is "And, as far as I can tell, life naturally wants to serve life." Anyone with biology in their head will think of a parasitoid wasp, and two chapters later you call the bad thing a parasite. In that paragraph, "life" means whatever is looking out of the eyes, and I can grant that. By "The Picture I Use", though, it covers "A man held as a thing, a forest as lumber, a river as a drain," followed by "Each needed only a living thing held as stuff". A river isn't alive in either sense. Here's my fix:
   - Say once that "life" here means what's doing the looking, and hold it to that meaning.
   - Drop the river, or the word "living."

   This is where an analytically minded reader decides whether you're being careful, and so far you have been.

3. **The common-knowledge hedge.** The sentence is "If that's right, the knowledge is exactly common; if not, it's close enough for the trap to work." Anyone who knows "common knowledge" from game theory will watch you borrow the term and give it back in the same sentence. Cut that sentence, and make "Either way the trap needs it." just "The trap needs it." The face exercise has already done the work. I'm the reader this sentence is for, and the exercise convinced me where this sentence nearly lost me.

### Chapter 3: CLOSE (nearly NOT READY, because of the first item)

1. **The section after the hurt note can harm the reader the note just protected.** The note is good. It says "What they do is theirs; nothing in your rage is responsible for them." Then the very next section opens with "Everything before that note stays true at the worst end. So bring the worst people back to mind, the ones whose harm you can't wave off, because the general case is easy to nod at and theirs is not."
   - For someone who was abused, the worst person is the abuser, so you have just called that face back.
   - Next they read "You think your contempt is punishing what they became. Aimed at anyone within its reach, it's one more push in the same direction."
   - Then they read "Adding the verdict is allying with his captor, because the urge to do it comes from yours."

   As written, a survivor is told that their rage isn't responsible for the abuser, and then, a page later, that their verdict pushes the abuser further and sides with what's holding him. The phrase "within its reach" carries the whole distinction, and nobody in pain will catch it. Here's what I'd do:
   - Move the note to after the hostage passage, so it's the last word before the locker room.
   - Make "bring the worst people back to mind" point at people the reader knows of, not people who hurt them. Or give Chapter 1's out again.
   - Say plainly that if you've cut contact, he isn't within reach.

2. **The choice argument contradicts itself in adjacent paragraphs.** First: "Try it: hold someone's eyes and decide, while holding them, to stop seeing them. See if you can find the moment. I can't." Then, a few lines later: "While you can still feel both pulls—the recognition, and the want the recognition would veto—you're still looking, and you can keep looking or stop. That's the choice." Then: "That's why, looking back, it feels like you chose it knowing better, and you did".
   - So you can't choose to stop seeing while looking, and you can choose to stop looking while looking, and you did choose it knowing better.

## READY?

**Preface and Introduction: READY.** No major objections.

**Chapter 1: READY.** No major objections. This is the best chapter. The Encounter is honest in a way I rarely see; the cutting-in-line paragraph and the sigh to the people you love are true and I'd teach them. Don't let anyone touch it except for the two or three echoes listed under Redundancy.

**Chapter 2: CLOSE.**

1. *The claims list turns transmission into doctrine.* After the face exercise, the reader is looking at a face. Then: "Here are the claims, stated flat." Nine bolded theses follow, most restating what the reader just found, and one ("Nobody chooses evil under that name.") argues in 155 words what Chapter 3 then argues properly. For a sangha this is the worst place to start preaching, because they will have just had the experience. Keep "Caring is not knowing what to do." and "No amount of turning away ever produces a turning back." as the chapter's last two paragraphs, unbolded. Cut the rest. Two callbacks in Chapter 4 point at it ("Chapter 2 claimed there is one guide" and "That's the claim from Chapter 2 in the flesh"); restate the first in place and drop the second clause.

2. *The stone arch contradicts the paragraph it sets up.* The check says "The tree has no conscience and the arch has no inside." The next paragraph concludes from it: "When I look most deeply into what I am, I find life." An arch isn't alive. If the arch passes the check, the conclusion can't be "life"; if it fails, it shouldn't be in the check. I'd drop the arch and keep the tree. A careful reader will catch this, and it's the one place in the book the metaphysics shows its seams.

**Chapter 3: CLOSE.**

1. *The book gives three causes for the turn after saying it can't give one.* "I cannot give an account of why the first turn happens" is honest and, for Mahamudra readers, right. Two paragraphs later: "You only ever turn away from someone because you suspect, below words, that care might *not* be what you are." That's a cause, with "only ever" on it. Earlier the cause was a want ("The want was innocent; the label was borrowed."), and in Chapter 1 it was a fear: "I'm afraid of what I already know: that I'm doing what they do." These can be reconciled (first turn unexplained; the loop kept going by suspicion), but the page doesn't reconcile them. One clause would: say that once the turn has happened, this is what keeps it going. Drop "only ever".

2. *A hurt reader is sent straight back to the worst people.* The note for people who were hurt is careful and good. It tells them "nothing here demands that you turn toward them at all." Then the very next section opens: "So bring the worst people back to mind, the ones whose harm you can't wave off". For someone abused, that is their abuser, and what follows calls that person "a hostage held up to the window" who needs "the most love". Chapter 1's buttons had a carve-out; this needs the same one sentence.

3. *The factory-farm line.* "Think of the worker on a factory-farm line." It arrives in one sentence, in a paragraph about murderers and the far end, with nothing around it. It reads as a jab, and it puts a low-wage worker in that company without a word of care. Develop it or cut it. I'd cut it.

4. *Appendix 3.* Three pointers send the reader there ("Appendix 3 has both.", "Appendix 3 says what.", and in Chapter 4 "Appendix 3 names them"). If the appendix isn't going out with this, they're dead ends, and the second one dangles a promise ("The traditions put something with no motive before the fear") the reader can't collect.

**Chapter 4: CLOSE.**

1. *The relief-or-robbed check contradicts the hurt note.* The note says: "Accountability is what you owe them, all of it, with force if it takes force." Then Chapter 4's central check: "if the harm simply stopped tomorrow, with nobody punished and nobody exposed, would you feel relief, or robbed?" Exposure and punishment are much of what accountability is. A survivor who would feel robbed if her abuser were never held to account is being told that's the parasite. Chapter 1's version didn't have this problem, because the pill changes the heart. Fix the wording so the check separates the two: the harm stops and they're held to account, but nobody gets to watch them suffer. Then it's clean, and it's still the most useful check in the book. (It was for me.)

2. *The leaders check invites the verdict it warns against.* "Compare that with how we usually judge the people we hand power to." You hedge it well, then end with: "But a person aimed away from it *isn't even trying* to make things better, however good the proof looks." Every reader will run this on the politician they already despise, whatever the hedge says. It's a judgement of a whole person's orientation, handed out in a chapter about not doing that. Cut the paragraph. The book loses nothing it needs.

3. *It ends too many times.* After "So here is the measure: *this one is like me.*" come "Turning toward has no target", "You don't get to choose", "The only way to end it", "Every act of integrity", "This is what the traditions were for", "There is a battle", "Out there". Each is a closing cadence. A serious reader feels the book reluctant to stop, and the last page loses force. See the cuts below.

**The Picture I Use: CLOSE.**

1. *The last paragraph undoes the opening note.* The note says: "None of the checks in this book depend on it, and if it doesn't fit, leave it". The section then ends: "Every objection I've raised to this picture has had the form *fine, but maybe in my case*." And: "the finding is the only check there is." For a reader with doubts about the Devil or wetiko, this says objections to the picture turn out to be self-deception. Cut the paragraph, or move it to the end of Chapter 4, where it's about contempt and fits. Then the Picture ends on "You find it in the turn in your own eyes", which is the right last line.

The rest of the Picture I'd send as is. The wetiko paragraph is careful about whose word it is, and the marking at the top is exactly what a skeptic needs.
