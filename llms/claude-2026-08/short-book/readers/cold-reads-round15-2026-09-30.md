## Kofi (45, ER physician, Methodist; runs cold at work, sighs at home)

### Stage 1

Aditya,

I read it Sunday night after choir, then again Monday after a shift, which turned out to be the better test. Here's what happened, in order.

**The opening.** The epigraph made me blink. I'm a Methodist; I know the Gospel of Philip is from Nag Hammadi, not the Bible, and part of me braced for a pitch for secret knowledge. That passed quickly. Your first paragraph is a big claim, but then you wrote "Maybe you'll recognize something of yourself in it. Or maybe not." That bought you a lot of room with me. People who've never worked a night shift don't usually leave the door open like that.

The central claim, "Contempt is the claim that you're a different kind of thing than me, but for it to land, you must be the same kind of thing," I held tentatively, as you asked. It sounded clever at first, which makes me suspicious. By the end it didn't sound clever anymore. It sounded like a description of my job.

**The memory.** I had one immediately, and I didn't enjoy having it. Six years ago, a man named Terrell came in with back pain, his fourth visit that month. I'd already filed him before I walked in. I wrote "drug-seeking behavior" in the note, gave him a lidocaine patch, and discharged him with the flat courtesy I'm good at. Loretta, the charge nurse, asked me on his way out, "You want another temp on him?" I said, "He's fine." He came back three days later with an epidural abscess. He walks with a cane now.

Did the knowledge feel like new information when I finally owned it? No. You're right about that, and it's the part of your chapter I'd sign my name to. When I owned it, at M&M and later in therapy, it felt like opening a drawer I'd been standing in front of the whole time. "It was right there to see, fully available to consciousness, but you were *turned away* from it." Yes. The low-grade fever was in the vitals. I read the vitals. I didn't let myself read them.

Did I deflect when someone came close? Loretta came close, and I deflected in two words. Did some part of me know I was deflecting? Yes. I remember the tiny hurry in my voice.

I liked that you wrote "If that doesn't match anything in your memory, the rest of this won't make sense, and I'd rather you know that now." That's a claim that says where it stops. That's what opens me.

**Where I pushed back.** "Did that knowledge arrive before or after you'd reasoned it through?" In my case, it's complicated in a way you might not expect. The fast, wordless knowing that came before reasoning was *also* the contempt. "Drug-seeker" arrived in me before any reasoning did, within two seconds of seeing his name on the board. What had to argue was the other side: the fever, the fourth visit in a month, the fact that nobody fakes back pain that consistently. So when you ask "Why did one side have to argue, and the other not? And which side did you listen to?", the question has its answer built in, and in my case the answer is backwards. In medicine we call the fast thing gestalt, and gestalt is sometimes the conscience and sometimes the prejudice. I kept going, but I noticed you assumed the quiet side is always the good one.

**Better nature.** "In fact, I *can't*, when I can clearly see myself in them." Tentatively bought. "Can't" is strong. But I thought about whether I could have sneered at Terrell if I'd really looked at him, and I don't think I could have. That's why I didn't look.

**The cashier.** I did it Monday at the Kroger on Ponce, with the older woman who wears her reading glasses on a chain. The smile went without a reason. The sneer wouldn't even rehearse without one. I had to invent "she's slow" before it would move. So the check worked. But I'd add something from 4 a.m. in my department. The flat voice comes out of me without any reason *in the moment*, because the reason was filed hours or years earlier. "Frequent flyer" is a reason I carry pre-loaded. So "Did either one need a reason before it would go?" is true, but the reason can be stored, and then it doesn't feel like one.

**Remorse.** "I've felt remorse for harm I've done, but never for having been decent." Checked it. I've been burned by kindness: a patient I believed, whose prescription I later heard he sold. I regretted it. I didn't feel I'd gone against myself. Bought. "Can you imagine it even in the worst person?" I can't, but that's my imagination, not evidence, and you didn't claim more.

"Under the right circumstances, I'm capable of the worst acts humans have committed." My profession proves this; I don't need convincing. Physicians ran the selections at Auschwitz, and physicians in Alabama watched men go untreated for forty years at Tuskegee. Not monsters. Doctors. That line is easy for me.

**The Encounter.** Believable, yes. My version is the boyfriend who walks in behind a woman with a split lip and does the talking for her. I've given that man the look you describe. "I want him to feel the full weight of it, almost as a kind of punishment." That's accurate.

The line that stopped me was "He can't prove I'm doing this, can he? No. And crucially, *neither can I*. So I know it's not on purpose." That is how I chart. "Patient discharged in stable condition. Return precautions given." Nobody can audit the voice I said it in, including me. I read that line three times.

I skimmed the "invitation" paragraph and most of the "net benefit to the world" paragraph. They were true, but I'd got the point by then.

**The cold version.** This is where you caught me. "I just write the person off: a quiet filing-away, a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them." That's me. The hot version is my Saturday nights. The cold version is every shift. The paragraph is short, and I wanted it earlier, or longer. When I read "they feel it just the same," I thought of the times patients have said to me, very calmly, "You already decided about me." They're right.

**The Corruption.** "If this is the kind of world I live in—one where people can lie to themselves and get away with it—then why the fuck shouldn't I?" Tentatively bought, and I have evidence you didn't use. My patients who've been labeled drug-seeking arrive the next time already braced and combative. That gets read as confirmation and charted as "hostile," and the next doctor reads the chart. There's a study in the Journal of General Internal Medicine, around 2018, showing that stigmatizing language in a chart shifts the next physician's attitude and even their pain prescribing. The corruption passes from mind to mind in writing. You'd like that paper.

**The two checks.** Is there something satisfying about seeing evil punished? Yes, obviously. My worst enemy: Brent Cahill, the administrator who cut two nursing positions from our ED. The pill or the vengeance? My first honest thought was that I'd like him to work one night shift short two nurses. Then I noticed I'd dressed up vengeance as enlightenment. Some part of me wants to press the first button, and it wants to watch. "I'll be honest: there is in me." That sentence did more than anything else in the section, because you went first.

"And that is exactly how I act on it, without seeing that I am." I read that twice and I'm still not sure I have it.

A small handling: "Forget about the 'right' or 'decent' answers." I'd forgotten them already; being told to made me feel watched.

**"I'll go ahead and name what's happening."** Mostly insight. The first two paragraphs I followed. "Normally, having my enemy see my self-deception would be horrible. In this case, it is ideal" I had to read twice. Then "I am in limbo about provoking him, and showing him that I *am* in limbo is itself the provocation" clicked. It explains why patients escalate at discharge. What makes them shout isn't the refusal. It's that they can see I've decided and can see I'll never admit it. "Nothing that can be audited, *even by myself*." Yes.

The next section started to feel like a lecture. "For me to do this, I must be present enough to plant the evidence..." is the third time you've explained the can't-prove-it mechanism. Then came the first place I **refused**: "it acts as if it has goals of its own, and it pursues them *intentionally.*" and "So the only thing that benefits is *it*." As a physician I'd call it a reinforcing loop, a habit that pays itself. You've given it purpose, and you haven't shown me purpose. I could still follow what came after.

Here's the odd thing, though. The choir singer in me heard Romans 7 underneath it: "Now if I do that I would not, it is no more I that do it, but sin that dwelleth in me." Paul made your move two thousand years ago, and like you he doesn't let himself off the hook. So I refuse it as evidence and grant it as a picture I already sang on Sunday. You write "None of this lets me off the hook, though. It's my mind, and I answer for what it does," and that's the line that kept me reading.

The paragraph that got under my skin was "In me it's always an accident; something I had 'no idea' I was doing. In them, it's done with 'full awareness.'" My mistakes are fatigue, system failure, crowding. The frequent flyer "knows exactly what he's doing." I have said those words in the break room.

**"Here's my claim: our better nature isn't one part among others. It's what we most fundamentally are."** Granted as your claim, partly found. It didn't sound grandiose to me. I was raised on the image of God, and this is close to that. But the evidence you offer, that I say I hide things from "myself," felt like wordplay. "I've hidden good things too, from the part of me that would call them weakness" I read three times and couldn't place. What moved me was the last question: "Did it feel like losing something of yourself, or like getting something back?" Honestly, both. I lost the doctor I thought I was. But something came back that let me sleep. It was the second one that I'd call me. So by the end I'd found about half of it, which is more than I expected.

**The corrector.** Loretta again, years before Terrell, when I was a second-year resident. She caught me in the med room and said, "You didn't look at that man. Go back in and look at him." She was fiercer than any attending I had. Did I dig in? For about thirty seconds. Then I went back in. "It feels like it's reminding me of something—that *you're better than this*." Yes. That's what she did. I should have remembered her six years ago.

"The fierceness stays; only the poison goes." This matters to me more than you'd guess. Some of my frequent flyers *are* seeking drugs, and I will still say no. Earlier I worried the book would ask me to be sentimental about patients, and this sentence told me it won't. I can still say no. What goes is the verdict.

**Common knowledge.** I followed the gist, not the levels. The paragraph about each level closing a way out I read twice and stopped. "Don't worry about tracing the logic" was a relief, and honest. The hall of mirrors I know. A patient says, "You think I'm a junkie," and I say, "Sir, I treat every patient the same." We both know what's happening, and he's the one who sounds crazy for naming it. That's exactly it.

"the faculty I'm seeing *with*... is the very thing I'm seeing *in* them" lost me. It sounds like a proof trick even if it isn't one.

The dehumanizing gaze: I've had one. A patient's son once asked the nurse, in front of me, for "an American doctor." Was he absent, turned away from himself? Something like that. His eyes didn't settle anywhere. But "Why would he have to turn away, if what he is, is essentially bad?" is a question with its answer inside it. And "Part of us already knows this" told me what I know before I'd checked.

"hypocrisy only means something between two people who answer to the same thing." That's the best sentence in the section, and I'd keep it if you cut the rest. It did more for me than the eyes-gazing-into-eyes.

"If seeing this makes you want to hide, notice that's the same verdict, pointed inward. It feels like accountability. It isn't." After the patient death that sent me to the burnout program, I spent a year calling self-contempt accountability. My therapist took months to get me to see that. You did it in three sentences. Put this where a reader like me will hit it before he starts hiding, not after.

I winced at "Believing I'm different and special is inherently self-deceptive" and "it always leaks." Always is a word I need evidence for. The next paragraph, "Whether that number ever goes to zero is something you can discover for yourself," won me back.

**Other kinds.** This caught me hardest, and it's nearly at the end. "The stupid, the slow and the weak get the same verdict, with better cover: *I'm only being accurate.*" That's triage. Then: "The *what's wrong with you?* that needs them to know they're broken. The sigh..." and "The look on their face that tells me it landed, which I pretend not to have seen, leaving them nothing to accuse me of." My son Kwame is fifteen. He's started answering me in one word. My wife Abena says I bring the triage voice home. I've never said "what's wrong with you" to him out loud. I've sighed it. He has nothing to accuse me of, so he says "fine" and goes upstairs. I stopped reading for a while here.

"It has the same flavor as what we call evil" made me wince. Maybe it's true. It's too big for a sigh on the reader's first pass.

**Self-serving.** "The private equity firm that buys nursing homes and calls the staff cuts efficiency." That's Brent, near enough. You chose my enemy for me. Then: "When I'm sure someone knew exactly what they were doing, I've made them a villain. When I'm sure they had no idea, I've made them a machine." I've done both to Brent, on alternate days. I've done both to my frequent flyers too. I don't have a third way to hold him yet, but I see why I'd need one.

"I suspect each knows" is fine because it's marked. "One that part of us always knows" isn't, because it's about everyone.

**The ending.** "This is why, if I want to end theirs, I have to see how to end mine." It's an honest ending, but the chapter's real climax for me was two sections earlier, in the sigh. The self-serving section feels like it's opening a new subject as the chapter closes. I wouldn't mind if it ended there, but I'd have ended it on the sigh.

**Length.** It drags in the middle. The can't-prove-it mechanism is explained in the Encounter, again in "I'll go ahead and name," again in "Conscious but unaware," and again in Common knowledge. "Better kind of thing" appears too many times. If I had to cut a fifth: the invitation paragraph, "Here's another way to look at it," most of the "it acts as if it has goals" material, the faculty-seeing-with paragraph, "Believing I'm different and special," and the "I saw *genuine darkness*" section, which restates what came before.

**Words.** "Limbo" works, though to a Christian it's a Catholic place, not a state, and I kept hearing that. "Crucially" and "here's the thing" inside the monologue are fine; they sound like a mind talking itself into something. "Double down" felt lazy. "Forsake" was a good word.

The one thing I missed: you own the mechanism beautifully, but I never learned what you actually did. The enemy is "someone who's just been cruel to an innocent person." Who? When? The sigh section is the closest you come to a real occasion, and it's the section that worked best. I'd trust the rest more if I knew your Terrell.

**Last.** Was it obvious? The limbo part, yes. Therapy taught me knowing-and-not-knowing. What became newly consequential is the idea that the unprovability *is* the provocation, and that the flat courtesy at work and the sigh at home are the same act. That changes this week. Wednesday I'm off, and Kwame has a game. I'm going to watch for the sigh. I'm going to notice when his face tells me it landed, and not pretend I didn't see it. At work, the next time "drug-seeker" arrives before I've walked in, I'm going to go back in and look at him. Loretta's words, not yours, but you reminded me.

Would I pass it on? Yes, to Marcus Bell, who was in the burnout program with me and runs a night shift at Grady. I'd tell him to read it through the cold-version paragraph, skim the middle, and slow down at "Other kinds." I wouldn't give it to our residents yet. It's too long for them, and the middle would lose them. I wouldn't give it to Brent. I noticed that, too.

### Stage 2

Aditya,

I read the second file expecting a different chapter. It's the same chapter with about six sentences changed. I had to put them side by side to find them all. That's a finding in itself, so I'll start there.

**Which did more, which treated me better.** The chapter did more to me, but that's partly because I read it first, on a night when Terrell and Kwame were both on my mind. On treatment, it isn't close. Every place the second file differs, it's a little more sure of itself, and a little more sure of me. Every place the chapter differs, it's the author speaking from his own experience and stopping there. With me, that difference decides whether I stay in the room.

Here they are, in the order I hit them.

**"Part of" versus "exactly."** The chapter says "So I didn't look—and that refusal is part of what caused it." The second file says "So I didn't look—and that refusal is exactly what caused it." I work in a field where nothing has one cause. A man doesn't harden only because of my look. He also has the last four doctors, his back, his landlord, the chart. "Part of" is something I can check against my own cases, and I did. "Exactly" asks me to take your word for it. The chapter is right.

**The "enough and necessary" sentence.** The second file adds "That combination is both enough to produce the outcome I need, and also necessary." This was the paragraph I had to read twice in the chapter, at "In this case, it is ideal." I hoped the added sentence would help. It didn't. It's a sufficiency-and-necessity claim, a logic-textbook sentence, and you give no evidence for either half. The two sentences after it, about pity and overt malice, do the actual work. The chapter was right to leave it out.

**"The hallmark of evil."** The second file ends the unprovable-message paragraph with "He sees that he cannot appeal to my better nature, because I'm convinced this *is* my better nature—and that's a condition that part of me knows he will recognize as the hallmark of evil." The chapter stops at "because I'm convinced this *is* my better nature."

This is the one addition I'd think about recovering, and I'm of two minds. It told me something the chapter didn't: *why* the man escalates. It isn't only that he can't appeal to me. It's that a person who's sure his harm is his goodness is the most frightening thing he can meet. I know this from the patient's side. The doctor who's sure his dismissal is professionalism is the most dangerous doctor in the building, and patients sense it. Isaiah has a line about it: "Woe unto them that call evil good, and good evil." So the idea earns its place.

But "the hallmark of evil" is a big phrase to set on a look, and "part of me knows he will recognize" is you telling me what's in two minds at once. When I read it in the second file, I flinched a little before I agreed. If you keep it, I'd want it smaller. As it stands, I'd lean toward the chapter, and bring the idea in somewhere it can be shown, not announced.

**"Whenever I've managed" versus "Take it out."** This is the biggest difference for me, and the chapter is right. The chapter says "Whenever I've managed to take it out, I haven't missed it." The second file says "Take it out and nothing is lost."

The chapter's sentence is testimony. It's bounded by "whenever I've managed," which admits that often you haven't. It's your own experience, and it tells me where the claim stops. That's the kind of sentence that opens me. The second file's version is a law about everyone, and I'd immediately want to argue. Nothing is lost? Sometimes the contempt is what made the man back off in the waiting room, and I'd want to talk about that before I agreed. With your sentence, I didn't need to. You'd already said it was your finding. I went to Loretta and checked it against her, and it held. Keep the chapter's version.

**"This is what you were sensing."** The second file adds it after the hall of mirrors. It's the one place in either version where I'd put the book down for a minute, annoyed. It's being told what I feel. I'd just had the hall of mirrors come alive with my own patient, "You think I'm a junkie." The recognition was mine. That sentence took it from me and handed it back labeled. The chapter was right to leave it out.

**"Then I judge that darkness from afar."** The chapter has "Then I judge that darkness from afar, as if I'd had no part in it, and that lets me get away with more." The second file cuts it. I didn't mention it in my first letter, but seeing it gone, I missed it. That sentence is my break room. I rail against Brent's staffing cuts with the other attendings. In the same week I've discharged a patient faster than I should have because I wanted the bed, and called that efficiency too. "From afar" is exactly where I stand when I judge Brent. Without it, the second file's ending jumps from "a very convincing story of how what it's doing is good" straight to "if I want to end theirs." The chapter's version gets you there. Keep it.

**What the second file has that the chapter should recover.** Honestly, only the idea inside "the hallmark of evil," and not in that form. Nothing else in the second file is something I'd want back.

**What the chapter was right to leave out or change.** "Exactly," "enough and necessary," "Take it out and nothing is lost," and "This is what you were sensing." All four are the same move. Each one turns something you found in yourself into something I'm told is true of me. My first letter said what opens me is a claim that says where it stops. The chapter stops. The second file keeps going.

**Order and length.** The comparison showed me nothing about order, since the order is identical. On length, it confirmed what I said before. The second file's two added sentences are in the "I'll go ahead and name" section, which is already the densest part and the part that repeats the can't-prove-it mechanism. Adding there makes it worse. The one sentence the chapter has that the second file lacks is at the end, where the chapter is thin. So the chapter is weighted better too. My fifth to cut is still the same: the invitation paragraph, "Here's another way to look at it," the "it acts as if it has goals" material, and the "I saw *genuine darkness*" section. The trims belong in the middle, not the end.

One more thing the comparison taught me. Every change between these two versions is about how sure to sound. That's the real editorial question in this chapter, more than any argument. For a reader like me, the less sure version earns more belief, not less.

Kofi

## Linda (67, retired Lutheran church secretary; runs cold, sure she has no contempt)

### Stage 1

Dear Aditya,

I'm not a book person the way you probably mean it. I read mysteries, and Kate Bowler, and whatever devotional Pastor Dahl hands out in Advent. So take this for what it is: a retired church secretary from Fargo going through your chapter at the kitchen table with a cup of coffee, trying to do what you asked.

The first thing I'll tell you is that I nearly put it down at the top. I was a church secretary for thirty-one years and I know the Gospel of Philip isn't in my Bible. It's one of the ones that got passed around after that Da Vinci Code business. I don't hold it against you, but it made me brace a little. I thought, oh, this is going to be one of those books.

Then your first paragraph ended with "Maybe you'll recognize something of yourself in it. Or maybe not." That let me breathe. You weren't telling me what I'd find.

Your central claim, "Contempt is the claim that you're a different kind of thing than me, but for it to land, you must be the same kind of thing," I read twice. I understood it the second time. I held it the way you asked, at arm's length, and I'll be honest that I held it there because I didn't think it had anything to do with me. I have never raised my voice at anyone in my life. I don't have contempt for people. That's what I thought going in.

THE MEMORY

You asked for something I knew was wrong and couldn't admit until later. It came right away, which surprised me. My mother's last year at the care center in Moorhead, I went every other Sunday when I could have gone twice a week. I told myself the roads were bad and Harold needed me and she didn't always know who I was anyway. When my sister Joanne said "Mom was asking for you," I'd change the subject to her grandkids. I didn't let myself know it until after the funeral, sitting in the car in the church lot.

Your question, "did it feel like new information? Or like something you had always known, but somehow hadn't *let* yourself know?" Always known. No question. So I bought your first claim, the limbo. And yes, I deflected every time Joanne came near it, and yes, some part of me knew I was doing it. "Everyone does it. It's not a big deal. Just this once." I didn't say those exact words to myself, but mine were "She won't remember anyway," which is the same thing wearing a church dress.

"Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" That stopped me. I'd never thought of it that way. The part that knew never said a word. It just sat there.

THE CASHIER AND THE REMORSE

I pictured the young woman at Hornbacher's with the nose ring, who always calls me "hon." The smile went out easy. The sneer I couldn't even make myself picture doing. So you're right that one needed nothing and the other would have needed a reason, and I didn't have one.

The remorse question I bought. I've regretted lending my nephew money. I have never had remorse for being decent to anybody. I sat with that one a while.

Then you called the part that sees yourself in others "better nature." I bought that tentatively. I'm Lutheran all my life, and we're taught we're sinners through and through, so I kept a finger in that page, you might say.

THE CONFRONTATION

Here's where I first refused. The man in your scene is angry and fierce, and "I would defy a message like that too, and violently." I would not. I would go quiet and be very, very polite. I kept thinking, well, this is a man, this is somebody's temper, this isn't me. I could follow it. I believed it as what a mind like that does. It's just not my mind.

And the swearing. I'm sorry, I know it's meant to sound like a real person thinking, but "dammit," and then "Shit," and then "make damn sure," and then later that line with the f-word in it. Each one I felt myself pull back a little. By the f-word I was skimming. I skimmed most of the middle of the encounter, from "I sense something underhanded" down to "Classic projection."

But then you wrote this, and I have to tell you it's the reason I kept reading:

"That's the hot version. Contempt often runs cold. I just write the person off: a quiet filing-away, a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them. It's the same verdict, delivered by withdrawal, and they feel it just the same."

"A politeness with nothing behind it." I put the pages down. My son Eric said to me last Christmas, in the kitchen, "Mom, she can feel it, you know." He meant Brooke, his wife. I told him I had no idea what he was talking about, I'm nothing but nice to that girl. And here you are telling me "they feel it just the same." I didn't like reading it. But I did not think you were calling me a bad person, because you'd just spent two pages on yourself doing something worse.

So I went back and read the encounter again with Brooke in mind, like you said to. It worked better the second time. Not the swearing. The part about how "it's really more like an *invitation*" and then "there's nothing inviting about my tone or gaze. Nothing that signals he'd be welcome back." I have told myself I'm keeping the door open for Brooke. I'm not sure there's anything in my face that says welcome.

THE WORST ENEMY

You asked me to forget the decent answer. My worst enemy is Brooke, and I'm embarrassed to write that about my own daughter-in-law. She keeps the grandkids from me, or I believe she does, and she rolled her eyes at Harold's funeral lunch. I saw it.

The vengeance or the pill. I don't want her hurt. But I'll tell you what I do want. I want Eric to see what she is. I want her to be found out. When I read "Something in me craves *punishment*, over and above any justice it might serve. I tell myself it's innocuous, as long as I don't act on it. And that is exactly how I act on it, without seeing that I am," I thought about how I tell Joanne every Sunday on the phone what Brooke did this week. I've never acted on it. That's what I would have said yesterday.

It helped that you said "I'll be honest: there is in me." If you hadn't said that first I don't think I'd have answered at all.

"Enlighten" was a word that made me wince a little. It's not a word we use. I'd have said "come to her senses," or "have a change of heart."

"I'LL GO AHEAD AND NAME WHAT'S HAPPENING"

This is where it turned into a lecture for me, though a lecture with some things in it. I had to read "Normally, having my enemy see my self-deception would be horrible. In this case, it is ideal—because it *is* my provocation." three times and I'm still not sure I have it. "In short: I am in limbo about provoking him, and showing him that I *am* in limbo is itself the provocation" helped some. But by then I was tired.

The line that got through was "He sees that he cannot appeal to my better nature, because I'm convinced this *is* my better nature." I am convinced my niceness to Brooke is my Christian duty. What would she appeal to? I'm being nice.

"Conscious but unaware" I had to read twice. I think I understood it by going back to my mother and the Sundays.

The part about a part of me that "acts as if it has goals of its own" sounded to me like the old Adam, or like the devil, honestly. I don't mind that. We talk about the devil at church without anybody getting nervous. I'm curious what you'll say about it in Chapter 2, and I was glad you said "None of this lets me off the hook, though. It's my mind, and I answer for what it does." A Lutheran wants to hear that.

"OUR BETTER NATURE ... IS WHAT WE MOST FUNDAMENTALLY ARE"

This is the second place I refused, or at least balked. It's a big claim, and it runs against what I was raised on. We're sinners, all the way down; the good in us is God's, not ours. It sounded a little grand, like the kind of thing people say at yoga.

But then you asked me to go back to the memory, and "Did it feel like losing something of yourself, or like getting something back?" Getting something back. That's what confession feels like, and the absolution after. So I'll grant it as your claim. I'm not ready to say it's mine.
