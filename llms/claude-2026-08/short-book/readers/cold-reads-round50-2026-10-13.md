## Hana (Zen teacher, Sanbo Zen; former trial lawyer)

### Stage 1 (the book)

Dear Aditya,

Steady. Not devoured. I read it at a desk with a pencil, which is how I read anything I expect to argue with, but it was never a drag for more than a page or two. My attention went slack in three places: the Introduction, the "What I'm claiming" list at the end of Chapter 2, and the run from the locker room through most of "The Picture I Use." If you weren't a friend, I'd have put it down in the Introduction, at the paragraph that begins "Contempt for our enemies is only coherent if both sender and receiver already know the things this introduction opened with". That is a brief's conclusion filed before the facts. Limbo rescued it within a page.

**What happened, in order**

The Introduction (the book) told me the mind is intrinsically compassionate and evil is a mind turned away from itself. Your preface says this version leans on those, and on the cushion I grant them. But the Introduction then restates the thesis three more times, each time more abstractly. By "An absence that behaves like a presence is hard to hold in mind." I was holding nothing.

Limbo. My case: 1998, my third year at the firm. I missed a disclosure deadline on a products case and let a paralegal, Rosa Delgado, carry it. I didn't lie. I let the partner's assumption stand. I wrote to her thirteen years later. Your question, "did it feel like new information? Or like something you had always known, but somehow hadn't *let* yourself know?", has a clean answer for me: the second. I knew in the elevator. "Did that knowledge arrive before or after you'd reasoned it through?" Before. The reasons came after, and they were good reasons; I was a good lawyer. "Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" is the best single question in the book. Bought outright.

I ran the remorse-over-decency check honestly. In 2006 I advised a client to settle early because I couldn't stomach putting a grieving widow through a deposition. It cost the client money and cost me the client. I've felt bad about it. Your list, "regret that it cost me, anger at being taken advantage of, guilt at breaking a rule I was raised on, or feeling foolish", caught it: regret at the cost, and some foolishness. Not remorse. I bought it grudgingly, because you'd pre-sorted the counterexamples, and a litigator notices when the other side has drafted the exhibit list. It held on inspection anyway.

One sentence in Limbo I refused on sight: "Nobody ever looked honestly at something in themselves while being told they were worthless for having it." I have watched people in AA rooms and in dokusan do it, with that voice going in their own heads. It's usually true. Say usually.

The Two Strangers. The first, bought; "I'd take that bet at any odds. But on what evidence?" is exact, funny, and yours. The second stranger was my first real refusal: "it still wouldn't have triggered the digging-in response." My case is Judge Ann Claiborne, 2004, who called me into chambers over a characterization in my brief that was technically accurate and plainly meant to mislead. She was harder on me than anyone has been since, and never once suggested I was a bad lawyer or a bad person. I dug in. For a day and a half I drafted rebuttals in the shower. Then it fell apart, and I've been grateful to her for twenty years. So the second stranger doesn't prevent the digging in. She gives it nothing to hold, and it collapses on its own. That is a stronger claim than yours, and truer to anyone who has been proud. I could follow after, because nothing later depends on "wouldn't."

The Encounter. My case is my former partner, Glen Marchetti, who padded hours on a matter for three years and, when the client caught it, arranged things so the exposure fell on the partnership. I bought him out in 2017 and I hold him in contempt still. I have done the Encounter to Glen, in a conference room, nearly line for line. "*Shit.* He's onto me." I remember his small smile at that moment. This section works because it's a narrated mind and it's unmistakably you. "Sure, it may be a bit dark, but it's the *good* kind of darkness—the kind that fights the real kind." I laughed, and then stopped.

The buttons: I'll match your honesty. For Glen, some part of me wants the first one. Not much. Enough. And the question about the pleasure being "in the punishment" landed; when he lost a matter before another state's bar I enjoyed it for a week.

The Corruption. Here the book starts telling more than showing, and it's uneven. "The reason I don't have to argue myself into doing the right thing is that in the absence of justifications, it's what I naturally desire." You've earned half of that (harm needs reasons) but not "naturally desire," which is a claim about appetite. The trap paragraph, "Deny it, and you're the kind of person who does. Get angry, and that's proof too.", is the truest account I've read of being on the receiving end of Glen, and of my sister. Then "**Love and contempt are addressed to the same place.**" I checked it and it held. When my sister Teresa said, at our mother's kitchen table, "you've always been like this," it didn't land on my pride. It landed where she used to say "I know you." Same address. That sentence is earned, and it's the hinge of the book.

The Way Out. The torturer paragraph is necessary; it stopped an objection I was forming. The grammar check, "you hid it from *yourself*. Not from a part of you; the part was the one doing the hiding. So what was it hidden from?", is Zen without saying so, and it's where my practice and your book meet. Bought. The hostage image I bought tentatively: it fits some people I've cross-examined and not others.

The Spread found me, in the case I least wanted to use. Teresa and I haven't spoken in four years, since the fight over our mother's last months and the sale of the house in Oak Park. Your later line, "It is possible to leave, cut contact, and never see them again with no verdict in it at all", draws the distinction, and by it I'm on the wrong side. Bought, and it cost something.

Chapter 3. "Try it: hold someone's eyes and decide, while holding them, to stop seeing them. See if you can find the moment." With Glen in imagination I found something: a small *enough* before any story. Which is your next paragraph's "one choice." So it held.

The parasite. The slow-people contempt and the toddler check: excellent. My case is a man in my sitting group who asks every few weeks whether it's all right to count breaths to twenty. I feel it. A child asking the same thing, I don't. Bought.

"The murderer, the abuser, the man in the news: each is this one step, taken thousands of times. You think your contempt is punishing what they became. It's one more push in the same direction." The note for people who were hurt: mostly right, and careful. My worries are below. The locker room is true to life, but "Nobody retains a record of what just happened." is false. Someone always does, usually the one it was about.

- "How much innocence you can see in others measures your own freedom more than their goodness." The caveat right after it ("That isn't a reason to doubt what you see when someone is dangerous") is the only thing standing between this sentence and a survivor doubting her own perception. Keep the caveat, and keep it adjacent.

## What's wrong, overclaimed, or could harm

I accompany survivors, many of them of abuse inside the Church. So this is the part I care most about, and I'll be plain.

**1. "Somebody is in there, and nobody is choosing."** The body of Chapter 3 is careful: there is a choice, made while both pulls can be felt, and everyone is responsible. But the heading in the claims list says the opposite in bold, and the explanation under it says "the judgement that *someone in there is choosing this* is aimed at a person who, for that moment, isn't steering anything." A survivor of grooming knows that her abuser chose. He chose for months. He chose which child, which car, which lie to her mother. If she reads "nobody is choosing", she will hear what she heard from the chancery: he couldn't help it. This heading must go. Put the Chapter 3 version in its place: there is one choice, it's real, and it's made over and over.

**2. "It aims at the sickness in a person, never at the person"** (Ch. 4). In the 1980s and 90s bishops used exactly this word. The priest was sick; he went for treatment; he came back to a new parish. Maggie's abuser was "sick" twice. I understand what you mean. Your group may not have this history; some of their readers will. Find another word.

**3. The hostage.** "a hostage held up to the window, not a monster on display" (the book, The Way Out) and "Being unkind to him is allying with his captor, because the urge to do it comes from yours" (Ch. 3). For a survivor, "hostage" makes her abuser into a victim, and asks her to feel for him the thing she has been asked to feel for him all her life. "the knowing doesn't shrink" is true and you can keep it. The hostage turns it into a plea. And "unkind" in that sentence will be read as "harsh," which is the opposite of what you mean everywhere else.

**4. Responsibility handed to the person who was hurt.** "The verdict is the one thing you don't owe them, and it's the one thing their parasite needs from you." And "It doesn't come from the hurt. It comes from the same darkness that hurt you." I believe the second is true as a matter of spiritual direction, and I have said versions of it, carefully, to people after years. But to a woman reading alone, "the same darkness that hurt you" puts her in the same sentence as him. And "the one thing their parasite needs from you" tells her she is feeding him. Write these as your own finding, about your own anger, and let her draw the line herself.

**5. Limbo applied to the wrong person.** The book trains the reader to say, of her own wrong, *I knew and didn't let myself know.* A survivor will turn that on her own abuse: *I knew, so I let it happen.* Children do not "half-know" in that sense; they are overpowered and confused, and the confusion is done to them. One sentence in Limbo, saying this is about wrongs you did and not wrongs done to you, would prevent real harm.

**6. Spiritual abuse uses this vocabulary.** *Fierce correction without a verdict*, *staying turned toward*, *I'm doing this out of love*: the men who hurt people in religious communities said these things. Chapter 4 says "you can tell which is which the same way you could tell with the second stranger." The people I accompany are precisely the ones whose telling was taken from them. They were told the hand on them was love. The book should know this and say once that the inner recognition can be hijacked, and that a person who was groomed is not failing if she can't trust it yet.

**7. Where the hurt note sits.** It is in Chapter 3. The first enemy exercise ("Bring to mind your worst enemy") is in Chapter 1, and the face exercise ("Now put your enemy's face there instead") in Chapter 2. For a survivor, putting the abuser's face there, in imagination, deliberately, is not a neutral exercise. You warn about the mushroom exercise in the footnote, "not for a day when you're already being hard on yourself, or for anyone in a fragile place". The face exercise needs the same. One sentence: if your enemy is someone who abused you, choose someone else for this; you can come back to him later, or never.

**8. "They started where you are, and nothing about you guarantees you'd have turned out differently."** (Ch. 3). I believe this; Teresa of Ávila believed it of herself. But the sentence before it, "The murderer and the abuser didn't start there.", read by the abused, says she might have been him. Keep the thought for the reader who has never been hurt that way. For the one who has, it needs one more sentence, or a different example than "the abuser."

**9. "How much innocence you can see in others measures your own freedom more than their goodness."** (Ch. 4). You add that seeing danger is sight. Still, a survivor who cannot see innocence in her abuser will read herself as unfree. In my experience she is often freer than the rest of us. Rewrite it as yours, *in my own case*.

**10. Wetiko.** You say you know it only through Forbes and Levy, which is honest. But you then say it "names what this book's parasite does", and that's taking a Cree word into a book on Buddhist and Christian terms. I'd keep it, and say once that the Cree term carries more than this, and that you are borrowing.

Two smaller things. "Nobody retains a record of what just happened" (Ch. 3, locker room): the men remember; what they lose is the willingness to say it. And "Nice is worse than useless against evil" (Ch. 4) is a good sentence that I'd keep; just know a survivor may hear it as a rebuke to the gentleness she survived by.

Those last points aren't a reason not to send it. Your book is right that contempt is where most of us hide, and I found that in myself with Derek and with Whelan in a way I didn't expect. The book is better than it is long. It's also more dangerous than it knows, in the specific places above.

With respect, and some gratitude,
Teresa

## Where the revision hurt

**The admission of intent.** This is the one I'd fight for. The earlier version: "As best I can tell, "pure evil" is this error turned up to maximum intensity. It looks intentional, just as mine did, because in an important sense, it is." The book: "I think "pure evil" is this error at maximum intensity. **The perception.** The earlier version: "I'd call it sacred, except the word turns it into an idea, and what I mean is the perception you just had: a face, looked at until someone looked back." **The title anecdote.** The earlier version: "This is the sense in which contempt is evil." The book: "It's why this book was originally titled *Contempt Is Evil*." The earlier one says the thing. The new one is about the book.

**What's new.** The earlier version had a paragraph the book cut entirely: "This gives the Buddhist account of ignorance an interpersonal form. **The home paragraph.** The earlier version: "So as long as you don't feel at home, you can't afford to let anyone else get there. **Anti-life.** The earlier version: "It is life turned against itself, eating itself, and calling the meal good." The book: "It is anti-life: life turned against itself. That's why there's no bottom to it, since nothing it eats feeds anything." "calling the meal good" is the thesis of the whole book in four words: the darkness wearing goodness. The revision swapped it for a fourth "no bottom." Restore the earlier.

**New repetitions.** The revision added "There's no bottom to what can be done to someone who doesn't count." **The orientation, mixed.** The earlier version had a creed you could hear: "*Serving life is good. This man is not. So hating him is good.* That's what I would have said I was doing to my enemy.
