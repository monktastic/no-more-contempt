## 2. INSIGHTS the appendix has that Chapter 1 doesn't give me

Starred: the five I'd fight hardest for.

**Italic opening**
- The method: “Not a proof; the inference that the simplest explanation of what you found is the one these assumptions give.” Chapter 1 never says what kind of inference it is making. This does.

**1. The trap**
- Seeing him as a different kind of thing is not a perception that comes with the turn. It is the turn: “So seeing him as a different kind of thing isn't a perception. It's the turn itself.” Chapter 1 has the weaker “I can only believe this while turned away myself.”
- Why the recursion exists at all: “because what's being seen is itself a faculty of seeing, and it sees back.”
- Limbo is normally hidden, and contempt is the one exception: “Contempt is the one place where I want to show it, because it turns him away too, which I will read as evil, making him look like something fundamentally different from me.”
- The look is itself the injury: “His recognizing it is already an injury: someone who can see him has put him out of view on purpose and let him watch.” (Chapter 1 says this of cutting in line, not of the look.)
- “The act's purpose is its own reception and its own concealment: to be read by him and denied by me.”
- The generator: “Everything on it, and anything that could be added, follows from one sentence read by two people who both know what a turned-away look is for: *I know what I'm doing to you; I won't admit it, even to myself; and you can see all of this.*”
- The fixed point: “Every level is the one act, the turn, under one more description. That's a fixed point: the recursion never contains anything but the turn.”
- Why it can't be settled: “He has everything he needs to know and nothing that settles the dispute.”
- The counterfactual target: “a being with nothing to turn from could see that I'm a fraud, but he wouldn't feel it as a betrayal, and the betrayal is what the look needs.”
- ★ Malice against contempt as a conjunction: “But malice as such needs neither of two things contempt needs.” With: “The trap is made by that conjunction and by nothing less.” Chapter 1 only says “Plain malice would frighten you. This maddens, because it won't say what it is.” Here the difference is taken apart: malice needn't be received (the sleeping target), and where it is received it needn't disavow itself.

**What the need was looking for**
- Contempt as the instrument for a need nothing true can meet: “Nothing true can satisfy it, since there is no such difference. So if it's going to be satisfied, something false will have to be made to look true, and contempt is the instrument built for the job.”

- Injury and harm pulled apart: “Reception is the injury; corruption is the harm the injury licenses, and it's how the thing spreads.”
- ★ “Turned toward, I can be as harsh as I like; it may hurt, but it hands him no license. The license travels only through the turn.” This is the second stranger's case turned into a mechanism. Chapter 1 shows it; this says why.
- At the scale of the world: “Then the need looks like perception. I'm not making anyone bad; look at them.”

**The punchline**
- The grading of the five conditions: “The first two come from the encounter alone; the rest lean on what you checked in your own case.”
- “It would put it on the record, and the verdict can't survive being heard.” Chapter 1 has the seed of the next pair (“My claim that I'm a *better kind of thing* is maddening precisely because we both know it's false.”). The new part is that saying it aloud adds no information and still destroys the verdict.
- ★ “Here they arrive as the conditions my own contempt was counting on, in the one act that denies them loudest. I didn't have to believe them. I had to have been using them, and I was.” After twenty years of practice, this is the sentence in the appendix that did something to me. I have heard the view many times. I had never caught myself relying on it in the act of denying it.

**Why it had to be this way**
- “The hiding shows, because the faculty I hide from is the one he reads with.”
- “The seventh is what he can do with it, and the chain forces the offer, not the taking.”
- The rival's predictions, especially: “Harshness without a verdict would be answered as harshness. The clearest-seeing would hold the most contempt.” And what “simplest” means: “That's what the simplest explanation means: not the shortest, but the one where one fact predicts the rest.”

## 4. UNCLEAR

1. **1. The trap**: “Start here: my better nature is what sees myself in others. In particular, it sees theirs.” I reread "theirs" twice. I think it means: it sees their better nature. Plainly: "In particular, it sees their better nature."

2. **1. ## 5. THE ARGUMENT

It mostly holds as what it says it is: an abduction from a strong opening assumption, not a proof. The structural core, the conjunction of reception and disavowal, is good philosophy and, as far as I know, new. Where it fails, it fails at specific steps.

1. **The punchline's unlabelled restatement inflates what was derived.** The labelled conditions are modest: “The care in him had to be there, obscured and not gone”. The unlabelled version says more: “The confusion covering it is not the same thing as it, and doesn't touch it.” "Obscured and not gone" does not give you "doesn't touch it". Likewise “He had to read the move as I would have.” becomes “What's looking out of him is no different in nature from what's looking out of me.” Same reading is not same nature. And “The standard had to be care” becomes “Care is the baseline, what I'm like when nothing is in the way.” The baseline claim comes from "Why it had to be this way", not from the count. The translation into the view is the most moving thing in the appendix, so it matters that each line says only what the trap needed. Otherwise the reader who knows the view will feel the view being smuggled in.

2. **The C/H subtraction is incoherent on one side.** H is defined as hiding C from himself: “the sender is hiding that from himself (call it H)”. Then “Take C away and keep H” describes hiding something that isn't there. You probably mean: the target doesn't share the ground, or the sender doesn't. Say which.

3. **"Honest malice" contradicts the malice paragraph.** "1. The trap" says: “All malice hides one thing from the one doing it: the sameness of the target. This is true even in the sadist.” "Why it had to be this way" says: “Fully aware, I'm looking at him, and the harm won't go; or it's honest malice, which frightens and doesn't madden.” That treats honest malice as fully aware, which the earlier paragraph denies. The fix is easy: honest malice hides the sameness but doesn't disavow the intent. Say that, and the limbo argument still goes through.

4. **"Nothing else can propagate it" outruns its support.** “it will turn out that nothing else can propagate it” and “It's also the only way it spreads.” What's shown is that the *license* needs the turn. It isn't shown that the turned-away state spreads only by license. [^converse] concedes that people turn from each other “to escape grief, or fear”, and honest malice frightens. Why can't fear, from a frank abuser, teach a child to turn away? Chapter 1's child (“A child can't refuse it; he believes it, and carries it for years.”) is a contempt case, but the general claim needs the frightened child too. I'd narrow it to "it's the only way the license spreads".

5. **The remorse asymmetry is shielded by reclassification.** The rival-account paragraph leans on “I've never found remorse running backward.” But Chapter 1, Limbo, has already sorted every candidate into something else: “regret that it cost me, anger at being taken advantage of, guilt at breaking a rule I was raised on, or feeling foolish.” I ran it and found "feeling foolish", so I agree with the finding. But a philosopher will ask what would *count* as backward remorse once those four are excluded. Name the mark: something like "the sense of having gone against *myself*, not against a rule or my interest". Otherwise the test can't fail.

6. **The dissonance rival is stronger than you allow.** “What they don't predict is the trap: that the harm has to be received”. Dissonance research does predict derogating the victim after you harm him, and provoking behaviour that confirms the derogation is a short step from there. Kant's predisposition to good is also universal, so on his account the target has a better nature too. What the rivals lack is not the target's better nature but a reason the harm must be *aimed at* it. Your last sentence says exactly that (“A propensity of the will would have no reason to need its target's better nature.”). Lead with it and drop the broader claim before it.

7. **The clarity test samples on the outcome.** “Take the people others bring their worst to, because they see what's there.” People are brought the worst partly *because* they don't form verdicts, so the group is selected for the result. 8. **Common knowledge "between us" versus "not universality".** The punchline says the conditions were “common knowledge between us before the encounter began.” "What is not claimed" says: “Not universality from this page; O1 to O5 are stated for everyone and open to counterexample, and the deduction needs only your own instance.” My own instance gives me the conditions in me. 9. **"The encounter taught neither of us anything"** sits awkwardly with [^ck]: “The second is made in the encounter: that you're turned away from them now, on purpose.” Both are true of different things. One clause would fix it: "taught neither of us anything about what we are."

**Where the appendix and Chapter 1 disagree:**

**5. THE ARGUMENT**

The core holds, and I think it's original. Contempt has to be received and has to be disavowed; that conjunction needs the target to be your kind; so the act presupposes what it denies. The H-and-C table makes that rigorous. The weaker parts are where the appendix tries to beat rival accounts.

**A step that doesn't follow.** The second bullet of section 1 gets "seeing him as a different kind of thing isn't a perception. It's the turn itself" from the opening assumption. That needs the checks (harm needs the turn, and only harm does), and even with them it needs more: a turned-away person could still perceive something true. Later the appendix offers a test for exactly this claim ("the part that says the verdict is not a perception"). So the thing the test is meant to check was asserted on page one as following from the premise. Say *the checks suggest* there, and let the test carry it.

**The five conditions outrun the count.** The count gives you that he read the move as I would, that the standard is shared, and that the care in him is "obscured and not gone". The label-free list then says:

> What's looking out of him is no different in nature from what's looking out of me.

> The confusion covering it is not the same thing as it, and doesn't touch it.

Reading a move as I would requires shared competence, not identity of nature. And "doesn't touch it" is stronger than "obscured and not gone". In my tradition that is exactly the difference between the stains being removable and the stains being adventitious, never having touched the nature at all. The contempt argument earns the first. The second is the view. The sentence "I didn't have to believe them. I had to have been using them, and I was" is true of the weaker forms. Either weaken the two lines to what the count gives, or mark them as where the view goes past the count.

**The remorse test is softer than it looks.** The rival paragraph says a drive that lost "would leave regret at losing". Chapter 1 reports that people do find backward remorse, and reclassifies it:

> Mine turned out to be something else: regret that it cost me, anger at being taken advantage of, guilt at breaking a rule I was raised on, or feeling foolish.

That list is what the drive account predicts. My own case fit it: anger at being used. The test needs a way to tell remorse from regret that doesn't assume the answer. You may have one; the appendix doesn't give it.

**Kant isn't beaten.**

> A propensity of the will would have no reason to need its target's better nature.

On Kant's account the predisposition to good is in everyone, the target included, so a Kantian gets the target's side of the trap for free. The need for the target's better nature comes from the trap's shape (common knowledge plus hypocrisy), not from the account of the sender's evil. Any account where everyone has a conscience predicts it. What actually separates you from Kant is privation against propensity, and nothing in the appendix makes that observable. I'd either concede that and say the difference is metaphysical, or find an observation that splits them.

**"Only" outruns its support.**

> It's also the only way it spreads.

Honest malice spreads too. Abused children become abusers without anyone posing as righteous. Since malice also hides the sameness of the target, it's a turn, and you could say it spreads through the turn. But then the license-through-hypocrisy story isn't the only route, and the sentence should say *the only way contempt spreads*, or say how malice spreads.

**The clarity test risks circularity.** "the people others bring their worst to, because they see what's there" are partly chosen for having no verdict. My teacher fits: people bring him the worst and nothing forms. But I have a friend who is an ER nurse. She sees more clearly than anyone I know what people do to each other and to themselves, and she has contempt for certain patients. You'd say she's turned. Then clarity is defined as having no verdict, and the test can't fail. It needs a measure of clarity that doesn't mention contempt, such as accuracy about what people will do next.

**Where the appendix and Chapter 1 disagree.**

- The cold form. Chapter 1, The Spread:

> It's the same verdict, delivered by withdrawal, and it's meant to be felt: by them, or by whoever's watching.

Section 2:

> Not the cold form; the argument is about contempt, the aimed form, and the general rule rests on O1.

Chapter 1 says the cold form is aimed. My sister's husband agrees with Chapter 1. If the cold form is meant to be felt, the argument covers it, and the exclusion should go.

- Malice is in two cells. "Why it had to be this way":

> Fully aware, I'm looking at him, and the harm won't go; or it's honest malice, which frightens and doesn't madden.

So removing the self-hiding gives malice. But section 2:

> Take H away and keep C, and you get the second stranger

> Take C away and keep H, and what you send can frighten or degrade but can't read as hypocrisy: malice, not contempt.

Removing H gives the second stranger there, and malice is what you get from removing C. Both can't be right. I think the fix is that the second stranger also has no verdict, and the table's premise ("carries the verdict") rules him out. Keeping C and the verdict but dropping H gives honest contempt or malice; the second stranger is what you get when the verdict goes too.

- The misquote: "the smallest morsel" against Chapter 1's "The smallest slight carries the whole". It's also attributed to the inside view, where Chapter 1 said it about the one who was slighted.
- Evil as the turning ("Evil, in this book, names the turning") against Chapter 1's "Badness is the result of turning away, not its cause." Probably both are meant, but a careful reader will notice the switch from result to the thing itself.
- References Chapter 1 doesn't supply: the airport, the face exercise in O4, Appendix 3. O5 also reports a finding Chapter 1 never asked for ("the care was there"). Chapter 1 only asked "Would you have dug in?"

**4. UNCLEAR**

- “That's a fixed point: the recursion never contains anything but the turn.” I reread this three times. I think you mean: each higher level (hiding the hiding, hiding that) is the same act under a new description, so the regress adds no new content. That is not what a logician means by a fixed point, and your reading list cites Barwise for a different fixed point (common knowledge as a situation containing its own knowing). Two fixed points in one appendix, meaning different things, will snag every reader who knows either. Plainer: *Every level up is the same act described again. Hiding the hiding is still the turn. Nothing new ever enters.*

- “The converse holds with a purpose attached.” I could not parse this until the footnote, and even then “with a purpose attached” is doing a lot. Meaning: when you hold someone out of view, it is in order to do something their presence would stop. Plainer: *And the other way round: when I hold someone out of view, it's so that I can do something their being in view would stop.*

- “That needs the distinction the book draws with an airport”. There is no airport in Chapter 1. If it is in a later chapter, the footnote should give the example in a sentence, which it nearly does already; just drop “the book draws”.

- “which is why the book says the smallest morsel carries the whole.” Chapter 1 says “The smallest slight carries the whole”. Quote it as it stands.

- “The blank with no motive behind it is the Aspiration of Samantabhadra's”. Which blank? Nothing earlier in either text is called a blank. I take it you mean the first turning, “with nothing behind it”. Plainer: *The first turning, with no motive behind it, is the one in the Aspiration of Samantabhadra...* and put it after the sentence that introduces the turning.

- “Evil, in this book, names the turning: your own caring held out of view so that theirs can be.” Theirs what? Their caring? Their person? I think you mean: you hold your own caring out of view so that they can be held out of view. Plainer: *Evil, here, is the turning: I hold my own caring out of view so that I can hold them out of it.*

- “In other words, the turned-away state propagates itself, and it will turn out that nothing else can propagate it.” The clause “it will turn out” is a promissory note; I did not know where it was paid until “The license travels only through the turn.” Say it there and drop it here.

- O2's “none of it calculated: holding someone out of view takes work.” Uncalculated and effortful sit oddly together. I think you mean: not planned, but not passive either. Plainer: *None of it was planned, and none of it was easy. Holding someone out of view takes work.* (If you take cut q, this goes anyway.)

- “the general rule rests on O1” under “What is not claimed”. Which general rule? I assume “harm needs the turn”. Name it.

- The last sentence of “Necessary and sufficient”: “The aimed look is what the conditions are conditions on; the channel it travels by, that turning away shows in a face, is what the encounter's own common knowledge is built from (the footnote to section 1).” I followed it only after reconstructing the argument. You mean: C and H are conditions on an aimed look; the look's being readable at all depends on a further fact, that turning away shows in a face. That further fact is a premise, and it deserves its own sentence: *One more thing is assumed: turning away shows in a face. Without that, nothing here could be read.*

- “Nor does the tower restart the self-reference of section 1. The self-reference is in the content (what's hidden-and-revealed is the hiding-and-revealing), and its base case is the turn itself.” Correct, and to me the most interesting paragraph in Section 2, but most readers will not know there were two regresses to tell apart. Say so first: *There are two regresses here, and they're different. One is in what I hide: I hide the hiding. The other is in who knows: he knows I know he knows. The first bottoms out in the turn. The second never needs to be climbed.*

- “Here's one way to check one part of this, the part that says the verdict is not a perception”. This test sits under “What I think is really happening”, which opens “From here on I'm telling you what I believe”. But the test is of a Section 1 claim, not of the belief. A reader concludes the test belongs to the metaphysics. Move it above the door, to the end of “Where this stops”.

- “From here on I'm telling you what I believe, and the one test I know of for it.” Same problem: the one test does not test the belief. If there is no test for the belief, say so; the appendix is stronger for it.

**5. THE ARGUMENT**

Credit first. The appendix's central move is a transcendental argument of a respectable kind: from a feature of an act (its working) to a condition the act presupposes, and from there to a performative incoherence between what the act says and what it relies on. The nearest thing I know is Strawson's point that resentment is a participant attitude: you cannot resent a thing you have really taken the objective attitude towards. Contempt, on your account, claims the objective attitude (*this is all you are*) while being performed entirely in the participant one (it needs his uptake, his sense of betrayal). That is a good observation and I have not seen it put this way. Kant comes closer than the appendix admits: in the Doctrine of Virtue he argues, from memory, that one may not deny even a vicious man all respect, because he never loses the predisposition to good and could not otherwise be improved. But Kant's version is normative (you must not). Yours is presuppositional (you cannot, and you were relying on it). That difference is your contribution. Say so.

Now the reconstruction.

**Argument A: contempt presupposes what it denies** (Section 1 and “The punchline”).

A0. (Assumption.) My better nature is what sees myself in others; in particular it sees theirs.
P1. (Checked.) I cannot knowingly harm someone while he is in view; harm needs the turn.
P2. (Checked.) Only harm needs the turn; decency needs no story, and remorse does not run backward.
P3. (Stated only in the footnote and at the end of Section 2.) Turning away shows in a face and a voice.
P4. (Definition.) Contempt is an aimed look, meant to be read, carrying a verdict of lesser kind, whose harmful intent the sender disavows and believes his disavowal of.
P5. (Checked, O3.) What the target experiences is hypocrisy, and it maddens rather than frightens.
L1. From P5: the target grasps a standard he takes the sender to hold and to be breaking. *Valid.* This is conceptual; hypocrisy just is breach of a professed standard, and perceiving it requires grasping the standard.
L2. From P1, P2, L1: the standard is care, and the breach is the turn. *Not valid as it stands.* It needs a premise that the one standard both parties are sure to share is care. The appendix concedes this (“the rest lean on what you checked in your own case”), and that concession is honest. But my own case gives me my standard, not his. What carries it to him is A0. So L2 is sound only given A0, and the text should say so at the point of use.
L3. From L2 and ‘the look corrupts’: care in him is present, obscured, not gone. **Here a term changes meaning.** In L1 the target's “better nature” need only be a capacity to *recognise* a breach: a cognitive capacity. In L3 it is *care*: a motivational one. A sharp cynic can see hypocrisy perfectly and care about nothing. The bridge is the identity of the faculty that sees and the faculty that cares: O4's “seeing carries care”. That identity is the real premise of the punchline. It should be stated as a premise, and the exercise that supports it should be in the main text, not in Section 2.
L4. The target can turn back, since otherwise “there would be no point in pushing him further”. *Invalid as an inference.* That I push him presupposes only that he can be moved. It does not follow that he can be moved back. Reversibility comes from O5, which is about my own case; carrying it to him is again A0.
C1. Contempt's successful operation presupposes L1 to L4, and its explicit content denies them. *Valid given P6 below.*
C2. Contempt is self-defeating: it can only say *this is all you are* by addressing what it denies is there.

P6, the unstated premise: **the verdict asserts a difference of nature, not of character.** This is where “kind” shifts. Chapter 1 opens with “Contempt is the claim that you're a worse kind of thing than me.” The appendix's verdict is stronger: “no better nature, nothing shared, nothing left to reach, nothing to turn back.” ‘Worse kind’ in the moral sense (a vicious settled character, Aristotle's hexis) is perfectly compatible with L1 to L4: a man of vicious character still recognises hypocrisy and might still reform. If that is what the contemptuous man means, there is no contradiction, and the punchline falls to a distinction. The appendix needs P6 stated, with Chapter 1's evidence for it, which is good evidence: “I need their behavior to come from something I don't have: some alien quality called "evil"”. With P6 explicit, C1 and C2 go through. Without it, they equivocate on “kind”.

Note also what C2 is and is not. It is a performative incoherence, like *I am not speaking*. It shows the contemptuous man cannot coherently deny equality while doing what he is doing. It does not show equality is true. The appendix mostly respects this (“I didn't have to believe them. I had to have been using them”), but in one place it uses the conclusion before it is reached: “Nothing true can satisfy it, since there is no such difference.” That sentence comes before “The punchline”. It is true on A0, so add ‘on the opening assumption’, or move it after.

**Argument B: care is the baseline** (“Why it had to be this way”).

P1, P2 as above. C: what one turns from is the baseline; the turn is a departure, not a second baseline. *Valid given a bridging premise* B: the state one is in when making no effort of turning is one's baseline. B is reasonable and should be said.

Soundness depends on P2, and here the appendix contradicts Chapter 1. The rival account is said to predict that “a drive that merely lost would leave regret at losing, now and then mourning”, and then: “None of those happens, in any case I've found.” But Chapter 1 reports exactly that happening: “Mine turned out to be something else: regret that it cost me, anger at being taken advantage of, guilt at breaking a rule I was raised on, or feeling foolish.” So did I, with my cousin. Regret over decency is observed. The rival's distinguishing prediction is remorse, not regret, and the sentence should be cut back to that.

Which raises the real question for P2. The case philosophers reach for is Huckleberry Finn, who helps Jim, feels sick with guilt for it, and has to argue himself into it. Chapter 1 files that under “guilt at breaking a rule I was raised on”. Fine. But then the asymmetry is only as strong as your criterion for telling remorse from rule-guilt. If the criterion is ‘remorse is whatever runs only one way’, P2 is true by definition and tests nothing. You need a mark of remorse that does not mention its direction: something like *it is about the person, not the rule*, or *it survives learning the rule was wrong*. One sentence. Without it, the line “I haven't found one, and I'd want to hear of it” invites the reply that you have defined the counterexamples away.

**The rival account.** Some of its listed predictions do not follow from it. “The clearest-seeing would hold the most contempt.” Why? Someone who clearly sees a bad nature might feel pity, or fear, as one does for a wolf. “Harshness without a verdict would be answered as harshness.” Nothing in the nature account says how harshness is received. These two are straw. The others (remorse backward, harm with eyes open, the look injuring without the target's better nature) are fair. Against Kant and dissonance theory the appendix finds the right differential, the reception structure, and I think it is correct.

**The chain, link by link.**
- Link 1, “The hiding shows, because the faculty I hide from is the one he reads with.” *Does not follow.* ‘The faculty’ names mine in the first clause and his in the second. That I hide from my instance of it does not entail anything showing to his. The inference goes through only if they are one faculty, and that is exactly the claim the appendix later marks as belief: “one faculty, in two bodies”. What actually licenses link 1 is P3, the empirical premise that turning away shows in a face. Put P3 here.
- Link 3, “The harm is deniable, because its only content is the hiding.” The deniability comes from the channel (gaze, tone, timing, unquotable), not from the content's being self-referential. A self-referential secret written in a letter would be perfectly quotable. Again P3 is doing the work.
- Link 7, “and only from the turned-away state”. This holds only if the target reads the sender correctly. A target who misreads the second stranger's harshness as contempt takes the license anyway. So “only” needs ‘when he reads me rightly’, or you need to argue that misreading is itself the target's own turn (which I think you could).

If you take my cut (k), these flaws go with the chain, but P3 must then be stated somewhere in Section 1.

**The limbo trilemma.** *Valid as a form*: unaware, aware, or limbo; the first two fail; so limbo. But the second horn contradicts the appendix itself. “Fully aware, I'm looking at him, and the harm won't go; or it's honest malice, which frightens and doesn't madden.” Earlier: “All malice hides one thing from the one doing it: the sameness of the target. This is true even in the sadist.” If all malice hides the sameness, honest malice is not fully aware. The fix is small: “fully aware” should be ‘fully aware of my intent’, and the malice horn is then aware of intent while hiding sameness, which is consistent.

**Necessary and sufficient.** The setup fixes three things: an aimed look, meant to be read, carrying the verdict *you're a lesser kind of thing*. Then: “Take H away and keep C, and you get the second stranger: fierce, no verdict”. But the verdict was held fixed. Removing H while keeping C and the verdict gives a man who knowingly tells his equal he is lesser: deliberate degradation, not the second stranger. Either H is independent of the verdict, in which case the case is misdescribed, or holding the verdict while C obtains entails H, in which case H is not a separate condition but a consequence. I suspect the second is your view, and it is the more interesting one: *given common knowledge of equality, the verdict can only be held in limbo*. Say that, and the 2x2 becomes a 2x2 on C and the verdict, with H derived.

**“Know”.** In Section 1's bullets “I know” means: available but not let in. In the common-knowledge tower it means what Aumann means. These differ, and in Aumann's sense a self-deceived party does not know. The footnote's appeal to Lewis repairs this, since Lewis needs only reason to believe at each level, and limbo leaves the reasons intact. That is the right repair. Make it in the body once: *In limbo, “I know” means I have every reason to believe it, and don't let myself.*

**“Absence”.** “An absence can act only through the presence it's the absence of”. As a general claim about privations this is false: blindness does not act through sight. What makes it true here is Chapter 1's grammatical point, “Not from a part of you; the part was the one doing the hiding.” Rest the claim on that, not on privation in general.

**Scope.** “Not universality from this page; O1 to O5 are stated for everyone and open to counterexample, and the deduction needs only your own instance.” The deduction of the punchline's conditions three to five is about *him*. My own instance gives me my case. Again it is A0 that carries it across. The sentence should say ‘your own instance and the opening assumption’.

**Contradictions with Chapter 1**, collected:
1. Regret over decency: observed in Chapter 1, denied in the rival paragraph (above).
2. The strength of the verdict: “a worse kind of thing” in Chapter 1, “no better nature, nothing shared” in the appendix (above).
3. The cold form. Chapter 1: “It's the same verdict, delivered by withdrawal, and it's meant to be felt”. The appendix: “Not the cold form; the argument is about contempt, the aimed form”. If the cold form is meant to be felt, it is aimed. Either the appendix covers the cold form or Chapter 1 should not say it is meant to be felt. My cousin is cold, and I can tell you it is meant to be felt.
4. ‘In view’. Chapter 1 is careful: “That makes part of this a definition, and I'll own it. What isn't a definition is what you checked: that your wrong needed a story, and your decency didn't.” The appendix then lists “Harm needs the turn: with the person fully in view, it won't go” among “two things you checked”. By Chapter 1's own account, the checked fact is the story asymmetry; “harm needs the turn” is partly the definition. And O1's “you couldn't give a stranger the gaze while looking at them” uses “looking at”, which is the very sense of “in view” Chapter 1 set aside (“Out of view doesn't mean out of sight.”). Argument B should be run on the story asymmetry, which is what the reader actually checked.

Does it hold, overall? The core does: C1 and C2 are valid once P3 and P6 are stated, and sound given A0 and O4, which is exactly what the introduction says the appendix is: an abduction from an assumption you need not hold. The claims that outrun their support are the chain's link 1 (which smuggles in the belief section), the two straw predictions, the regret sentence, and the H-removal case. None of these is fatal. All of them are visible to a reader like me, and a reader like me is who Section 2 is for.
