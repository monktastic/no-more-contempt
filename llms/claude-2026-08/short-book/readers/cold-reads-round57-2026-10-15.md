Dear Aditya,

**Chapter 2: CLOSE. Chapter 3: CLOSE. The Picture I Use: CLOSE. Epilogue: NOT READY, and only narrowly. Two sentences stand between it and CLOSE.**

The major objections, in order of weight:

Epilogue. A woman I sat with for two years was told by her pastor that forgiving her husband was "the only way back." This sentence would find her:

> The only way back is forgiveness, theirs and mine, which turn out to be one act, since there was never anything to forgive but the turning.

You mean your own way back, from your own vision, and "theirs and mine" means the people you wronged and yourself. She will not read it that way. Chapter 3 told her:

> And nothing here demands that you turn toward them at all.

The Epilogue takes that back. And "there was never anything to forgive but the turning" will sound to her like what was done to her was nothing. The safety sentence you added is the right one. It is just in the wrong place, two paragraphs too late:

> None of this is a reading of what anyone has suffered at someone else's hands.

Put it, or something like it, next to the forgiveness line.

Epilogue, second. This one is for a reader the safety sentence doesn't cover at all, someone who is ill, or whose child died, or who lost a house:

> I saw why hiding stuff from myself could never work, because part of me always saw that I was doing it, and that part was essentially God, and God is reality, so knowledge of my wrongdoing always takes physical shape in my "external" world.

Your disclaimer covers harm "at someone else's hands." It doesn't cover the tumor. People who already blame themselves for what happens to their bodies will read "takes physical shape" as a diagnosis of themselves. One clause saying this is not a reading of illness or misfortune either would do it.

Chapter 3, A note for people who were hurt. The note is mostly very good. "Seeing clearly what they are capable of is not the verdict" is the sentence I'd want every survivor to read. One line in it I'd change:

> If it's there, look at it, and ask whether you want to recreate the thing that hurt you.

The revenge fantasy that comes in the second year after leaving is often the first sign that a woman has found again the self that knows she counts. It is not abuse, and asking her whether she wants to "recreate" it equates the two at the worst moment. "Look at it" is enough. Your fourth paragraph makes the point better: the license "doesn't come from the hurt."

Chapter 3, the end. A woman who left a violent marriage will read this as "you could have been him":

> The murderer and the abuser didn't start there, and nothing about you guarantees you'd have turned out differently.

It's true in your sense, and the next sentence ("That isn't said to frighten you") shows you know it lands hard. But "the abuser" is one word too specific for a reader the note has just made room for. "The murderer" alone, or "the worst people you know," does the same work.

The Picture I Use. This sentence lost me for a page:

> Our institutions meet a hidden need in all of us. Their visible evil lets us establish our own relative goodness by contrast, while providing cover for our smaller betrayals: _At least I'm not like them. I'm just sticking it to the man!_

I worked thirty-one years for Multnomah County. Some of what I saw there was rotten, and most of it was tired people trying. "Their visible evil", said of institutions as such, is the contempt the book warns against, aimed at a building. The paragraph's real claim is about corrupt leaders, and it gets there in the fourth sentence. Start there and I'm with you.

**3. CONFUSIONS**

Chapter 2:

> That feels *wrong* to use for my own ends.

"That" has to be the person, but the sentence before it is about their wellbeing. I read it as "Using them for my own ends feels wrong."

> That care is what I'll call the light: what I turn from to cause harm, and what anyone is turned toward when they aren't, in themselves and in everyone else.

"when they aren't" what? I had to go back. I think it means "when they aren't turned away."

> **To wield contempt, we have to already know that it's evil, and that its target is redeemable**: the verdict was aimed at pushing them further, and nothing can be pushed from a place it isn't in.

I read this three times. "Further" from what? I think it means: contempt tries to push someone out of the light, so it must believe they're still in the light. That's a good point, but the sentence makes me do all the work.

> Now put your enemy's face there instead, (if it's someone who abused you, pick another), and try for the same recognition.

Just the stray comma before the parenthesis.

Chapter 3:

> If harm can only be done from the turned-away state, then deciding *to stop seeing someone*, as such, would be a wrong done while still looking, which is the one thing Chapter 2's face showed can't happen.

I got it on the second try: you can't choose the turning under its own name, because choosing it would be harming while still seeing. But "as such" carries the whole argument, and the next paragraph says you *can* choose to stop looking. See item 4.

> The want was innocent; the label was borrowed.

Borrowed from whom? From the parasite, I suppose, or from the culture. I liked the sentence and didn't know what it was saying.

> When I speak it to my allies, or into a crowd, mine is looking for others to resonate with.

This is the one I couldn't place at all. It opens a section after a break. "It" is the verdict, I think, and "mine" is my parasite, but neither has been named for several paragraphs.

> What they do is theirs; nothing in your rage is responsible for them.

Is "them" their acts or the people? I think it means "your rage didn't cause what they do." For a survivor that's an important sentence, and it should be unmistakable.

> The way you hold your own darkness is the way you'll end up holding theirs, and it's true of you first.

What is true of me first? I think it means "the contempt you'd aim at them, you aim at yourself first." Say that.

> This is what the buttons in Chapter 1 were measuring. Not whether you've done anything terrible, but how much contempt you're still capable of, toward anyone, for any reason.

The paragraph just before it says most turning away "has no enemy in it at all", and is self-serving rather than contemptuous. Then the buttons are said to measure contempt. I couldn't tell whether the low-grade infection *is* contempt or only the soil it grows in.

The Picture I Use:

> Everyone who remains caught in it (all of us, to varying degrees) imports the plausible deniability from Chapter 1 into the structures they participate in.

It's abstract enough that I couldn't see anyone doing anything. I think it means we each bring our small deniable turnings to work, to church, to the county office, and the institution adds them up. Show me one person doing it.

> One that raised the part that proves as high as it could go and quietly demoted the part that knows: across all of us, what the locker room built across ten men.

The locker room built a shadow space where the men grew bolder. It didn't demote knowing in favor of proving. I couldn't make the comparison fit.

> You can only choose the counterfeit once you've forgotten what the real one feels like, the way you can only choose junk food once you've forgotten real food.

I choose junk food while remembering real food perfectly well. The analogy works against you.

Epilogue:

> Every action is either a step back toward it, or away from it—and that choice doesn't just affect you, because of what I saw next.

"what I saw next" turns out to be the story of the first forgetting. The reason the choice doesn't just affect me comes much later, in the "chains of causality". I went looking for the link and didn't find it where you pointed.

**4. CONTRADICTIONS**

Turning toward the one who hurt you. Chapter 3, the note:

> And nothing here demands that you turn toward them at all.

Epilogue:

> The only way back is forgiveness, theirs and mine, which turn out to be one act, since there was never anything to forgive but the turning.

Covered above. For a hurt reader, these two can't both be true.

Choosing to stop seeing. Chapter 3, first paragraph:

> Try it: hold someone's eyes and decide, while holding them, to stop seeing them. See if you can find the moment. I can't.

Chapter 3, second paragraph:

> While you can still feel both pulls—the recognition, and the want the recognition would veto—you're still looking, and you can keep looking or stop.

I see how you reconcile them: you choose it under another label. But on the page, one paragraph says you can't decide to stop seeing while looking, and the next says that's exactly the choice. (When I did the exercise, I found I *can* catch it sometimes, in sitting: a small tightening just before the story. That supports your second paragraph more than your first.)

Effort at the far end. Chapter 3, in one paragraph:

> On the line, the hiding has become so practiced that it no longer takes effort, which is what makes it complete.

Two sentences on:

> The most lost person is carrying *more* of that knowledge than you are, not less, and holding it down harder, with more of themselves committed to the holding.

Effortless, then harder. Chapter 2 sides with the first ("Turning away takes upkeep, at least until it's practiced enough to run by itself"). I think you mean the effort goes underground, not away, but that's not what it says.

The tree and the lumber. Chapter 2:

> Somebody cuts down a tree that has stood for a thousand years, for fun.

The Picture I Use:

> A man held as a thing, a forest as lumber.

The check worked on me *because* it was for fun. My neighbors' sons log in the Coast Range, and they aren't holding anything as stuff any more than a carpenter is. The Picture slides from gratuitous harm to use and counts them as one refusal.

The finger. Chapter 3:

> I give the finger to the driver who cuts me off, and ten minutes later I cut someone off myself and don't even register it, because I had somewhere to be.

Epilogue:

> I like to tell myself that every time we do this, we give the Devil a middle finger.

Same gesture, contempt in one place and the good act in the other. I smiled at the Epilogue line, then remembered the driver, and the smile went.

"The light." Chapter 2 sets a limit:

> It's all the word will mean.

Here, "all" is care belonging to awareness. In the Picture the lowercase light has become a combatant that can be usurped ("The darkness can't win a head-on fight with the light"), and "the one orientation every one of us steers by". The Epilogue honestly says when it starts meaning more. The Picture starts meaning more without saying so. And "nothing to differ by" is used of the light (Chapter 2, Epilogue) and of the darkness (the Picture). I followed it, but a careful reader will ask whether "nothing to tell them apart" is a fact about awareness or about absence.

The parasite's goal. Chapter 3 grants it one:

> What it wants is what Chapter 1 found: to stay hidden from the one it lives in.

The Picture gives it what reads as a second: "until the one orientation every one of us steers by is the one thing none of us believes is there." These may be one goal, since staying hidden and making us forget the light might be the same act, but neither place says so.

The creature. Chapter 3 asks:

> If the word makes you picture a creature, drop the picture and keep the behavior: a groove in attention so old that its shape looks like intent.

Later in the chapter, the locker room has "all their parasites come out and play." That's a creature.

**5. THE BACKGROUND HUM**

The first link holds. Chapter 3's low-grade infection is the best thing in the chapter for me: the finger, the cut-off, "no fever, nothing anyone would notice, and running the whole time." I recognized my whole day in it. The Picture names it "a background hum of darkness", and that's the right name.

Hum to institutions: asserted, and needlessly. You have the link already, in Chapter 3: "you can only be good *relative* to someone worse." That's why we need someone corrupt at the top. Say so, and the institutions paragraph is earned rather than announced. As it stands, "Our institutions meet a hidden need" arrives as a claim, and "Their visible evil" overreaches (see item 1).

Hum to the conspiracy archetype: earned, and the best paragraph in the Picture. "coordination with nobody coordinating" names something I've felt in every agency I worked in. "that misplacement has filled graves" is the right warning, in the right place.

Hum to the Devil: it works as a picture, and you've marked it as one. "the background hum seen as one thing" is the hinge, and I could follow it. The step that went by too fast was "It has a bottomless appetite." Where did the appetite come from? Chapter 3 gave you "Nothing in it says *far enough*". Point back to that and the appetite is earned. Augustine on the Devil's nature being good is exactly what a Buddhist reader needs, so that "Devil" doesn't land as a substance.

Hum to the biosphere: the weakest link. It rests on Chapter 2's widening, which you yourself offered as a report ("If it doesn't, take what follows as my report"). The Picture then closes it: "This is all it takes". It also slides from the tree cut for fun to the forest cut for lumber. I believe something like this is true. I don't think the page has shown it, and "all it takes" makes it sound shown. Wetiko is handled carefully and with credit, and I was glad of it.

The Epilogue's use of the hum ("Our collective self-deception, the background hum, takes on a structure") adds the vision's version, which is fine. But it re-argues the conspiracy instead of just showing what you saw.

Overall: earned through the conspiracy, a good picture for the Devil, asserted at the institutions and at the biosphere.
