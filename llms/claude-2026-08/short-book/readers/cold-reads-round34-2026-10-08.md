## Maureen (56, dental hygienist, 17 years sober in AA, sponsor; lapsed Catholic)

### Stage 1

Dear Aditya,

I read it at my kitchen table over two evenings with a pencil, and the margins are a mess, so this will be a little out of order where I went back. I'll tell you what happened as it happened. I've been sober seventeen years and I've sat through more fourth steps than I can count, my own and a dozen women's. So when somebody starts in on "the knowledge was right there and you wouldn't look at it," I'm either going to say amen or I'm going to smell a sales pitch. With you it was mostly amen, with a few places I smelled it.

**The opening.** "Maybe you'll recognize something of yourself in it. Or maybe not." Fine. I relaxed a little. "Hold them tentatively, until you see the picture they assemble." Okay, I can do that. We say "take what you like and leave the rest."

**The memory.** I didn't have to hunt. Summer of 2007, I drove Danny home from my sister Carol's in Maumee after four glasses of wine, him eight years old in the back seat with his Little League glove. I told Carol I was fine. When I finally wrote it down in my fourth step two years later, it was not news. Not one bit of it was news. So when you say "It lived in a strange kind of limbo, in which you simultaneously knew and yet somehow didn't," I bought that outright. That's just a description of drinking. And I liked "If that doesn't match anything in your memory, the rest of this won't make sense, and I'd rather you know that now." That's an honest man talking.

"If you did, did that happen passively?" No. I picked fights with Rick on purpose so I'd have a reason to pour. Some part of me knew I was doing it while I did it. Your question about whether worse things are harder to remember this way: yes. The drive with Danny took me two years. The things I said to Rick took longer.

"That's not owning up; it's another way to hide." Amen. Self-pity is the drunk's favorite hiding place, and "I'm a piece of garbage" is just self-pity in a hair shirt. I hesitated on "Nobody ever looked honestly at something in themselves while being told they were worthless for having it," because I've watched women get sober after somebody told them flat out they were a liar and a drunk. But I thought about it and that's not the same as worthless. Bev, my first sponsor, called me a liar plenty. She never once said I was garbage. So I bought it.

"Everyone does it. It's not a big deal. Just this once." I laughed out loud, and not a happy laugh. Then "Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" That one went in. The part of me that knew I shouldn't drive never once made a case. It just sat there. The other part had a lawyer.

**The cashier.** I used the girl at the Kroger on Monroe Street, Saturday, the one with the purple streak in her hair. The smile went right out, no trouble. The sneer wouldn't go until I gave it something: she was slow, she was on her phone. I made it up to get it out the door. So yes, it needed a reason. I'll add, since you want honesty, that I've sneered at cashiers for real when I was tired, and "tired" was the reason. Bought.

**Remorse.** I tested this hard, because of Danny. Around 2017 I gave him two thousand dollars for "rent" and he put it in his arm. I felt sick about that money for a year. So I asked myself: was that remorse for being decent? And no. It wasn't decency, it was fear dressed up as kindness, and Al-Anon beat that into me. What I felt about the money was foolish and furious. What I felt about the *fear* was the sick one. So your asymmetry held, but it made me realize I'd been calling something decent that wasn't. "But never remorse: that sickening sense of having gone against myself." Yes. That's the feeling. The bad man lying awake guilty over secret kindness: I couldn't picture it either. Bought tentatively. Enough to keep going.

"I'm usually not aware of this need. Watch how it affects my behavior anyway." Good. That made me want to watch.

**The confrontation.** Believable, yes, and I'll tell you why. I've been on both ends. I was on his end for ten years: Carol's face at Thanksgiving, my mother's. And I was on your end with Danny, every year from when he was nineteen to about twenty-five. "I would defy a message like that too, and violently." Danny did. I'd have bet my house he'd do it, and I'm ashamed to say part of me was glad when he did, because it proved me right in front of Rick.

"Surely that's not what I'm doing. I'm just being fierce with evil, right? Right." That's exactly the voice. I know that voice. Where I skimmed: the "invitation" paragraph, and "*Ha!* Classic." I'm not sure why the "Classic" bothered me. It sounded more like you writing a character than like a mind. The rest sounded like a mind.

**The cold one.** This one's more me than the hot one, if I'm truthful. "a quiet filing-away, a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them." That's me with Rick at our grandson Mason's birthday last spring. I was very polite. I was so polite. And "If the hot version didn't sound like you, read what follows with the cold one in mind" was a kindness, because I'd have gotten my back up thinking you'd decided I was a yeller. "If not, I have no argument to offer here." That's the second time you've let me off the hook if it doesn't fit, and both times I trusted you more for it.

**"I'll just go ahead and name what's happening."** Insight, mostly, but I had to read two sentences twice. "In brief: I am in limbo about provoking him, and showing him that I'm in limbo is itself the provocation." Three times on that one, actually. I got it on the third. "The signal is the pretense that there is no signal." Twice. I'm still not sure I could explain it to someone, but I knew what you meant once I thought of Danny's face.

Here's my first real refusal. "Neither half alone would be enough. If it felt accidental, he might forgive or pity me instead." I wrote "NO" in the margin. Danny dug in when I was purely, honestly confused and scared and trying. He dug in when I did it the Al-Anon way. Drunks and addicts dig in, period. Then I sat there with my pencil and heard myself, and that's your narrator's line from two pages before, "He would have reacted poorly no matter what I did." So I don't know. I'm leaving the "NO" in the margin and I'm also leaving a question mark next to it. I could still follow everything after it.

Then this, which went straight to my stomach: "Deny it, and you're the kind of person who does. Get angry, and that's proof too. Stay calm, and you don't even care. Apologize, and it's too convenient." I have been on both ends of every one of those four. I have said "well, look how angry you're getting" to my own son. That paragraph is a hall of mirrors all by itself, and you had me there before you named it later.

"My stories kept changing; my conclusion never did." Underlined twice. That's the disease in nine words.

"It won't feel like a loss to him. It will feel like he's gotten a bit wiser, a little more realistic about how the world works." That's the bitterness I see in the men at the Tuesday meeting who've been around a while but never got happy. They think they got wise. Bought.

"From the inside, being proven right and being right are the same experience. The only difference is who produced the evidence." That's one I'll be repeating to sponsees, and I'll probably forget to give you credit. "If I'd looked carefully, I'd have seen my role in causing it. So I didn't—and that *was* my role in causing it." Good. Bought.

**The two buttons.** My worst enemy is a man named Kyle Brenner, who got Danny on pills at twenty and sold to him for three years after. I put Kyle in front of the buttons. Honest answer: my hand was on the first button before you'd finished describing the second. Then the AA in me kicked in and said, I'd want him to get the pill, I'd want him to get sober. But that came second, and it came in a voice I use at meetings. "I'll be honest: there is in me." Thank you for that. It made it possible for me to admit it.

"And that lie is exactly what lets me act on it, in ways I rarely recognize until later." Bought, because I could name three ways I've acted on the Kyle button without touching him, mostly in how I talk about him to other mothers.

**"Whose intention."** "It acts as if it has goals of its own, and it pursues them intentionally." I didn't refuse it, I heard something else in it. In the rooms we say "my disease is doing push-ups in the parking lot," and "cunning, baffling, powerful." So I've lived with the idea that something in me plans. What I liked is that the very next thing was "Don't mistake the grammar for the claim," and then "None of this lets me off the hook. It's my mind, and I answer for what it does." That came in time. Right in time. If you'd left it a page later I'd have thought you were selling the devil-made-me-do-it, and I've heard enough of that from men explaining their third relapse.

"When I'm sure someone was fully aware of what they were doing, I've made them a villain. When I'm sure they had no idea, I've made them a machine." This caught me. I do both with Danny depending on the day. When I'm angry he knew exactly what he was doing. When I'm scared he's sick and couldn't help it. Neither one is my son. That stopped me for a good while.

"I don't think this part of me is ultimately trying to help me get away with stuff. I think it's trying to protect me, and the getting away is a side effect." That I found in myself. Drinking started as protection. Everything does.

**"Yourself."** I tried it. I went back to the drive with Danny and asked, who was I hiding it from? I didn't find my better nature, not by that name. What I found first was the one who'd have to *do* something about it. Stop drinking, tell Carol, pull over. I was hiding it from the part of me that would have acted. Under that, if I sat longer, there was something like the girl I was at my first communion, which surprised me, and I'm not sure what to make of it. So I found something near what you point at, not the thing exactly.

"Feel into it." I'm sorry, but that one made me wince. That's a yoga phrase. It's what the gal who ran the women's retreat at Lourdes said every five minutes. It made me feel like I was being handled for a second. I did it anyway because the question before it was good.

"did it feel like *losing* something of yourself, or like getting something back?" Getting something back. No contest. That's the whole promise of the program.

**"What we *are*."** "It's a description of what we *are* in the absence of all self-deception." I granted it as your claim. I didn't find it. It sounded close to the nuns, made in the image of God, and I'm a lapsed Catholic who still goes at Christmas, so I don't throw it out. But "in the absence of all self-deception": I've never met that woman, in me or in anybody, so I don't know how you'd know what she is. Grandiose? A little. You saved it some by saying right after that sometimes even when you think you're turned toward it, there's self-deception you're not seeing. Later on, the Common knowledge part moved me partway. I'll get there.

**The gaze.** The dehumanizing look I've received: the intake nurse at St. V's, January 2009, after I fell on the ice outside the Fifth Third with a .19. She didn't hate me. She just looked at me like there wasn't anybody to talk to. And yes, I could feel her absent. When I tried your question, "Why would he have to turn away at all, if what he is, is essentially bad?", I'll be honest, I got nowhere with it about her. I don't know what was going on with that nurse. Where I got somewhere was turning it around: when I give that look, there's effort in it. It's not my face at rest. I have to hold something shut. If I were just bad, I wouldn't need to hold anything. That's as far as I got, and it was far enough to keep going.

"Is it possible that you look a tiny bit like this when treating him with contempt? I've discovered I do, and I don't think it's a coincidence." Here I felt pushed. Not wrong. Pushed. The question was fine and then you told me what you'd found and that it wasn't a coincidence, and I felt like the answer had been filled in for me.

**The worst thing.** I thought of the man in Sylvania who kept his stepdaughter locked in a basement, it was in the Blade years ago. "Could they have done it with kindness in their eyes?" No. But my honest answer was that there probably wasn't anything in his eyes at all, which I guess is your point.

**"Bad essence."** "It's convenient to ascribe the worst behavior..." This caught me rather than reading as you heading something off, because of Kyle. I want Kyle to be made of something else. I want it badly. "But as far as I can tell, badness is the result of turning away, not its cause." I can't say I agree all the way down. But I've watched men at meetings who did things I'd have called pure badness get sober and get honest, and if it was their essence that couldn't have happened. "Like the *presence* of something, rather than the absence it actually is." That's good. A drunk in a blackout looks like a monster. It's an absence.

"When I feel a fundamental difference between me and my worst enemy, I ask which part of me needs there to be one." I wrote "Kyle" next to that.

**The corrector.** Bev, March 2010, in my kitchen. I was calling Danny's counselor behind his back to check whether he'd been going. Bev said: "You're running his program because yours bores you. Knock it off." Hard as nails, in front of my other sponsee who happened to be there. I didn't dig in. I cried and then I laughed, because she was right and there wasn't anything in it but me. "Not more gently. Just that their anger was directed at what you did, and not what you are." Yes. And "*you're better than this*." That's exactly what it felt like, though Bev would never have said it in those words. "It's a little darkness I inject into otherwise-good behavior, poisoning it: it turns a correction he could take into an attack he has to resist." Bought outright. "What's left is fierce kindness." That's Bev.

**The time I corrected someone badly.** Danny, nineteen, kitchen table, I found a foil in his jeans. "What is *wrong* with you?" And worse. Six years later, different approach: "I love you. I'm not giving you money. Call me anytime." Did it work better? I can't honestly say it fixed him. He's fourteen months clean now and I don't take credit for that. But he kept calling. Under the first approach he stopped calling. So yes.

**Integrity.** "But someone always is: me." That's the tenth step, and it's my grandmother's "God sees," and it rang. I bought the saying, and what you did with it.

"What would happen if I could just stop pretending I don't already know this?" I thought the chapter was ending there. It's a good last line. It didn't end.

**Common knowledge.** The hall of mirrors: Rick and me, 2004 to 2008. Both drinking, both knowing, both knowing the other knew, and if either one said it out loud it was crazy talk or picking a fight. Yes. I've lived in that house. "You may have felt this before, like a maddening hall of mirrors: you both know exactly what's happening, and that it can't be named without sounding crazy."

The argument from the look to "same kind of thing": I followed it, slowly. "How could it do that, unless it already knew that he's the same kind of thing as me" — I bought that tentatively. You can't aim something at a man's conscience unless you believe he's got one. That's true. Then the bold sentence, "**Contempt only works because we already know we're the same kind of thing.**" The bold put me off. It felt like a slogan on a poster. And "only" made me itch; I'd have said "works because."

The hypocrisy part held better for me than the look part, and here's why. Danny, two years ago, in the parking lot at Mercy: "You of all people." That went in to the bone. It went in because I hold the thing he said I'd broken. "But the charge only landed because I hold the one he accused me of breaking, and his outrage in making it showed that he holds it too." What surprised me was the second half. I never once thought that his throwing it at me meant he held it too. I only thought it meant he was angry. That changed something about how I remember that afternoon. So that part added something.

"Try it now: imagine the same act without turning away." I did. I put myself back in the car, looking straight at Danny in the rearview with his glove, knowing I'd had four. I couldn't have turned the key. I couldn't. "Does it surprise you that a stranger could count on that about you?" A little, yes. That's a good question and it didn't feel like a push.

Where contempt reached in me: my ex-mother-in-law, Dolores, at Danny's eighth-grade graduation, looked at me like I was what was wrong with her family. "Not your pride: an insult to your pride is easier to shrug off." True. It reached lower. But here's where I'd differ. It didn't reach for the me that knows I count. It reached for the me that wasn't sure I did. Maybe that's the same place from two sides. I don't know. I'm telling you what I found.

"**Love and contempt are addressed to the same place.** One says *I know you're in there.* The other says *there's nobody in there*, and they both say it to the somebody who is." That one I'll keep. Bold and all.

The last time I sent that look: two Sundays ago, to Tasha, my sponsee, who'd gone out for the third time. She called me from a parking lot. I didn't say anything cruel. I said "Okay." But I said it a certain way, and she heard it. "Did it feel like justice, just a little sweeter than justice needs to be?" Yes. God help me. It was sweet.

**"Evil."** You use it a lot and I didn't flinch at it. I'm Catholic-raised; we have the word. What I stopped at was "It's why I don't think "pure evil" is a different kind of thing, but the same thing at full strength." I thought of the man in Sylvania and I thought of Kyle and I pushed back. Then I thought of a man named Walt who used to sit in the back at the Saturday morning meeting, who'd done eleven years for something he'd only ever talk about with his sponsor, and who was the gentlest man I knew. I didn't stop trusting you. I don't accept it yet. I'm holding it, the way you asked at the start.

"I couldn't have been argued into this: I had to really taste that dark quality in myself." I trusted that more than anything else in the section. It's how the program works.

"Bad people" is just the name I use for those whom I feel okay doing this to." That one stung and I wrote "Kyle" again.

**The dumping ground.** It arrived fast but not from nowhere, for me, because of the Blade. When they post an overdose story, the comments say "one less junkie." Danny could have been one of those stories three times. So "_you will be the dumping ground for our collective darkness._" landed. But it landed because of my life, not because of the page before it. If I didn't have Danny I think it would have read like a swerve. And "It only works because we all already know what's happening": the people writing "one less junkie" don't know anything, I wanted to say. Then I wasn't sure.

**"Part of me doesn't want to believe we answer to the same thing."** "The same thing," to me, was conscience. Or God, if you'd asked me at nine. Or what we call in the rooms "a power greater than ourselves," which I've never had to define because I know it when I'm going against it. This caught me hard: "I treat it like an advisor offering an opinion, which I can weigh against other practical concerns and set aside if the price is right." And then "that I have the right to look at what is right and say not today." That's "just tonight." That's every drink I ever had after the first year of knowing. And "I'm just another person who knew what was right, decided it was negotiable, and then looked for someone worse than me to prove I wasn't so bad after all." That's the whole reason I used to go to bars where the drunks were worse than me.

The end of that section, "If it does end up reminding them, I can't take credit for it." Good. Felt like another ending, though.

**"Only a chump believes otherwise."** Landed. And I did connect it back, to his voice, "I'd be a chump," a page or two earlier. I liked that it came back. The fear you taught him is the fear you had. That's very AA too: we pass on exactly what we were trying to get away from.

**The spread.** "the next stranger he won't give the benefit of the doubt, the kid who watches him do it." The kid. That's Danny watching me and Rick. Caught me.

"I'm making the world a little darker, and I can feel it even as I do it." Here I didn't go along. I don't feel it while I do it. I feel it afterward, sometimes. Maybe you do. "Part of me knows it will spread, and that's part of why I send it." Same: I granted it as yours, I didn't find it.

Then this, which is the paragraph I'll remember from the whole chapter: "The *what's wrong with you?* that needs them to know they're broken. The sigh that tells them what I really think of them, dressed up to make me seem like the reasonable one." That's me. That's me with Danny on the phone last week when he said he might not make it for Sunday dinner. The sigh. I know exactly the sigh. I put the pencil down after that one.

"as I increasingly contact mine" — I didn't understand "contact" there. Contact my what? My better nature? It read like a word got dropped.

"Sometimes it looks like me writing this book *at* people, instead of *for* them." Good that you said it. Some pages it did feel *at*. The bold sentences, mostly.

"If seeing this makes you want to hide, notice that's the same verdict pointed inward." I thought the chapter was ending here too, and honestly it would have been a fine end. "What I need in order to look is what my enemy needed from me, and what the corrector with integrity gave me: to be seen as more than what I do." That's a good place to stop.

**The last exercise.** I did it, and here's the thing nobody would think of: I look at faces all day, eight inches away, upside down, with their mouths open. Most days a patient is a mouth. So I tried it Monday with Mrs. Ochoa, eighty-one, in my chair. "Imagine looking into a stranger's face until it stops being something you're looking at and becomes a place somebody is looking out of." It happened. Her eyes were looking up at me, a little scared of the scaler, and for a second there was somebody in there. I couldn't have been rough with her after that. I went slower.

Then Kyle's face. "For me the story is there before the face is." Same here. The story was there before I'd even got his face clear. That's the most useful sentence in the chapter for me, because I can check it. I can do that one in the car.

Did it gather things up or was it one ending too many? It gathered, for me, because of the chair. But I thought the chapter ended three times before it did: at "What would happen if I could just stop pretending...", at "I can't take credit for it," and at "to be seen as more than what I do." By the exercise I was tired, and if I hadn't had Mrs. Ochoa that morning I might have skipped it.

**Length.** If I had to cut a fifth: "Most contempt is quiet" and the paragraph about turned toward or away not being binary, which I already knew by then. Some of "Whose intention," the part about villain and machine is worth keeping but the paragraph about "the genuine darkness I saw in my enemy" I skimmed. And "Part of me doesn't want to believe we answer to the same thing" says the Common knowledge section over again, though it says it in a way that caught me better, so maybe cut the other. "I'm already doing it" and "this is all you are" and "same kind of thing" come back so many times that by the end I felt them being pressed into me.

Said twice: "Contempt only works because we already know we're the same kind of thing" and "It only works because we all already know what's happening." "Whatever else divides us, we answer to the same thing—and we both know it" and then the whole next section starting "Part of me doesn't want to believe we answer to the same thing." You "double down" twice, which is a phrase I'd lose; my sons say it.

Words: "feel into it," "wellbeing," "orient," "contact mine." "Self-deception" so many times it went flat; in the rooms we just say lying.

**Last.** Was it obvious? A lot of it I already knew, the way anybody who's done a real fourth step knows it. The limbo, the lawyer in your head, the asymmetry of remorse. AA got there. What I didn't have was the part about the other person: that my look is aimed at something in them I'm claiming isn't there, and that Danny's "you of all people" meant he held it too. That's new to me, or newly heavy.

This week: I'm calling Tasha back tomorrow, and I'm going to say the "okay" again the way I should have said it the first time. And I'm going to catch the sigh with Danny. I won't catch it every time. I'll catch it once.

Would I pass it on? Yes, to Linda from my Al-Anon group, who has been polite to her ex-husband's new wife for six years in a way that could freeze a lake. She'd get it, and she'd be mad at me for a week. Not to my sponsees under a year. They don't need a hall of mirrors yet; they need to not drink today. And not to Danny. He'd hear it as me sending him a book about himself, and that would be its own look.

Thank you for writing it. I mean that.

Maureen

### Stage 2

Dear Aditya,

Second letter. I read the second file this morning before work, pencil again, and I kept the first one open beside it to check myself. Where I quote, I'll say which.

**Which did more, which treated me better.** The chapter did more to me. It's got the face exercise at the end, which is the one thing I've actually used since, and the dumping ground paragraph, which went straight to Danny. The second file has neither. But on the hard middle part, the second file treated me better as a reader. It explained itself where the chapter made me read things three times. So: the chapter for what it did, the second file for how it walked me through Common knowledge. Except in one way the chapter treated me better all through, and that's the outs. I'll come back to that.

**What the second file has that the chapter should get back.**

First, and this is the biggest one for me. In the chapter I refused "Neither half alone would be enough," because Danny dug in no matter what I did. The second file has: "Neither half alone would be enough to get me what I need: both his reaction and my innocence." Well, that's a different sentence. It's not a claim about what makes *him* dig in, it's about what *I* need. My refusal doesn't touch it. I'd have bought that one on the first reading. Put it back.

Second, the opening of Common knowledge. The second file starts it with "A curious question remains: how could the part of me that was provoking him be so sure of his reaction?" That's the question I didn't know I was carrying until I read it. Then: "As hard as I tried to deny it, I knew he was right—and he *knew* that I knew it, which is why he dug in. And, given that part of me *wanted* him to dig in, I had to *rely* on his knowing it." That's the argument laid out so I can follow it with a finger. In the chapter I got to "same kind of thing" by squinting. Here I walked. The bold sentence still put me off, but in the second file it comes with its reasons right after it, "I have to know that he has a better nature to be pushed from, that he can read that I'm turned away from mine and what it implies, that he knows *I* know he can read it (so that it reads as a signal and not a slip), and so on." That's Rick and me in 2006. I could tick off every layer.

Third, on hypocrisy: "But hypocrisy only means something between people who share a standard—and since we're enemies, I tell myself we share none." Clearer than the chapter. And this line, which the chapter doesn't have: "Neither of us had to be told what my look meant, or what turning away is." That's true. Danny never had to be told what my sigh meant. Nobody in my family ever had to be told anything, which was the problem. Keep that.

Fourth, the threat. The chapter says "What I do to you no longer qualifies as harm." The second file says "There's no bottom to what can be done to such a person." I'll tell you, "no bottom" hit me different. In the rooms, bottom is the thing that saves you. A drunk with no bottom dies. So "there's no bottom to what can be done to such a person" scared me in a way the chapter's line didn't. And then the second file comes back to it later: "Remember the threat my look carried, and how there was no bottom to it. That's the thing I normally call "evil"—cruelty for its own sake; the thing I hate most in the world—and I was revealing it to him on purpose." That did something for my flinch at "evil." In the chapter I couldn't tell what you meant by the word, so I held it at arm's length. "Cruelty for its own sake" I can work with. I know that when I see it, and I've seen it in my own "Okay" to Tasha. The chapter should have that definition.

Fifth, "It's still a net benefit to the world!" That's in the second file's encounter and not the chapter's. It made me laugh, and it's true, that's exactly the voice. I've said it about Kyle. I'd keep it.

Sixth, from Whose intention in the second file: "Part of me *knows* it's the same thing, and relies on him recognizing it as such, or else the trap wouldn't work. But if I admitted that to myself, I would be forced to stop—and that is why I never do." "That is why I never do" is the fourth step in a sentence. I'd want that.

Seventh, the bad essence part in the second file ends with "I feel shame in myself and contempt for others, and the cycle continues." That's the two sides of the same coin I see in every newcomer. Shame in and contempt out. The chapter doesn't put them side by side like that.

Eighth, in the spread: "The stupid, the slow and the weak get the same verdict, with better cover: *I'm only being accurate.*" That's me at work with the new assistant, and "I'm only being accurate" is closer to what I actually say than the chapter's "just being honest."
