## Evelyn (64, retired trial judge, AME church; runs cold)

### Stage 1

Dear Aditya,

You asked me to hold things tentatively, and I did. Twenty-two years of juries taught me to wait for the evidence before I rule. So here is my read, in the order it happened, with my own cases where you asked for them.

**The epigraph.** A small thing first. The Gospel of Philip is a Gnostic text. I know that from my reading, and half the women in my Bible study would know it too. The sentence itself is fine. But some readers from my church will stop at the door before they get to you. I kept going.

**The opening.** You won me in the first paragraph, and for a particular reason: you put yourself in the dock before you put me there. "My conviction that I'm nothing like the 'bad people' compels me to act just like them." That is a man owning his conduct first. Then you stated what you meant to prove: "Contempt is the claim that you're a different kind of thing than me, but for it to land, you must be the same kind of thing." I liked that. You told me the charge, so I could hold you to it. I wrote it on the notepad beside me so I'd know at the end whether you had carried your burden.

**Limbo, and my memory.** You asked for something I knew was wrong at the time and couldn't admit until later. I have one, and it is not a small one. In 2009 I sat on a probation revocation. The young man's name was Darnell Hughes. His lawyer, a man named Gary Pruitt, had spent the whole week wasting my time on another matter, filing things he hadn't read and asking questions he already had the answers to. At the revocation I gave Mr. Hughes six months more than I would have given him on another day. I was within my discretion. It was his second violation. The guidelines allowed it. Those were my reasons, and I had them ready before anyone asked.

Did the knowledge that it was wrong feel like new information when I finally owned it? No. It felt like a file I had never opened but always knew was in the drawer. I owned it in the judicial-wellness program, eleven years later, and I recognized it the way you recognize your own handwriting.

So I bought your first claim, not tentatively. And I respected the sentence that followed: "If that doesn't match anything in your memory, the rest of this won't make sense, and I'd rather you know that now." You stated the condition on which the rest depends. Lawyers almost never do that.

Did the knowing come before the reasoning? Before. The reasons came after, and they came from the side that was losing. "Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" has its answer built in, and I noticed. But it is also something I learned on the bench. The party with the facts states them. The party without the facts argues. So I let it pass.

A lawyer's note you might want. What you call limbo, the law already knows. We call it willful blindness. The Supreme Court described it: the defendant takes deliberate action to avoid confirming what he strongly suspects, and the law treats him as knowing. I gave that instruction to juries for years. I never once thought to give it to myself. You are not making up a strange new state of mind. The courts have held for a long time that it exists and that it counts as knowing.

**Better nature.** Here I slowed down. "In fact, I *can't*, when I can clearly see myself in them." I sentenced people I could see myself in. I saw my nephew in some of those boys. I still sent them to prison, and some of those sentences were right. So either sentencing is not "harm" in your sense, or your "can't" is too strong. Later, when you got to the person who corrected you with integrity, I understood you mean the harm in the verdict, not the consequence. But at this point I held it tentatively and wrote a question mark in the margin.

**The cashier.** I pictured the young man at the pharmacy counter at the Rite Aid on Reisterstown Road, the one with the careful handwriting who always double-checks my prescriptions. The smile went through with no reason at all. The sneer would not go. I could not make my face do it until I gave myself a reason: he's slow, he kept me waiting. The moment I had the reason, the sneer came easily. That is a good check. It did what you said it would, and it showed me something you didn't say: how fast I will manufacture the reason when I want the sneer.

**Remorse.** "Can you imagine it even in the worst person?" That is a claim about everyone, and I bristle at those. I have heard men at sentencing say they were ashamed of having been soft. "I should have handled him, Judge. I let him slide and look what happened." Is that remorse, or is it regret about the cost? I honestly don't know. I held your asymmetry for myself. I have never once felt remorse for being decent to someone. For the worst person, I held it as your claim. You came back to it later with "I've hidden good things too, from the part of me that would call them weakness. But only one of those hidings has ever brought me remorse." That went some way toward answering my young men. Not all the way.

That you could do the worst things humans have done: I granted that at once. My church has been saying so since before I was born. "There but for the grace of God." Watching people come through my courtroom taught me the rest. They were not made of different stuff. That was never my difficulty.

**The encounter.** Believable, yes, and in one place it was too believable. "When you look at it that way, it's really more like an *invitation*—to give up his darkness and return to the realm of those who matter." That is what I told myself about Gary Pruitt. I was teaching him. I was holding the bar to a standard. I said to a young clerk once, "If I don't correct them, who will?" And when I asked Mr. Pruitt, in front of his client, whether there was a question anywhere in what he'd just said, and he fumbled, the fumbling proved he was unprepared. "I'm only being fierce, and if he reacts poorly, well, it only proves my point." I have lived that sentence.

"He can't prove I'm doing this, can he? No. And crucially, *neither can I*. So I know it's not on purpose." That is a very good sentence. It is exactly the defense I would have offered for myself, and it is no defense at all.

Where I skimmed: "Ha! Classic projection." and the "he's **bad**, dammit." These are a mind doing its thing, and I see why they're there, but they read as a performance of a mind rather than a mind. I also noticed "here's the thing" and "crucially" in the narration. They didn't bother me there; it is a man talking to himself.

The eyes: "Don't pretend you're doing something noble here." I have seen that look. Mr. Pruitt gave it to me once, from the lectern, and I held him in contempt of court for something else ten minutes later. I had not connected those two events until this morning.

**The cold version.** This is where I stopped reading for a while. "A politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them." That is my son-in-law, Marcus. I am gracious to Marcus. I am more than gracious. I pass him the rolls. I ask about his work. And last year my daughter said to me in the kitchen, "Mama, you're still ruling on him." I didn't answer her. I would say I was waiting for the evidence.

You wrote "If the hot version didn't sound like you, read what follows with the cold one in mind; all of it applies." I don't like being told that all of it applies before I've seen it. But I did read on with Marcus in mind, and much of it did apply.

**The corruption.** "Just before I relabel it disgust or dismay." When Marcus's second business folded in March, I felt something before I felt concern. I called it concern. I told my daughter I was worried for them. I was, and I was also something else, and I named it the respectable thing before I could look at it.

The two checks. "Forget about the "right" or "decent" answers." That instruction helped, because my first answer to anything like this is the Sunday-school answer. Is it satisfying to see evil violently punished? Yes. I have sat on the bench while a man who beat his three-year-old was sentenced, and I felt something warm and settled in my chest, and it was not justice. Justice is colder than that.

Worst enemy. I don't think I have one, in the ordinary sense; judges have parties. The nearest is the attorney who filed a complaint against me with the Commission on Judicial Disabilities in 2014. It was dismissed. The pill or the vengeance? My mouth said the pill before I'd finished the question. Then I sat with it. What I actually wanted was for him to repent, and to watch him do it, publicly, and to have it cost him. I wanted the pill to taste terrible. That was news to me.

A word: "a pill that would instantly enlighten them." "Enlighten" is not my word. It pulls me to a tradition I don't know. I translated it to "bring him to repentance" and went on. You'll have readers doing that translation all through the book, and I'm not sure every one will bother.

"I tell myself it's innocuous, as long as I don't act on it. And that is exactly how I act on it, without seeing that I am." Yes. Bought.

**"I'll go ahead and name what's happening."** Mostly insight, with one passage I had to read twice. "Normally, having my enemy see my self-deception would be horrible. In this case, it is ideal—because it *is* my provocation." Twice, and then I had it. The provocation is the visible gap between what I'm doing and what I'll admit. Once I had it, it was the sharpest thing in the chapter. It's what the courtroom felt when it "went quiet in a bad way," as my clerk put it. They weren't afraid of my ruling. They could see me doing something I would not own.

"He knows exactly what that is: harm that's sure it's good." That landed.

Then a sentence that troubled me, and should have: "He's watching me reject the part of me that knows (I'm causing harm) in favor of the one that can prove (I'm doing good)." I spent my career privileging what can be proved. A courtroom that ran on what someone knows in his heart would be a lynch mob. I believe that still. But you are not talking about the verdict. You are talking about me, in the robe, and there the sentence is true. I let what I could prove about Darnell Hughes (second violation, within guidelines) outvote what I knew about why I was doing it.

"To demote his inner knowing." "Inner knowing" made me wince. It sounds like a workshop. You say "conscience" two lines later, and that's the right word, and it's mine.

"Conscious but unaware—the limbo state: it's all right there to see, and I'm turned away from it." I read it twice. The first time it sounded like a contradiction. The second time it sounded like willful blindness again, which is to say, like the law.

**My first refusal.** It came at "So the only thing that benefits is *it*." I can't buy that. A few pages earlier you showed me exactly what I got: the relief, being right, not having to look at my own motives. You wrote "Well, look what it got me." It got *me* something. You may mean that what I get is counterfeit and what it gets is real, but that isn't what the sentence says. Right around there, too, "It is the part that craves punishing them, and for this very reason: it is how it keeps them trapped" gave this part of me a purpose. That's more than you've shown me. I held both as your claims and marked them unproved.

I could still follow what came after. What saved it for me was "None of this lets me off the hook, though. It's my mind, and I answer for what it does." Without that I would have heard Flip Wilson, and I'd have closed the book: the devil made me do it. With it, I heard Romans 7. "It is no more I that do it, but sin that dwelleth in me." Paul said a part of him did what he hated, and he did not claim to be excused. Maybe you know that passage and are keeping it back. For a reader like me it would carry you a long way.

**"Here's my claim: our better nature isn't one part among others. It's what we most fundamentally are."** Granted as your claim; not found fresh in myself, because I already believe it. The imago Dei. Not grandiose to me at all. I hear grander from the pulpit every Sunday. A Calvinist friend would fight you on it. The Wesleyans, my people, would mostly nod and add "and fallen."

Your evidence was the hiding: "the only thing I would have to hide it from is the part that would object." That's a clever argument, and I went back and forth on it. Then the question: "Did it feel like losing something of yourself, or like getting something back?" I'll tell you truthfully: both. When I owned what I did to Darnell Hughes, I lost the picture of myself as a fair judge, and I grieved it; I'd had it twenty years. And underneath that I got something back. I was lighter walking out of that session than I'd been in a decade. So your answer is the true one, but your question offers only two doors and makes one of them obviously correct. It would have been truer for me if it had allowed for the loss.

"My better nature will simply never fall for it." "Simply never" is you raising your voice. You don't need to.

"Not only can I not fool myself, but I can't fool him, either." You'd already shown me that in the scene. I didn't need it said again.

**The corrector.** This check worked best of all. In 2014, after that complaint, my administrative judge, Clarence Mobley, called me into chambers. He had the transcript. He read me two of my own sentences aloud, without comment. Then he said, "Evelyn, you are not a cruel woman. That was cruel." He was harder with me than anyone had been in years. I did not dig in. I went home and cried, and I enrolled in the wellness program the next month. "It feels like it's reminding me of something—that *you're better than this*." That is almost word for word what he did.

"The fierceness stays; only the poison goes." Yes. A sanction without scorn. It took me too long to learn that the order was doing the work and the sneer never was. But "Whenever I've managed to take it out, I haven't missed it." I did miss it, for a while. The way you miss cigarettes. It is a real power, cutting a lawyer in half with one sentence in front of a full courtroom. I don't want it back, but I missed it.

**Common knowledge.** I followed it, and I wanted to. "Don't worry about tracing the logic" was a little like a judge telling the jury not to trouble themselves with the instructions. I traced it. "Each level closes a way out that the one below leaves open, until his only move left is to protest, which I've already counted on." That is sound, and it described the bench exactly. Mr. Pruitt could not say "Your Honor is being contemptuous of me." He'd have sounded unhinged, and I'd have been within my rights to sanction him for saying it.

Then came the sentence I'd been waiting for since the notepad: "And hypocrisy only means something between two people who answer to the same thing." That is where you carried your burden. You told me at the top what you had to prove, and there you proved it. Hypocrisy presupposes a shared law. If he and I didn't answer to the same law, his look could not have accused me. It did accuse me. I felt it. So we answer to the same law. I'd rule for you on that.

The dehumanizing gaze: yes. A clerk in the Annapolis courthouse in 1983 who looked at me as if I were at the wrong counter. You asked whether he seemed absent, turned away from himself. He did. He looked like a man doing something he'd been taught and didn't quite believe. Then "Is it possible that you look a little bit like this when treating others with contempt?" My former clerk's quiet courtroom answered that before I could.

Where you lost me a little. "Part of us already knows this." That told me what I know, just before you offered the exercise that would let me find it. And "the kind that everyone already agrees on." Everyone does not agree on what good is. I watched a courtroom full of people disagree about it every day for twenty-two years. I think you mean something narrower: how a turning-away reads to the one on the receiving end. That I'd grant. "Everyone already agrees" I refuse.

"If seeing this makes you want to hide, notice that's the same verdict, pointed inward. It feels like accountability. It isn't." My grief counselor spent a year trying to get that into me. Then "to be seen as more than what I do" was Clarence Mobley in one line. That was the best paragraph in the section.

The second half of the section repeats itself. "I am speaking directly to the better nature..." and "Of *course* that's a painful thing..." go over ground you'd already won. I'd keep "What I'm hiding isn't that I *could* do what they do, it's that I'm *already doing it*." That one is a real sentence.

**Self-serving.** The private equity firm and the nursing homes: fair, and you owned it as a suspicion, which I appreciated. Then this: "When I'm sure someone knew exactly what they were doing, I've made them a villain. When I'm sure they had no idea, I've made them a machine." I read that and put the pages down. Deciding which one a person was is what I did for a living. Purpose, knowledge, recklessness, negligence. The law makes me choose, and it should. But I see now that I took the choice home with me, and ruled on Marcus with it.

**Other kinds of contempt.** "The stupid, the slow and the weak get the same verdict, with better cover: *I'm only being accurate.*" That is my sentence about lawyers. I have said "I'm only being accurate" out loud, in chambers, about a public defender who was carrying ninety files. And the part about sending it "about" people to allies: that was chambers, every afternoon, with my clerks. They learned it from me. I am not proud of that.

Then the last section caught me, and it was the right place to end. "For one second the verdict flashes through my eyes, and I make sure they see it, and then I go back to loving them as if nothing had happened." Thanksgiving. Marcus said he was thinking about going back to school for a certificate. I said "That's wonderful," and before I said it, something went across my face, and my daughter saw it, and so did he. Then I passed the sweet potatoes. You have described me more accurately than I would like.

"It has the same flavor as what we call evil." A hard last line. I accept it. "Flavor" is a softer word than the claim, and I think that's deliberate and right; you aren't calling me evil, you're telling me what it tastes like. But the chapter's door, "to be seen as more than what I do," is several pages back. I closed the chapter feeling ruled on. Maybe that's the point, and Chapter 2 opens the door again. I'd want to know it does.

**Length.** Cut a fifth? The second half of Common knowledge, as I said. The paragraph "Turning away from my *better nature* produces a gap in my awareness" is dense and does work the scene had already done. The "Not only can I not fool myself" paragraph. And you have many sentences that begin "Here's my claim" or "My claim is." I counted four. You announced the central one at the top, which I loved, but after that I began to lose track of which claim was the charge and which were the counts. Keep the first. Let the others be claims without the announcement.

Words that made me wince: "enlighten," "inner knowing." "Limbo" gave me a small pause. For a Catholic it's where unbaptized babies went, and it isn't that. But you define it, and after the first page it was your word and it worked.

**Last.** Was any of it obvious? "Hate the sin, love the sinner" is the most worn sentence in my church, so the correction-without-contempt part I have heard all my life. What I had not seen is the machinery. The contempt manufactures the defiance and then cites it as proof. I tell it to myself as an invitation. I can't be caught at it, even by myself, and I take that as proof of my innocence. That is new to me, and it has made something I already knew newly expensive.

This week: Sunday dinner. I am not going to stop being gracious to Marcus. Graciousness isn't the fault. I am going to watch my own face when he talks about the certificate, and see what crosses it before "That's wonderful." And I think I owe my daughter an answer to what she said in the kitchen.

Would I pass it on? Yes, to Dolores Kearney, who sat three courtrooms down from me for fifteen years and who is the only person I know harder on lawyers than I was. She'd fight you at "the only thing that benefits is *it*," as I did, and she'd stay for the corrector and the hypocrisy argument. I would not pass it to my daughter. She'd think I was sending her a message about Marcus, and she would be right. And I'd hesitate to give it to my Bible study, because of the epigraph, which is a shame, because the chapter underneath it would be at home there.

With respect, and with a good deal more self-knowledge than I had on Tuesday,

Evelyn Carter

### Stage 2

Dear Aditya,

I've now read the second file. I'll tell you where I came out and then go through it.

**Which did more, and which treated me better.** They are different answers. The second file did more to me in places, especially the section called "When You Send It" and the two strangers. But the chapter treated me better, and it's the one I'd hand to Dolores. I trusted the chapter from its first paragraph because you put yourself in the dock before me: "I'll narrate what happens in my mind when I confront someone like this." The second file never does that. The man doing the contempt is a stranger, and then he is me. You stand outside, asking questions, and most of the questions already know their answers. I spent twenty-two years listening to lawyers lead witnesses. The second file leads the witness.

A few examples, all from the second file. "Of course not." "Harder, almost certainly." "I mean, *obviously*. Why trust something that can't even justify itself?" "It's hard to do, isn't it?" And "We all know this is how it works" in several forms, with "Because we **all** know it." set on a line by itself. By the middle I was reading with my arms folded. The chapter asks the same things, but mostly as questions I could answer, and when you claim something you call it yours.

Also: the chapter states its charge at the top, "Contempt is the claim that you're a different kind of thing than me, but for it to land, you must be the same kind of thing." The second file never tells me what it means to prove. It proves a great deal, but I never had a notepad to check it against. The chapter was right to add that. Keep it.

**What the second file has that the chapter should recover.** More than I expected. In order of how much it mattered to me:

1. **The funnel, made concrete.** The second file: "Of course not. Deny it, and you're the kind of person who does. Get angry, and that's proof too. Stay calm, and you don't even care. Apologize, and it's too convenient." Then: "Every road funnels into the same conclusion." I don't love the "Of course not." But the four moves after it are the best thing in either version for a reader like me. That is a rigged proceeding, described by someone who has stood in the dock of one. And I've run one. When I went after Gary Pruitt from the bench, there was no answer he could give that would not be used against him. The chapter has the same idea in the Common knowledge section: "Each level closes a way out that the one below leaves open." I followed that, but I followed it as logic. The second file made me feel it, and then made me see myself doing it. That's a check I could run on my own record. The chapter doesn't have it in that form.

2. **The record.** The second file: "And yet there will be no objective evidence of this. The record will show that the second stranger was *harsher*—and so he should have been the one that made it worse." And later: "one that can't be recorded on any ledger or in any transcript, and yet is louder than the stuff that can." You may not know how hard that lands on a judge. When Clarence Mobley called me in, he had the transcript, and the two sentences he read me were bad enough. But the transcript did not have what my clerk meant when she said the room went quiet. The quiet isn't in the record. The chapter says the same thing ("Nothing that can be audited, *even by myself*"), and that's good, but the second file's version names the record, and the record is what people like me trust. It also turns the harsher-but-better second stranger into an argument, where the chapter only asserts it.

3. **The second stranger, harsher.** The second file: "Not more gently. Just that his anger was directed at what you *did*, and not what you *are*." And: "In fact, he could have been *harsher* than the first person—said things that stung more, in front of more people, left you less room to wriggle out—and it still wouldn't have triggered the digging-in response." Mobley was harsher with me than I was with Pruitt, and I thanked him. The chapter's "They might have been *more* fierce than I just was with my enemy" says it, but thinly. And given what your book's title is fighting, "Not more gently" matters. A reader like my Bible study will hear "without contempt" as "nicely" unless you stop them.

4. **"What is that thing whose side you are both on."** The second file: "Whatever he was standing up for wasn't *his*. It wasn't a rule, or a side. It was something you were already on the side of, and he was only reminding you." Then: "What is that thing whose side you are both on; that two people who agree on nothing can both be serving? Hold that question." In my first letter I refused the chapter's "the kind that everyone already agrees on." Everyone does not agree. The second file asks where the chapter asserts, and puts the question where I had the evidence in hand: Mobley and I don't vote alike. It leaves the question open and lets me carry it. That is the fix for the sentence I refused, and it came from you, not me.

5. **"Somebody who had it coming."** The second file: "It didn't feel dark. It felt like justice, a little sweeter than justice needs to be." In my first letter I told you about the man who beat his three-year-old, and the warm settled feeling at sentencing that wasn't justice. The chapter got me there with its punishment question. The second file named it better than I did. "A little sweeter than justice needs to be" is a sentence I will remember. It's measured, and it's exact, and it doesn't call me a monster for feeling it.

6. **The check with no answer built in.** The second file: "Check whether, from here, looking at the flash without the reasons, you still endorse it. If you don't, that isn't a defense." That is a check I can run on my own record, and it respects me: it doesn't tell me what I'll find. I ran it on Thanksgiving and Marcus's certificate. I do not endorse what crossed my face. And "that isn't a defense" is exactly right; it's what I'd have told a defendant.

7. **"Who produced the evidence."** The second file: "He feels good being proven right, in real time, by you. And from the inside, being proven right and being right are the same experience. The only difference is who produced the evidence, and that's the part he can't see." That's a judge's sentence. The whole chapter is about planted evidence, and this is the plainest statement of it in either version. Then: "Which is why it never mattered how small the thing you did was. It only had to be _evidence_." Mr. Pruitt's misfiled motion was never the point. It was evidence.

8. **"Judging you from the state he was judging you for."** The second file: "He was judging you *from* the very state he was judging you *for*." That is the chapter's whole middle section in one line.

9. **The no-bottom argument before "evil."** The second file: "A person who's out of view is a person anything can be done to. There's no bottom." Then: "So a flash of it, even for someone you love, is a flash of **the thing with no bottom.**" The chapter ends on "It has the same flavor as what we call evil." I accepted that, but I accepted it on credit. The second file pays for it. A one-second flash at my son-in-law is a flash of the thing with no bottom. That's why it deserves the word. Without the argument, the chapter's last line is a verdict. With it, it's a finding.

10. **The cover story.** The second file: "*My darkness isn't real darkness—it's the kind all good people must harbor to keep bad people in check. They just take it the wrong way, because of course they would.*" That is word for word what I told my clerk: "If I don't correct them, who will?" The chapter's "invitation" paragraph catches the same move inside the scene, and it caught me. But the second file names it as the general excuse, the one I'd use anywhere.

11. **The disclaimer on the agent.** The second file: "But don't mistake the grammar for the claim: I am not saying there is a literal thing inside him." My first refusal in the chapter was "So the only thing that benefits is *it*." Part of why I refused was that the chapter gave this part of me goals and a purpose without saying how literally I should take it. The chapter does say "it acts as if it has goals of its own," and I should have given the "as if" more weight. But the second file's sentence would have settled me sooner. And its line "The plan is Machiavellian. But he's not the one executing it. He *cannot* be, because the plan requires his absence to function." is a better account of the strange agency than the chapter's.

12. **The ending, and the door.** The second file ends: "It's still there in you, quietly knowing what it knows, patiently waiting to be remembered." And then: "Just as it is in him." In my first letter I said I closed the chapter feeling ruled on. The door in the chapter ("to be seen as more than what I do") is pages back. The second file ends on the door. It also reaches back to its own early line: the conscience "never budged an inch. It kept on quietly knowing what it knew." I'm not telling you to trade endings. The chapter's loved-ones section caught me in a way the second file's didn't, because in the chapter you are the one sighing at your family. But I'd want those two sentences, "Just as it is in him" above all, somewhere after the chapter's last line. Or at least before Chapter 2 starts. I think the chapter's ending and the second file's ending want to be one ending.

13. **Smaller things worth a look.** The second file: "If all you could hear was your own conscience—no justifications to hide behind—could you have even done what you did?" No. Without "within guidelines," I could not have given Darnell Hughes those six months. That question did something the chapter's "Why did one side have to argue" did not. Also, "Would the big ones be easier to recall, or harder?" Harder. Darnell Hughes took me eleven years. And "Again, we normally call it "unconscious." Do you see what incentive we might have to call it that?" asks early what the chapter tells me later. And "Seeing this is the only thing that makes full responsibility safe enough to bear." I resisted "the only thing." But the idea is what my grief counselor built for me over two years, and the chapter only half says it.

**What the chapter was right to leave out or change.**

- The epigraph problem goes away in the second file, since it has none. But the chapter's opening paragraph, you owning your conduct, is the reason I kept reading. That was the right change, and I'd keep it at any cost.
