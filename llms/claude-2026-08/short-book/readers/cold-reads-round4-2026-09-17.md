# Round 4 cold reads of the short book (17 Sept 2026)

Open briefs, fresh readers, clean copy with no TODO notes. Letters saved verbatim.

## 1. A practitioner for whom it is already lived

I read the file in full: preface, introduction, four chapters, the interlude, and all three appendices. Here is the letter.

---

Dear friend,

I read it twice, once fast and once on the cushion, as he asks. It's a real book by someone who saw something true. It is also, in places, doing the thing it describes.

**What's right, and what was new to me**

The anatomy of the sending end is the best thing here, and it was new to me. I knew my contempt was a contraction and that it lied. I hadn't noticed that the hiding is what gets shown. "You have to be present enough to plant the evidence, while absent enough not to recognize it as planted." And from the appendix: "You don't hide the intention and then let it slip; what you show them is that you're hiding it." I tested that against a few memories and it held. It explains why being on the receiving end is maddening rather than merely painful.

The same goes for "Contempt and love are addressed to the same place." That one I can vouch for. The verdict only stings because it is delivered to the part of you it says isn't there. I've felt that from both ends and never had a sentence for it.

The checks are better than most practice instructions I've been given on this. "If the harm simply stopped tomorrow, with nobody punished and nobody exposed, would you feel relief, or robbed?" I asked it about a particular person and got an answer I didn't like. "Something in you would be disappointed if they got better" is accurate and specific.

So are the one-word differences: "You lied to me" against "Of course you lied to me." The clip of someone on my side humiliating someone on theirs still gets me. I still have that "hot little surge," and I had mostly stopped counting it because I wasn't the one sending it.

The shrug, "well, who can really say?", is also well caught. So is this, which I'd want every dharma student to read: "Believing someone has light when you can't see it is its own kind of not-looking." Most books on this subject fall into that ditch, and he fences it off.

He sees the far-end trap too: "*poor you, still in the grip*, which is contempt with a soft face." He admits his own version: "contempt for the people who still have contempt." That is my live edge, and I'd guess it's yours. Among people who have sat for decades, contempt mostly goes to the unpracticed, the politically crude and the other lineage. It gets one sentence in the book. For the audience he names, it should be a chapter.

**Where it's wrong or muddled**

The biggest problem is that Chapter 1 behaves like his first catcher. It works while he stays in the first person. The run from "Is this a bit disingenuous? Maybe a little" down to "*Ha!*" is funny, exact, and recognizable because he is confessing, not accusing. Then he says "put yourself back in the driver's seat." From there it's "you're holding them in the very state that all harm comes from, showing them that you're doing it, and relishing their reaction."

Then come the musts. "You *had* to know this." "I *had* to have." "We *cannot* be fully aware of its operation, by definition." Later: "Not won't; cannot." Those are deductions from his premises, presented as things I'll find when I look. He tells me what I'll find, and then tells me what I'm feeling about it: "the horror you may be feeling is the evidence that none of this is your nature."

"Nothing here is a verdict on you" is only a disclaimer. By his own account, what lands is carried in the address. The chapter's address is a man who needs me to get it. He'd recognize that if someone pointed at it.

Related to that is the certainty. He writes that "certainty is a mark of the other thing," and that the real thing "doesn't need to be sure." Then he writes: "There is one kind of evil, and this is it." "There is only one guide you can trust." "Nice is worse than useless against evil." "It is how it always works." He also writes: "I can't find a hot atrocity that didn't begin there."

The bold and italics are the same insistence in typographic form: "**instantly**", "*genuinely bizarre*". By his own test, the prose is surer than the seeing warrants. The preface knows better: "it is not one that I can claim to be fully living myself, by any stretch." I trust that sentence more than any of the bold ones. I wish the book had its temperature.

The horror is the part I'd most want to talk with him about. He builds it deliberately and then uses it as evidence. My experience differs. The gut-punch was real but it was not the recognition. "I have been darkening the world" still had a very large "I" at the centre of the drama. When the contempt actually released, there was very little horror. It felt more like relief, and often it was funny: I'd see the small prosecutor in there with his files and laugh. There is no laughter anywhere in this book, and that tells a reader which stage it was written from.

A reader earlier on needs to know that seeing this lightens you. Without that, they will do what he himself says contempt produces, and hide.

That leads to who it could hurt. "So there is no neutral. Whenever you're not fully turned toward, you're leaking." The next sentence patches it: "being frightened of the leak is the same move, aimed inward." Another line goes further: "the darkness is operating in you. Not in the past, and not only in your enemies. Now, while you read this, waiting for its chance." And then: "the road to the worst is open, and what's left is time and conditions."

Given to a scrupulous meditator, and we both know several, this produces hypervigilance with a dharma gloss. A one-sentence patch will not hold against a paragraph of dread.

The self-contempt paragraph is excellent: "*you are what you did.* It feels like accountability". But it is one paragraph at the end of Chapter 3. For people who sit, self-contempt is the main form, and it should come before the heavy part of Chapter 1.

The parasite is my next objection. He knows the problem: "Reifying it is a mistake, but treating it as a mere absence undersells the urgency." He also writes the best sentence on the subject: "a groove in attention so old that its shape looks like intent." That is habitual tendency, and nothing more is needed.

But after it the parasites "want," "collaborate" and are "relieved." Fierce refusal "gives the thing nothing to eat." People practice with the working image, not the disclaimer, and the working image here is a war: "Fight it with everything you have."

You know what happens when a student fights an affliction. They suppress it, and suppression is his limbo under a new name.

The actual instruction arrives on the last pages, in three sentences: "look at the contempt itself, not the person. Where is it? What's it made of? In my experience it doesn't survive that, because there was nothing there to fight." From where I sit, that is the whole method. It should come first, not serve as a coda after forty pages of battle. The ending, "there was never a second thing there: only the light, turned away", is right. The route to it trains the opposite reflex.

He has also handed readers a new name to call each other. I predict someone will say "that's your parasite talking" within a week of the first reading group.

What I'd have said differently is mostly about emptiness. The book is almost all compassion and sameness: "this one is like me." That is equalizing self and other, and it's true and good. But the verdict "this is all you are" did not stop forming in me because I became confident they were good underneath. It stopped because "what you are" no longer found anything to land on, in them or in me.

His fix for a bad essence is an innocent essence underneath it. I understand the Uttaratantra warrant, and his Appendix 3 is careful. The practical risk is this sentence: "the only solution is to recognize (and actualize) our innate goodness." An identity as good is what contempt defends. He says as much himself: "Trying to simply be a 'good person' often recreates the idea of 'bad people'". Yet "know that care is what you are" can become the subtlest edition of the same identity.

His fear account also says "You only ever turn away from someone because you suspect, below words, that care might *not* be what you are." That "only ever" does not match what I found. Under most of my contempt was helplessness and grief. Despising someone was easier than feeling the heartbreak of harm I could not stop. He mentions grief once, in passing, and offers one root as the only root.

A few more places where I winced or got lost:

- **The part that knows versus the part that proves.** This is useful, but the Hitler paragraph is glib: "He's the perfect example of the opposite. *Thought.*" Plenty of cruelty is done by gut, with the victim in plain view. "keeping them in view is what it *is*" makes the claim true by definition. He is honest about this in the back: the Gödel footnote calls it "a problem the book carries," and Appendix 3 gives real marks for "fully seeing." But a newer student's "just knowing" is usually the klesha, and the main text hands them a licence.

- **The far end.** "So the most lost person is carrying *more* of that knowledge than you are, not less". He says "I can only report", but he has no place from which to report it. He hasn't been that person.

- **The choice paragraph in Chapter 3.** "The want was innocent; the label was borrowed." I read it three times. The turning "can't have been chosen," there is "one choice, and it's real," and "you chose it knowing better, and you did." My own looking finds a small flinch that can be caught. He says that too, more clearly, under "how fast you catch it."

- **Common knowledge.** The Introduction's footnote says "yes, common knowledge in the game-theoretic sense". The appendix footnote says "The encounter itself is not common knowledge in the game theorist's sense" and "shared experience is enough." He should pick one, and the second is the true one.

- **"Fierce."** Everyone believes they are the second catcher. He knows it: "Fierce is where the darkness finds it easiest to get in". But the model he offers is someone "harsher," "in front of more people." Wrathful activity comes late on the path. For most of us, most of the time, the honest instruction is to say less until the extra ingredient is actually gone.

- **The Introduction as a whole.** It opens with conclusions, the Devil and "evil global conspiracy" before the reader has looked at anything. "We are seeding **evil** into the world" is on page one. I'd cut it and open on the stranger's eyes.

- **The conspiracy and "meaning crisis" passages.** They read as assertions from a different book.

- **Tonglen as "the second catcher done as a discipline."** That isn't quite right. Tonglen undoes my self-cherishing; it doesn't catch anyone.

- **Editing notes left in.** "Place late in the book" and "Chapter 6's claim" are still there.

**Who it would help and who it would hurt**

It would help a practitioner with a stable seat and a righteous streak, the activist sangha member who is sure their anger is clean. The interlude and the Chapter 4 checks can do in an afternoon what took me years.

The section for people who were hurt is careful and mostly earns it: "Your rage, your refusal, your distance". Still, "distance" is one word. I would not hand "stay turned toward" to someone still living with the person who hurt them.

It would hurt the shame-prone and the scrupulous, and anyone who takes "fight" literally.

Appendix 2 deserves real credit. "So that claim is false, and I withdraw it" is the most trustworthy sentence in the file. The same is true of the caution that non-fabrication is not "permission."

If he wants collaborators, I'd tell him four things. Stay in the first person. Move the looking instruction to the front. Put relief on the page beside the horror. Trust that the reader was already using it; he says they were, but the prose doesn't act as though he believes it.

With love,
—

## 2. A practitioner who half sees it

I read the whole file (preface, introduction, four chapters, interlude, three appendices) and nothing else. This is the letter, written as the practitioner you described.

---

Dear M.,

I read the book you sent in two sittings. I went back on a third morning and did the exercises on the cushion, as he asks. I'll tell you what happened in order, because my reaction changed as I went, and the changes are probably the useful part.

The preface disarmed me. "Alas, it is not one that I can claim to be fully living myself, by any stretch." I trust a writer who starts there.

Then the introduction nearly lost me. By the third paragraph I'm told that when I see my enemies as other, "in however small a way, we are seeding evil into the world." The word evil is in bold. A few lines later: "I want to show you that you already know them, and how *believing that you don't* is what enables "evil" to keep operating through your blind spots." At that sentence something in me folded its arms. If I agree, I knew it all along. If I disagree, my disagreement is the mechanism. I hadn't formed an objection yet and it already had a diagnosis. The same introduction also says to read it "as a kind of poetry," and then says contempt is "the purest expression of evil." I couldn't tell which contract I was under.

Chapter 1 turned me around, and it did it by asking questions. I used a real person. You know who: D., from the board. I held his face. "Something in you wants this verdict to *land*; to register deep inside. Can you feel that?" Yes, I could, and I didn't enjoy it. What I've always called "seeing D. clearly" has a wish inside it that he know what I think he is. That was new to me as something I'd noticed, and it was not new as a fact. I suppose that is his point.

The hot first-person monologue ("they're **bad**, dammit," "*Ha!*") slid off me. It's a cartoon of someone angrier than I am. My contempt is cold and polite. I write people off. Reading that passage I thought "not me" and relaxed, which can't be what he wants.

What got me was quieter, and it was about my wife, not D. "The sigh that lets them know what we really think of them in that moment, dressed up to make us seem like the reasonable ones. The look on their face that tells us it landed, which we'll pretend not to have seen." I put the book down, because I do that. I know the exact sigh, and I do check whether it landed. No argument was needed there. I wish the whole chapter trusted that the way that passage does.

Limbo is the best section in the book. I remembered taking credit for a colleague's analysis in 2019. When I finally admitted it to myself, it was not new information. "Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" is the best single question he asks, and I've carried it all week.

I also tried the small experiment. I tried to be dismissive toward my wife while actually looking at her. I couldn't hold it, and it fell apart into something like embarrassment. The instruction is ambiguous, though: "Try it, with something small, with the person in front of you." For a moment I thought he was asking me to go and harm somebody.

The places where I felt handled are all in one register. "That's not something an unconscious process (in the normal sense) does, is it?" Well, yes it is. Habitual patterns defend themselves. Any of us who has watched a klesha dodge the looking knows that. He says so himself two chapters later: "A habit that lives by going unseen will push back when seen." So why lean on me in Chapter 1?

Then there are the "had to" sentences. "I saw that I knew it, fully well, even as I did it. I *had* to have." "You *had* to know this, for that dark message to function or even be coherent." Where I had found the thing myself, as with the sigh, I didn't need a "had to." Where I hadn't, "had to" felt like being told what was in my pockets.

In particular, I did not find the claim that part of me needs D. to dig in so I can use his reaction as evidence. I found one memory where that is plainly true. I did not find it as a general fact about me. He goes from "you know contempt stings" to "you are planting evidence" faster than I could check.

He also tells me what I'm feeling. "The horror you may be feeling is the evidence that none of this is your nature." I wasn't feeling horror. I was feeling interested, a bit caught, and a bit skeptical. Being offered a rest stop for a horror I didn't have put me outside the book for several pages.

There is a lot of bold and a lot of "only," "always," and "all." He writes that "certainty is a mark of the other thing." Then he writes "As far as I have ever seen, it is the only thing that works," and "There is only one guide you can trust." His voice is most believable when he says "in me," "I can't," and "I don't have it." It is least believable when it is sure.

The interlude is where he earned the most from me. I ran the pill test on D. and did not get the answer I expected. If the harm simply stopped and he understood what he'd done, with nobody ever knowing, I felt robbed. I want him exposed in front of the people who believed him. Most of them have left the center. It would protect nobody. That is the draw, and it is in me.

The clip test caught me too. I forwarded one of those last week. And "well, who can really say?" is my shrug, word for word, the one I use to end the thought.

Then comes this sentence: "If you find none of this, good; I have no way to argue with that and won't try." That bought him more of my trust than anything in Chapter 1. If he wants to know what leaving room for the reader looks like, it looks like that.

The two catchers are true to my memory. A retreat manager once took me apart in front of the kitchen crew. I left straighter, not smaller. But look at what he says about the first catcher: "he needed you to feel it, and he'll never look at that." How does he know? He is inside a stranger's head, passing something close to a verdict. I noticed it because the book had just taught me to.

Chapter 2 is where I disagree, and I have practice reasons. "Whenever you're not fully turned toward, you're leaking, and what leaks is what the sigh carried." He titles that one "So there is no neutral." Most of my day is non-recognition, and so is most of yours. The tradition distinguishes plain dullness from aversion mixed with pride. His own Appendix 3 describes the first not-recognizing as "a momentary blankness, with no motive given." So there is a neutral in his account too, and it comes first. I think he has folded all three poisons into the aimed one.

"There is one kind of evil, and this is it" went past me too quickly. What about the addict who steals from his mother while seeing her perfectly well, and weeps? The appendix says "a drive gets outweighed while you're still looking, and this one can't be." I'm not sure. I have snapped at my daughter when exhausted, and I believe I saw her fine.

He deserves credit for giving three marks for "fully seeing" and for saying what would count against him. But that sits at the very back of the book, while Chapter 2 speaks with much more confidence than the appendix does. The appendix says outright: "It does not get you that care is what awareness *is*." Chapter 2 reads as if it does.

I should add that the claim matches my glimpses. In the clearest hours on retreat there was a tenderness toward everyone that I hadn't produced. I'm inclined to believe him. I'm wary of the speed of the inference, not of where it lands.

Chapter 3 is mixed. He handles the parasite word well for someone like me: drop the creature, keep "a groove in attention so old that its shape looks like intent." Fine. But then the parasites "all want the same thing: a darker world," and the reason given is that two absences "have nothing in them to differ by." That is wordplay. An absence can't want anything. He says so and then keeps telling me what it wants.

The institutions passage is the weakest thing in the book. "At some level, we need them to remain." There is no exercise for it and nothing to try. The introduction promised I'd reach that claim "after you'll have watched it happen between two people." I didn't watch it happen. I was told.

The same goes for "What isn't obvious, and what I can only report, is that the knowing doesn't shrink." He is reporting it from where? He hasn't been the most lost person. The hostage at the window is a moving image, and I want it to be true, which is why I'm holding it at arm's length.

"You think your contempt is punishing what they became. It's building it." I think that is out of proportion. What builds a violent man is mostly what was done to him as a boy, not the sighs of decent people. Inflating my guilt is, by his own account, how you make someone hide.

The passage addressed to people who were hurt is the best-judged writing in the book. "The verdict is the one thing you don't owe them, and it's the one thing their parasite needs from you." I copied that out. It takes nothing from me. I can still think D. should never sit on a board again, and I do.

On responsibility I'd like more explanation. Chapter 1 says I'm "fully responsible" and that there is a paradox to hold. Chapter 3 says the turning "can't have been chosen," then that there is one real choice, then that "all evil is innocent." The closing version is that responsibility lives in how fast you catch it and what you repair. I recognize that as familiarization. But I had to assemble it from three places.

Chapter 4 is the practical one. "*Of course* you lied to me." I have said that exact "of course."

I checked the claim that "the innocence you can see in others is a measure of your own freedom, not of their goodness" against my old boss. I despised her until I managed people myself. She hadn't changed.

And this: "Which is also its own trap, and the one I fall into most: contempt for the people who still have contempt." By page ten I had a list of three people who needed to read this book. That was my tell.

His last instruction is one we already know: look at the contempt itself. "In my experience it doesn't survive that, because there was nothing there to fight." I sat with D. in mind and looked. I found heat in the chest and a set jaw. Under that, to my surprise, was something like fear that I'd been a fool to trust him. It loosened. It did not dissolve, and it was back by lunch.

So his report is rosier than my result. He does say elsewhere: "The first several times I found nothing. Then one day I caught it." I'd put those two sentences closer together.

There are some loose threads. The introduction's footnote says "yes, common knowledge in the game-theoretic sense." Appendix 1's footnote says "The encounter itself is not common knowledge in the game theorist's sense." One of them should give way. My own checking found only the first two or three mirrors, which the appendix admits is all the trap needs.

The appendices also refer to things I never read. There is a "Chapter 6." There is a flash "shameful to admit out of proportion to its size." There is a "corpse case" cited before it is introduced. Appendix 2 still opens with a note to himself about where to place it.

This week I'll put the relief-or-robbed question to D. each morning. I'll watch for the half-second after I say something sharp at home, when I check whether it landed. I'll stop forwarding the clips. And I'll do tonglen with D., which I have been avoiding for a year, and I now know why.

I'm not signing on to "no neutral," or to the conspiracy chapter, or to the idea that my sigh feeds atrocities. I still can't stand him. But I no longer think that feeling is information about him.

Thank you for sending it. Tell your friend that the questions work, and the "had to" sentences get in their way.

Yours,
R.

## 3. A relative novice (a year and a half of sitting; anxious, self-critical)

I read the whole file (preface, introduction, four chapters, interlude, three appendices) as the reader you described. Nothing else was opened or changed. The letter follows.

---

Dear M.,

I read it over about nine days, with a two-day gap I'll explain. You said it mattered to you, so here is what it was like for me to read, not a book report.

Mostly I felt it was written for you and I was reading over your shoulder. He says so at the start: "This version is for friends who sit, and it assumes things a general reader might not grant: that the mind has a nature, that the confusion covering it is not the same thing as it, and that recognition is available on the spot rather than at the end of an argument." I sit, so I kept going. But I don't grant those things so much as hope they're true, and that mattered more than I expected. Then: "If it provokes the recognition, the rest is detail." I spent the rest of the book checking whether I'd had the recognition. I don't think I did, and I'm not sure I'd know.

The opening exercise worked, to my surprise. "A place somebody is looking out of" is a lovely phrase, and on the cushion I got something like it. For the atrocity version I used my brother-in-law, then the man in the news you know I can't stand. "This is all you are" was right there. Then came the sentence that got me: "Something in you wants this verdict to *land*; to register deep inside. Can you feel that?" I could. I'd always told myself I just think he's a bad person, which sounds like an opinion. The wanting it to land is not an opinion, and I hadn't seen that before. If I keep one thing, it's that.

The monologue after it ("they're **bad**, dammit, and this proves it") made me laugh in a wincing way. It helped a lot that he was doing it to himself. Wherever he says "this is what I found in me," I relaxed and looked. Wherever he says "you," I tensed.

The "common knowledge" idea lost me both times. "Go ahead and check that in yourself—not only that you know that contempt is dark, but that you can rely on your target to know it, and for them to know you know it, etc." I could check the first layer. After that the "etc." carries it and I was nodding along, not seeing. "Because we **all** do" felt like being told the result before I'd looked. The game-theory footnote might as well have been in another language. I gather this is the central idea, so you should know it's the part I least have.

Limbo was the best section for me. I used the time I let a colleague take the blame for something that was partly mine. "Did it feel like new information?" No, it didn't, and I'd never looked at it that way. "Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" is a great question and I'm still chewing on it. One thing confused me: "Try it, with something small, with the person in front of you." Is he asking me to actually harm someone a little? I didn't do it. I wasn't sure what I was meant to do.

Right after that it sped up: "Limbo aimed at a person *is* malice. Malice that needs to be seen, and needs to deny itself, is contempt. And the darkness is what both are made of." Three definitions stacked in three sentences. I wanted him to slow down.

Then came the stretch that made me feel bad, and I've tried to sort the useful bad from the merely bad. "So you *know* they're redeemable, and you're blocking their way back." "You were cutting off access to the one part of themselves that could have saved them." "That's how the thing behind the atrocities is fed by a sigh." You know how I am. My inner prosecutor took these as exhibits. I spent a whole day monitoring my sighs. I stopped reading for two days, which, to be fair, he says to do: "If you need to stop here for a while, stop. This is the heaviest part, and it doesn't get heavier." I was glad of that sentence, but it arrives after three paragraphs of piling on. I'd have liked it earlier.

Here is what I most want to tell you. His consolation is "the horror you may be feeling is the evidence that none of this is your nature." For you that's probably a floor you can stand on, because you've glimpsed the nature. For me it's an IOU. I could feel the indictment directly, but I had to take the reassurance on faith. I suspect that's what happens when someone at my stage reads the version written for someone at yours.

There's also the "you knew" problem. "I *had* to have." "You *had* to know this, for that dark message to function or even be coherent." "Again, none of this should be new to you, if the message ever worked." Some of it was new to me, or felt new. The "had to" sentences left me two options: I'm lying to myself or I'm slow. The limbo exercise showed me I already knew something. The "had to" sentences only told me I did. It felt like being argued into a memory.

The worst sentence in the book for me is in Chapter 2: "**So there is no neutral.** Whenever you're not fully turned toward, you're leaking." He follows it with "being frightened of the leak is the same move, aimed inward." I understand why. But I was already frightened by then, and now the fright itself was the darkness. Later, "To the degree it's there, the road to the worst is open, and what's left is time and conditions," followed by "That isn't said to frighten you." It did frighten me. "Now, while you read this, waiting for its chance" in the interlude was just creepy. None of those told me anything the gentler passages hadn't. They only turned the volume up.

The bad feeling that was useful came from the small, specific things. "The takedown you reread after posting it." The clip of someone on my side humiliating someone on theirs, and the "hot little surge": I watch those all the time and send them to people. I've noticed it twice this week, and the second time I didn't send the clip. "If the harm simply stopped tomorrow, with nobody punished and nobody exposed, would you feel relief, or robbed?" About the man in the news: robbed, no question. About my brother-in-law it's more tangled. I'd want him to see what he did, and I'd also feel robbed if nobody else in the family ever knew. That stung, but I can work with it. "The first several times I found nothing. Then one day I caught it" was kind, because it gave me permission to find nothing.

The most practical page is the "extra" page: "*You lied to me.* With the extra: *Of course you lied to me.*" The day after reading it I said an "of course" to my partner and caught it about an hour later. Which connects to the sentence that encouraged me most: "What is yours is how fast you catch it afterward: days, then hours, then sometimes in the moment." That is a task sized for me. I wish the book had more of that and less of my place in the darkening of the world.

The two people who catch you gave me my first felt sense of what he means by "the light" that wasn't borrowed from a podcast. I had a manager years ago who tore a strip off me in front of the team, and I walked out, in his words, "a little more myself." I'd never understood why that didn't feel like humiliation. The Chapter 2 exercise worked for the same reason, where you feel *where* someone's contempt was trying to reach: "Contempt and love are addressed to the same place." I could actually check that. But then "We already know how to do this, because we *are* it" is a jump. I recognized it in her. I can't get from there to "I am it."

My favourite sentence is the one about the guide: "It's what's there when someone is fully in front of you, and you're not sure of anything except that they matter." And "certainty is a mark of the other thing" helped with a private worry, which is that my conscience and my inner critic sound alike. My critic is extremely certain, so maybe that's how to tell them apart. I wanted more on this.

Chapter 3 was mixed. I read the argument that turning away can't be chosen four times and it still reads like a logic puzzle. I did try the eye exercise with my partner. We couldn't find the moment either, and we ended up laughing, and I don't know what that proves. "The want was innocent; the label was borrowed" I don't understand. But "that isn't shame's shape, *I am it*. It's remorse's, *I did it*" is going on a sticky note.

"Absolute goodness" and "the relative" are terms I only half have. But the next paragraph landed harder than anything after Chapter 1: "You only ever turn away from someone because you suspect, below words, that care might *not* be what you are."
