## Peggy (67, Sister of Mercy, spiritual director, prison ministry; cold, the scrupulous courtesy)

### Stage 1

Dear Aditya,

I read your chapter at the kitchen table after Lauds, with a pencil, and then again in the chapel before the examen. You should know who's reading, so you can weigh what I say. I'm sixty-seven. I've been a Sister of Mercy for forty-five years, and for twenty I've gone into a men's state prison every Tuesday. I've sat with men who killed people. I've sat with the mothers of the people they killed. I close books quickly when a writer is sentimental about evil or hands out grace like parish bingo prizes. I didn't close yours. I'll try to tell you why, step by step, and where it went wrong for me too.

**The opening and the memory.** Your first paragraph made me wary. "My conviction that I'm nothing like the 'bad people' compels me to act just like them" is the sort of sentence I've heard from young retreat leaders who have never been inside a cell. Then you wrote "Maybe you'll recognize something of yourself in it. Or maybe not." That loosened my shoulders. I held the central claim the way you asked, tentatively, and went on.

The memory came at once, which I didn't want. In 2011 a man I visited, Tommy Reale, wrote a letter to the family of the boy he killed. He asked me to get it to them through the victim services office. I held it for five months. I told myself, and told Tommy, that the Brennans "weren't ready." I knew at the time that wasn't true. I was the one who wasn't ready. I didn't want to be the woman who handed a murderer's letter to a grieving mother, and I didn't want her to look at me the way I knew she would.

Did owning up feel like new information? No. When I finally said it aloud to my director, it felt like setting down something I'd been carrying with both arms. You wrote that the knowledge "lived in a strange kind of limbo, in which you simultaneously knew and yet somehow didn't." I bought that whole. I'll admit the word "limbo" made me smile; we Catholics had a place by that name for unbaptized babies, and it was abolished, more or less, in 2007. A state between, without punishment and without the face of God. It suits better than you may know.

Your question "If you did, did that happen passively?" caught me. No. I changed the subject every time Tommy asked. I scheduled the victim services call for weeks I knew I'd be on retreat. That was work. And "Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" is one of the best questions in the chapter. The side that knew never said a word in five months. The other side wrote me memos.

"If seeing that makes you want to call yourself names, hold off. That's not owning up; it's another way to hide." Yes. I give that counsel to scrupulous retreatants, and I was glad to see a layman know it.

**The cashier.** I tried it with the young woman at the Weis Market on Saturday, the one whose name tag says Kayla. The smile and the thank-you went straight through. The sneer wouldn't go. Here's what interested me: a reason arrived almost before I asked for one. She'd been slow with the coupons. I didn't go looking for it. It was handed up to me. So I bought your claim, but not quite in your form. The cruelty did need a reason, and something in me was standing by to supply one. That matched your "another part was busy providing reasons" better than I'd have liked.

**Remorse.** You asked whether I've ever felt remorse for having been decent. No. I've felt scruple about kindness that broke a rule; as a young sister I gave a man in county my convent phone number and confessed it. That was the fear of disobedience, not the sickening sense of having gone against myself. I've also heard men in the block say they hated themselves for showing mercy in the yard. One, Curtis, told me he felt "soft" for days after he let another man off a debt. I sat with that one for a while. I think it was fear and shame, not remorse. He wasn't sick at having betrayed what he was; he was afraid of what others now thought he was. So I bought "it only points in one direction" tentatively. I'd want you to know that men in prison will test it on you, and the line holds, but only if you know the difference between remorse and shame. Your sentence "that sickening sense of having gone against myself" draws that line well. Keep it.

"So some part of me needs to believe I'm made of 'better stuff.'" Bought. I've had forty-five years of habit and a veil to prove it.

**The Encounter.** Believable, yes, and uncomfortably so. I skimmed nowhere until the end, where I slowed down because I was ashamed. The line that stopped me was "The message *this is all you are* carries a subtle but primal threat: *... and therefore your wellbeing has ceased to matter.*" I have watched that threat pass between a corrections officer and a nineteen-year-old at the visiting room desk. I have watched it pass, I'm sorry to say, between me and Monsignor Hanlon across a conference table at the chancery.

"It's really more like an *invitation*" made me laugh out loud, alone in the kitchen. That is exactly the story I tell. "Surely that's not what I'm doing. I'm just being fierce with evil, right? Right." I know the "Right" with the period. I've said it to myself in that voice.

The swearing ("he's **bad**, dammit", "*Shit.*") didn't bother me. It sounded like a mind. I've heard worse in the chapel at Graterford.

**The cold version.** This is where I live, and you gave it one paragraph. "A quiet filing-away, a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them." That is my scrupulous courtesy at diocesan meetings, described by a man who has never seen me at one. I put the pencil down.

Then here is the first place I refused. "It's the same verdict, delivered by withdrawal, and they feel it just the same." I didn't believe it of Hanlon. I believed he never noticed me at all; men in his position are trained not to. I held that refusal for a few pages. I could still follow everything after it. Then in "The spread" you wrote "And I send it *about* people, to my allies, with nobody's face in view," and I understood my courtesy was never for him. It was for the other sisters at the table, so they'd see how carefully I wasn't saying it. So I'll grant you the verdict, but not "just the same." Some targets don't feel it. The room always does. And my sister in community, Sister Clare, the one I think of as all theology and no mercy, feels my gentle corrections in front of everyone. I know she does, because she goes quiet in a particular way. I've been pretending not to see that, which is your line later about the look on their face that tells me it landed.

**The Corruption.** You asked whether this is insight or lecture. Mostly insight, and dense. I read the second paragraph twice: "He feels it as a maddening combination of messages—*I know exactly what I'm doing* and *I'll never own up to it*." The second time it opened. "In brief: I am in limbo about provoking him, and showing him that I'm in limbo is itself the provocation." That I understood on the first reading, and I think it's the sentence the rest of the section is explaining. You might trust it more.

"If you've been on his end, you know how it closes around you. Deny it, and you're the kind of person who does. Get angry, and that's proof too. Stay calm, and you don't even care. Apologize, and it's too convenient." I've been on his end, from the other side of a parole hearing, watching a board do this to a man. Every response he gave was evidence. I've also done it to a parishioner. That paragraph is true.

"My stories kept changing; my conclusion never did." Good. Short and true.

"If I'd looked carefully, I'd have seen my role in causing it. So I didn't—and that *was* my role in causing it." I'm keeping that one for a talk, with credit.

**The two checks.** "Is there something primally, viscerally satisfying about seeing evil violently punished?" Yes. I'm not proud of it.

My worst enemy, if I'm allowed one, is Gerald Hanlon, who was vicar general when they moved Father D. from parish to parish and wrote letters about his "health." Two buttons. Here is what happened, and I want you to have it exactly. I reached for the pill first, and felt holy doing it. Then I noticed that in my imagined pill there was a clause: his changed heart would have to understand fully what he'd done, and suffer it. I'd built the vengeance into the mercy. Then a second thought arrived, a theological one: a pill that changes a heart violates free will, so I couldn't press it in good conscience. That reason came very fast, and it would have let me press nothing at all, and keep my grievance clean. I recognized your "another part was busy providing reasons" again. So yes, some part of me was itching for the first button, and the rest of me was dressing that part for Mass.

"I'll be honest: there is in me." That sentence opened me more than anything before it. You named your own sin first. I can follow a man who does that.

"And that lie is exactly what lets me act on it, in ways I almost never recognize." Bought. I acted on it in August of 2018, when the grand jury report came out and Hanlon's name was in it. I read his section twice, slowly, the way you'd eat something sweet.

**Whose intention.** I followed "Conscious yet unaware: that's the limbo state," and I bought it from my own Tommy letter. "If it weren't, I'd have been embarrassed or confused when he called me on it" is a good test, and I'll use it on myself.

Then "It acts as if it has goals of its own, and it pursues them intentionally." I didn't refuse this; I heard something older in it. Saint Ignatius, in his rules for discernment, says the enemy of our human nature behaves like a false lover who wants his words kept secret, and is undone when they're told to a good confessor. That's your part exactly: it needs the dark, it needs not to be named. So I didn't need the disclaimer, but it came in time and I was glad of the second half of it: "None of this lets me off the hook. It's my mind, and I answer for what it does." Ignatius wouldn't have let me off the hook either. A secular reader may need that sentence more than I did. I'd keep it right where it is.

"*Yes, we ultimately cause harm through the same mechanism—but I'm going to get away with mine, while making you rot for yours.*" That one I'd bring to a cell. Not to the man, but to myself on the drive in.

"When I'm sure someone was fully aware of what they were doing, I've made them a villain. When I'm sure they had no idea, I've made them a machine." This is true and it's the error I see every week. The victims' families make the men villains. The reform people make them machines, products of their circumstances. Neither one sits across from the man. I'd have underlined it twice if the pencil weren't dull.

**The bad essence.** "I want to ascribe all this to some kind of 'bad essence,' which is a convenient way to stop further investigation." This caught me, and didn't read as heading off an objection. It's Augustine, of course: evil as a privation, an absence of good, not a substance. I already knew that. I've taught it. What I hadn't done was notice I don't believe it about Gerald Hanlon. "Here's what makes it especially difficult: when it's operating, it looks and behaves exactly like you'd expect a 'bad essence' to look." Yes. That's why the doctrine is easy to hold in a classroom and hard to hold at the chancery.

"I don't think this part of myself is ultimately there to help me get away with stuff. I think that's just a byproduct of that same fear." I read that twice and still wasn't sure what you meant. It's the one sentence in the section I'd call unclear.

**The way out.** "What does 'yourself' mean here?" I tried it in the chapel, in the way I'd do a centering prayer: not thinking about it, just letting the question sit. What I found: the one who hid the Tommy letter from herself, and the one reading your page, and the one kneeling, were not three. I didn't find a theory. I found that I couldn't get a knife between them. That's as far as I got, and it was further than I expected.

"Why would you have to hide something from *yourself* in order to keep it from your better nature?" This made me stop for a long time. I hid it from myself because I was the one who'd have objected. That's the only answer I could find.

"And when you finally owned up to what you'd done, did it feel like *losing* something of yourself, or like getting something back?" Getting something back. Unambiguously. I went to the prison the next week and gave Tommy the date I'd mailed it. He said, "Took you long enough, Sister," and laughed, and I felt more myself than I had in months.

"Our better nature isn't one part among others. It's a description of what we *are* in the absence of all self-deception." I found this in myself, or rather, I recognized it from Merton, who called it the true self, and from my own prayer. It didn't sound grandiose to me, because I've heard it said with more grandeur by better-known men. I did notice I have to hold it alongside what the Church teaches about the wound in us, and I'm not sure you'd mind that. Your "in the absence of all self-deception" leaves room for the wound to be self-deception, which I can live with. Later, the "advisor" passage made me hold it more firmly. I'll come to that.

**The gaze.** I've been on the receiving end, from Mr. Brennan, Kevin's father, at a victim-offender dialogue in 2013. He looked at me as if I were on the side of the man who killed his son. You asked, "Can you feel the sense in which he was nonetheless absent, or somehow 'turned away' from himself?" With Mr. Brennan, honestly, no. He was entirely present, and entirely in pain. I'd want you to be careful here: a grieving father's look is not always contempt, and I wouldn't let anyone tell him it was. So I tried it instead with Hanlon's look, across the table, the one he gave me when I asked about Father D. in 2004. There I could feel it. He was polite and he wasn't there.

"Why would he have to turn away at all, if what he is, is essentially bad?" I tried to answer it, and couldn't, which I suppose is the point. If Hanlon were simply bad, he could have looked at me plainly. He had to fold his hands and look at the agenda. Something in him was being kept out of the room. I hadn't thought of that in twenty years and it changed how I remember the meeting. That's not a small thing for a woman my age.

"Is it possible that you look a tiny bit like this when treating him with contempt?" Yes. That's the courtesy again.

**Integrity.** "Think of a time when someone corrected you with integrity." My superior, Sister Agnes Mulcahy, in 2016: "Peggy, your mercy has a verdict in it." I sulked for a week. I'll be honest and say I dug in a little at first. But there was nothing in her look that said I was the kind of thing that does that. So the digging-in had nowhere to stand, and it fell over. "It feels like a reminder—*you're better than this*." Yes, that's what it felt like, after the week.

"Contempt isn't what does the stopping. It's a little darkness I inject into otherwise-good behavior, poisoning it." Bought. "It's possible for the fierceness to stay while the poison goes." Bought, and needed. If you'd left out the fierceness I'd have closed the book as cheap grace.

The time I corrected someone badly: Sister Clare, last spring, at house meeting, when she quoted the Catechism at a sister whose brother had just died by suicide. I corrected her gently and audibly. I have not yet found the better approach. I know it would involve not having an audience. Your question didn't give me the answer, and I was glad you didn't try to.

"It's said that integrity is what you do when no one is watching... But someone always is: me." I've always said God is watching, and I think I used that to make the watcher someone outside me, whom I could argue with. You put him closer. "And I knew it was there the whole time." Yes.

**Common knowledge.** I followed it, slowly. The step that held me was the hypocrisy one: "But hypocrisy only means something between people who share a standard—and since we're enemies, we might share none. Except there is one we share, or his charge couldn't have landed." That's a real argument, and it's from the inside. I haven't seen it put that way. It added something: I'd always taken the shared standard on faith. You took it from the fact that the charge stung.

Then the question about where someone's contempt reached in me. It was Mr. Brennan again, I'm afraid, though I said his look wasn't contempt. Perhaps some of it was. It didn't reach my pride. It reached the part of me that believes I'm a decent woman who does God's work. "This reaches further in, for the *you* that knows you count." Yes.

"**Love and contempt are addressed to the same place.** One says *I know you're in there.* The other says *there's nobody in there*, and they both say it to the somebody who is." This is the sentence I'd bring to a man in a cell. I'd bring it to Curtis on Tuesday. I'd bring it to the COs if they'd let me. It surprised me, though once I'd read it I felt I'd been watching it for twenty years.

The last time I sent the look: a Lenten talk in March, at St. Anne's, when a man in a good coat said, "Lock them up and throw away the key." I told him the story of a man I'd sat with who taught himself to read at forty. I aimed it. I knew he'd read the aim. I counted on his seeing that I was being kind, so he couldn't call it an attack. Your three questions about that are cruel and correct, and I answered yes to all three.

**Evil, cruelty, sadism.** I accepted "evil" throughout; you don't use it lightly, and you use it on yourself. "The very essence of cruelty" I flinched at, then accepted, because you said it about your own eyes. If you'd said it about someone else's I'd have stopped trusting you.

"It's why I don't think sadism is a different kind of thing, but the same thing at full strength." Here I stopped. I've sat with one man, I'll call him D., who enjoyed what he did, and told me so, and smiled. I didn't feel he was the same kind of thing as me. I felt cold. Then I remembered reading Hanlon's section of the grand jury report slowly, the way you'd eat something sweet. Is that the same thing at a lower strength? I think it may be. I don't like it. I'll grant it, because you framed it as what you found in yourself and not as a law, and because I found the start of it in me. But I'd want you to know that a reader who has sat across from real sadism may balk here, and you're asking her to look at something she's spent years not looking at.

What I missed in the whole chapter, and I'll say it plainly: the victim. "My target has just been cruel to someone who didn't deserve it." Then that someone vanishes. You never come back to her. When I sit with a family, their question is not whether my contempt entrenches the man. It's whether anyone remembers their son. I'm not asking you to answer an objection; I'm telling you that I noticed the absence and it made me wonder whether you've sat across from it. The chapter didn't tell me.

**The advisor and "the same thing."** "I treat it like an advisor offering an opinion, which I can weigh against other practical concerns and set aside if the price is right. But it's not an advisor." This is the moral life of every committee I've sat on. "The moment I pretend that listening is optional... I've already committed to the harm." That one went into me. It's what the chancery did for thirty years: weighed it, and set it aside, with reasons.

By "the same thing" I took you to mean what I'd call conscience, and what Newman called the voice of God in us. You never say God. I noticed you don't, and I respected it, though I also wondered whether you'll have to say what it is eventually. "Part of me knows the difference between the 'good' I've invented to excuse myself and the good we each recognize. I just tell myself I don't." Yes. The good I invent is always the one that lets me be courteous to Hanlon.

"If that rubs off on others, great." That "great" was too light for the weight before it. It sounded like a man in a hurry.

**The spread.** "What gets passed along isn't my look, or anything that resembles it. It's the turning away itself, and the permission it gives." I've watched this in the prison, generation to generation, and in the chancery. It caught me.

"I send it to people I love." I sent it to my sister Eileen on the phone last Sunday, about her husband, a sigh. I know the sigh. "The look on their face that tells me it landed, which I pretend not to have seen." That one hurt. That's Clare going quiet.

"The stupid, the slow and the weak get the same verdict, with better cover: *I'm only being accurate.*" Kayla and the coupons. You got me twice with her.

"Sometimes it looks like me writing this book *at* people, instead of *for* them." Thank you for that. It's the line that let me trust the rest.

The ending is right. "What I need in order to look is what my enemy needed from me, and what the corrector with integrity gave me: to be seen as more than what I do." That's where it should stop, and it does. It's also, I think, what a man in a cell needs, and what Gerald Hanlon needs, and I don't like that last part, but there it is.

**Where you pushed and where I was free.** I felt free in the opening questions: "Or like something you had always known, but somehow hadn't *let* yourself know?" and "Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" They left me to find my own answer, and I did. I felt free in "Whether that number ever goes to zero is something you can discover for yourself." I felt pushed at "If that doesn't match anything in your memory, the rest of this won't make sense," though I understood why it's there; at "Contempt only works because we already know we're the same kind of thing," which arrives in bold before I'd finished checking it; and at "As best I can tell, the only solution is to see through that illusion." And, most of all, at the cold version's "they feel it just the same."

**Length.** It drags in "Whose intention," between "Conscious yet unaware" and the bad essence. "The same thing is happening in my enemy" comes up three or four times. The limbo is explained in the opening, in The Corruption, and again in Whose intention; by the third time I knew it. If I had to cut a fifth, I'd cut "Here's what makes it especially difficult" and the paragraph about the byproduct, shorten the second paragraph of The Corruption, cut the "Here's what I've found in myself" paragraph near the end (it reads like a summary), and cut "If that rubs off on others, great." I'd also give the cold version more room and the hot version less; but I would say that.

**Words.** "Combo" made me wince; it's a fast-food word in a serious paragraph. "Mash" is fine. "Net benefit" is good in the monologue. "Better nature" I'd have called conscience, but I see why you won't.

**Last.** Was it obvious? Some of it. I've known evil as privation since novitiate. I knew conscience isn't an advisor. What became newly consequential: that my courtesy is a verdict, and that it's for the room. On Thursday there's house meeting, and Clare will say something I think is merciless. I'm going to say nothing in front of the others, and ask her to walk with me after. I don't know what I'll say. I'll try to find out what she's turned away from, if anything, before I decide she has.

Would I pass it on? To Father Joe Kelleher, who does the Catholic services at the prison, yes, this week. He'll argue with the sadism line and he'll be better for the rest. My first thought was to give it to Clare. I caught myself, and I'm not giving it to her. That was the verdict wearing a bookmark.

With gratitude, and a dull pencil,
Peggy Doyle, RSM

### Stage 2

Dear Aditya,

I've read the second file now. Most of it is the same chapter word for word, so I read it the way I'd read a second draft of a homily: looking for the places where it breathes differently. There were fewer than I expected, and nearly all of them mattered.

**Which did more, which treated me better.** The chapter did more to me. The second file treated me a little better in two or three places, by saying less. But the chapter has the passage that went deepest, and the second file doesn't have it at all.

That passage is the advisor. In the chapter: "I treat it like an advisor offering an opinion, which I can weigh against other practical concerns and set aside if the price is right. But it's not an advisor." And then "The moment I pretend that listening is optional—that I have the right to look at what is right and say not today—I've already committed to the harm." I told you in my first letter that's the moral life of every committee I've sat on, and the history of my diocese. The second file goes straight from the sadism lines to "My justifications are believable only to me and my allies." Reading it without the advisor, I felt the chapter lose its floor. The claim that our better nature is what we are, not one part among others, was something I'd granted from Merton. The advisor passage is where you earned it for a reader who hasn't read Merton. Without it, "it's what we most fundamentally are" in the second file is just asserted twice.

I'd also miss "I'm just another person who knew what was right, decided it was negotiable, and then looked for someone worse than me to prove I wasn't so bad after all." That sentence is the whole chancery in one breath. It's also me reading Gerald Hanlon's section of the grand jury report. Please don't lose it.

**What the second file should give back to the chapter.** One thing, and I'm less sure of it than of anything else in this letter. The second file has a question the chapter doesn't: "In the opening, were you surprised that I knew you needed limbo to cause harm? If not, why not?"

The first time through I didn't know what it referred to. You hadn't said, in the opening, that I needed limbo to cause harm; you'd asked me to recall a memory. Then I went back and saw it. You'd predicted my deflection before I told you about it. You'd known I changed the subject when Tommy asked about the letter, and I wasn't surprised you knew. Why not? Because I know it of everyone. That's your common knowledge, proven on me, the reader, rather than argued about you and your enemy. It's the only place in either version where the argument turns and looks at the person holding the book. I'd keep it, but the wording made me stumble; "I knew you needed limbo to cause harm" isn't quite what the opening did. Something nearer to what you actually asked would let it land the first time.

**Where the chapter is better.** The hypocrisy step. The second file has: "He charged me with hypocrisy, and I knew his charge was valid. That only makes sense between two people who answer to the same thing." The chapter has: "But hypocrisy only means something between people who share a standard—and since we're enemies, we might share none. Except there is one we share, or his charge couldn't have landed. Whatever else divides us, we answer to the same thing." The second file states the conclusion. The chapter lets me make the objection myself, enemies share nothing, and then shows me why the sting says otherwise. I told you that was a real argument from the inside. In the second file it's a claim. Keep the chapter's.

The marrow. The second file: "But between the two people facing each other, it's obvious." The chapter: "But the person on the receiving end knows the difference in their marrow—and so do I." The "so do I" is the reason I trust you. The second file's line is about two people. The chapter's puts you in the dock. That's the version for a woman who's spent years watching people stand at a distance and describe what's obvious.

"Every enemy this part of myself helps me make gives that fear somewhere to point other than at me." That's only in the chapter. I didn't mention it in my first letter, but reading the second file without it, I felt the gap. It's what Hanlon was for me for twenty years. He was somewhere for my fear to point. I'd keep it.

"I feel shame in myself and contempt for others, and the cycle continues." Also only in the chapter. It ties the bad-essence idea back to the scrupulous retreatant and to the end, where wanting to hide is the same verdict pointed inward. I'd keep it, though I didn't feel its absence as strongly.

"If that's true, think how someone who has completely forgotten it might behave—and how most of us would feel compelled to treat him." The chapter's ending to that sentence is better. The second file stops at "might behave," and I thought of D. and felt cold, and nothing told me to look at the coldness. The chapter's last clause turns me back on myself, which is where the whole chapter keeps rightly turning me.

**What the second file was right to leave out.** The byproduct paragraph. The chapter's "I don't think this part of myself is ultimately there to help me get away with stuff. I think that's just a byproduct of that same fear." I told you I read it twice and still didn't follow it. The second file doesn't have it and I didn't miss it.

And this one, where I think the second file is better: in The Corruption, the chapter says "And at some level, we both know what that state is for: doing harm while feeling righteous about it." The second file ends "And at some level, we both know what that state is for." and stops. I did know. I'd known since the cold version, since my courtesy at the chancery. When you told me in the chapter, it was a half-second of being handed an answer I already had. In the second file I supplied it, and it was mine. That's the second file treating me better, and it's the kind of thing a man in a cell would notice too. Men inside can always tell when they're being told what they already know.

**Order and length.** The second file is shorter by the advisor section and a few sentences, and it didn't feel lighter in a good way. It felt like the argument skipped a step at the place where I most needed it. So the comparison taught me something about my own cuts: I'd have cut the wrong fifth if I'd cut the advisor. My cuts from the first letter still stand: the byproduct paragraph, "Here's what makes it especially difficult," the "Here's what I've found in myself" summary near the end, and "If that rubs off on others, great."

On order, the advisor passage sits well where the chapter has it, right after the sentence where "bad people" becomes the name for the people you feel okay doing this to. That sentence leaves me exposed, and the advisor tells me how I got there: by treating conscience as one voice among several. The second file goes from the exposure straight to "My justifications are believable only to me and my allies," which is a step back into argument too soon.

If you put the reader's question from the second file into the chapter, I'd place it where the second file has it, right after the bold line in Common knowledge. That's where I was ready to be asked.

Nothing in the comparison changed what I'm doing Thursday with Clare.

Yours in Christ, and in some impatience for Chapter 2,
Peggy

Aditya,

I read this in one sitting on Sunday night, then reread parts of it Monday morning before my men's group. That timing matters to what follows, so I'll be honest about it.
