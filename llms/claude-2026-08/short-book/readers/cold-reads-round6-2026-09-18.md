## Renata: 44, ER social worker, not a Buddhist; Quaker meeting, Thurman and Weil, five years of IFS therapy, immigration volunteer

### Stage 1

Dear J.,

I read your friend's opening on Sunday after meeting, at the kitchen table, with a pencil, and I did the exercises with real people. I'll tell you what happened in order, and then answer his questions plainly, because I think the order is the most useful thing I have to give him.

**What happened**

The first line got me. A face that "stops being something you're looking at and becomes a place somebody is looking out of." That is as good a description as I've read of what Friends mean by that of God in everyone, and he got there without any of our vocabulary. I was with him.

For the atrocity I didn't need to invent anything. I used the Border Patrol agent in the video from a few years back, the one emptying our water gallons into the dirt and smiling at the camera. I've left those gallons. I looked at his eyes and no, I could not find the recognition, and yes, "this is all you are" is the sentence. It is word for word what my face says. So the first page works on me.

But notice the small thing on that same page. "Something in you wants this verdict to *land*; to register deep inside. Can you feel that?" He told me, and then asked. It was true, so I let it go. It happens a lot from there on, and I let it go less each time.

The monologue. Yes, it's believable, and yes, it's mine. "It's not like he's a mind reader—so if he accuses me of it, it can only mean one thing: he's projecting. Classic." I laughed out loud, the unhappy kind. "Not that I'd take any pleasure in that." Also mine. The one beat I couldn't follow was the "invitation" paragraph; I read it three times and I'm still not sure whose voice is talking. And the scene isn't enough to see. "I'm arguing with a political opponent" is all I get. No room, no subject, no face, not even whether it's a dinner or a comment thread. He asks me to look into a man's eyes and then doesn't give the man any. My own case filled it in, but only because I had one ready. Also, "when I'm in your shoes" tripped me: it assumes I've just done the thing, when all I did was imagine a stranger.

Then the cold form, and here I sat up, because that is the one I actually live in. "A politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them." That is me with the man who comes in by ambulance four times a month for a sandwich and a bed. I am never rude to him. I am correct with him. He knows. It took me two years of therapy to say that sentence and your friend said it in a line. And then he left it. It gets one paragraph, and almost everything afterward is built for the hot case: aiming it, keeping the enemy there, needing the reaction. When I write a patient off I am not working to keep him anywhere. I'm leaving. In Chapter 2 he says "I was working hard to keep him there, which only makes sense if part of me thought he could leave," and I held it against the cold case and it didn't fit, and the text didn't seem to know that. Tell him people in my line of work mostly don't have the hot kind at work. We have the cold kind, all shift.

After "I'll just go ahead and name what's happening," the chapter changed for me. The monologue was a man talking. This was a man working something out on a whiteboard, with me watching. Some of it landed: the good kind of darkness, "the kind all good people must harbor to keep *real* evil in check." I have said almost that to my husband about my volunteer work. But by "we know they know we know" I had stopped checking anything against my life and was just trying to keep up. He seems to know, because the next line is "You don't have to trace the logic." If I don't have to, I'd rather not have been walked through it.

Limbo. A small thing first: I was raised Catholic, and limbo is where the unbaptized babies went. I had to set that aside every time the word came up. The exercise itself worked. I used the time I documented a patient as "non-compliant" when I knew perfectly well he had no way to get to the pharmacy. Did it feel like new information when I admitted it? No. "Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" is a very good question, and he left it as a question. That's him at his best.

The grace passage is true and I needed it, and this sentence is one I'll keep: "Nobody ever looked honestly at something in themselves while being told they were worthless for having it." That is five years of my therapy in one line. But it sits in an odd place, and the voice in it is different, more like a facilitator: "the thing we're studying," "in what follows."

The interlude is the best thing here. I'll come back to it.

Chapter 2 I read with my husband as the case, not the agent, because he offered that door ("take one with someone you love") and I knew which one was costing me more. This passage is what I'll carry out of the room:

"The look on their face that tells us it landed, which we'll pretend not to have seen."

And then: "you went back to loving them as if nothing had happened." I put the pencil down. That is a thing I do. I have never seen it written. It's buried four-fifths of the way through Chapter 2, introduced as an afterthought ("And before you file this under things we do to enemies"). For me it was the centre.

**His questions**

*What's working, what isn't.* Working: the opening page, the monologue, the cold-contempt paragraph, the Limbo questions, the whole interlude, the loved-ones passage, "nice" versus "kind—fiercely kind," the little prosecutor with his files (I know that fellow; in my therapy he has a name), and every place the author says what it cost him. "There are times I still fall into it" bought a great deal of trust. Not working: the back half of Chapter 1, which explains what the monologue already showed; the number of times I'm told what is in me; and the repetition across the three parts.

*A real recognition.* Two. The loved-ones sentence above. And in the interlude, the ticket machine: "the look you trade with whoever's standing next to you... *I'm not that.*" That's the look I trade with the charge nurse when a certain name comes up on the board. Nobody did anything wrong. A small, pleasant lift. I had filed that look under gallows humour, which we're allowed. I don't think I can file it there any more. The pill test also did something: I was sure I'd take the pill for the agent, and then I noticed I wanted him thirsty first. One honest flicker. He asked and then said "I know there is in me," which is the right order.

*One voice or stitched.* Stitched, and I could point to the thread. "Okay, back to the scenario. That's the strange thing from before": I had to page back to find which strange thing. Limbo reads as a separate piece set into the chapter, and the chapter has to clear its throat to get going again. "We just checked that darkness is essentially a kind of turning away": we didn't check it, he said it and invited me to. Contempt gets defined three times (first page; "We call that level *contempt*"; then again under "What it is"). "There is no good kind" arrives at least four times. "It's all a single move" twice. The second conversation under the spoken one in Chapter 2 is the hall of mirrors from Chapter 1 again. "The two parts from the Limbo section" and "Chapter 4 takes that up" and "the next chapter is about it" are a man managing a manuscript, not talking to me. "That's a major thesis of this work" is a different man altogether, a lecturer. The footnote about the Fundamental Attribution Error is a third man. And "collective shadow" walks in from Jung with no introduction. In the second test he says "think of two times you were caught" and then seems to mean one time and two catchers, and the pronouns slide in the middle ("I left a little more myself. It weakened the grip of *your* darkness"). "Only one of them knows better" I couldn't parse.

*Does each of the three earn its place.* Chapter 1, the first half entirely. The interlude entirely; if I could only keep one of the three it would be that. Chapter 2 is half necessary and half being told again. What I'd keep if it were cut hard: the invitation to bring a case; the paragraph about being present enough to plant the evidence and absent enough not to see it planted; "What you knew about them," including the punch to the gut; the conscious/unconscious paradox with nice versus kind; the loved-ones passage, moved much earlier; and "Why" from "For me, that knowledge was too horrifying" to the end. What I'd lose: the taxonomy under "What it is," most of "What is doing this" except the paragraph that begins "You don't have to believe any of that," and the big block quote about humanity's sacrifice, which sounded to me like the author moved by his own sentence. The plain line next to it does more: "I needed to know that no, some people really are just fundamentally different."

*Where I was told rather than asked.* "Something in you wants this verdict to land." "Usually the latter." "If the above section was hard to admit, this one will be even harder." "If I know that, why do I still do it? That's the wrong question" (that one stung; it was my question). "Few things are as primally, viscerally satisfying as punishing evil." "The darkness is operating in you. Not in the past." "Nobody, underneath, believes their contempt is righteous." "We all know what it is, while pretending not to." "You have now watched the alternative recruit you": have I? "If this is horrifying, notice why: because **you don't actually want to be doing it**." That last one is probably true of me, and it's the one I most wanted to arrive at without a hand on my back. What he does well, he does by going first: "I know there is in me." "What I did was dig in." "If you find none of this, good; I have no way to argue with that and won't try." When he talks like that I'll follow him anywhere.

*Words and beliefs.* "Darkness" and "the light" cost me nothing; they're my words. But two claims arrived as settled that I hold only as hopes. "All harm begins by turning away", where he says he can't prove it, good, and then five paragraphs later it's "we just checked." And the end of the interlude: "It is the light. We already know how to do this, because we *are* it." I believe something close to that on First Days. I still felt the floor tilt, because nothing on the page had got us there; the second catcher got us to "there is something I recognise at once and can't define," which is plenty. "A hint about your true nature" is the same leap, smaller. And here is my one real disagreement, from the therapy side: he calls it "a dark part of my mind," something that commandeers. When I finally met the part of me that despises, it wasn't dark. It was exhausted and it was guarding grief. He gets there in one clause ("some people find tiredness there instead, or grief") and I wanted that clause to be a page. A reader like me will trust him more if the despiser is treated as something that can be looked at with the same eyes as the stranger, which I think is actually his point.

My colleague Marcus, who is the best charge nurse I know and thinks church is a hobby: he'd be fine through the monologue and Limbo, he'd do the interlude tests and grin at the clip one. He'd slow at "It is the very *root*," stop reading charitably at "we *are* it," and close the file at "evil" and "commandeers your mind." The paragraph that would have kept him ("You don't have to believe any of that as a fact... It's a stance") comes after the place he'd have left.

One last thing, maybe the most important for someone like me. I fight people for a living and on my weekends. The whole way through I was braced for being told to stop. The sentence that let me breathe is in the second-to-last section of Chapter 2: "That need is the only part to drop. The outrage at the harm stays, and so does the fight to stop it." Chapter 1 says he can be "fierce and uncompromising" without the darkness, but he never shows me one time he was. The only picture of it in all three parts is the second person who caught me. I believed her. I'd like to see him do it once.

I'm not left anywhere dark, for what it's worth. Sober, and a little tender toward my husband.

With love,
Renata


### Stage 2

Dear J.,

I've now read the other version. I'll call the one I read first A (with the interlude) and this one B.

**Which is better**

A, and for me it isn't close, for three reasons.

First, B has no interlude, and the interlude is where the thing happened to me. The pill, the clip you want to send to someone, the ticket machine and "*I'm not that*," the two people who caught you. B goes straight from the author's whiteboard to "bring a case of your own," and I arrived at Chapter 2 having understood him and not yet having caught myself. In A, I arrived with the look I trade with the charge nurse already in my hand. B also has nothing that shows me what the alternative looks like. The harsh second catcher in A is the only picture anywhere of fierce-without-contempt, and without her, "fiercely kind" in Chapter 2 is just a phrase.

Second, A asks where B tells. Put them side by side:

- B: "If it is, then you *know* they're redeemable, and you're blocking their way back." A: "If it is: what did part of you believe about them, to need to work that hard?" With B's I felt a hand close on my arm. With A's I actually went and looked, and found that with my husband, yes, part of me believed he could hear me, or I wouldn't have bothered with the sigh.
- B: "If you look carefully, you'll find that limbo contains knowledge of itself." A: "If so, limbo contains knowledge of itself."
- B: "My darkness cannot survive my looking directly at it, by definition." A: "In my experience, my darkness doesn't survive my looking directly at it." "By definition" is a man winning an argument. "In my experience" is a man telling me something.
- B: "This was all there to see, if you had looked... by turning away from your better nature, you turned away from theirs." A keeps all of that in the first person: "It was all there to see, when I finally looked." When the hardest paragraph in the book is about *him*, I can stand next to him and look. When it's about me, I'm in the dock.
- B: "through the common knowledge we've just traced, some part of you already knows that it cannot be anyone else's, either." A: "And if it isn't yours, it's hard to see how it could be anyone else's." A's is one of the best sentences in the opening. B's is a proof I'm told I've already accepted.
- B: "It's a tremendous relief when that sinks in." A: "For me it was a tremendous relief."

Third, A has the paragraph that would keep Marcus in the room, and B doesn't: "You don't have to believe any of that as a fact. Not that the darkness is a thing, not that 'bad people' are under its control... It's a stance." Where that paragraph sits in A, B has 2 Corinthians and Satan masquerading as an angel of light. I know the verse; I was raised on it. But he has just told me the darkness is "not a literal metaphysical entity," and then the very next voice I hear is Paul's, naming the entity. B also has the aside that everybody's recognising the look is "a hint about the nature of awareness itself. I say more in Chapter 3 and Appendix 1." That is exactly the kind of thing that makes me close a book: a large spiritual claim dropped in parentheses with a forwarding address. A was right to lose both.

And B's pause ("Sit with that for a moment... If you need to stop here for a while, stop... If what you're feeling is horror, keep hold of what it's for... remember what that is"): I know it's meant well, and "it doesn't get heavier" is useful to know. But it told me what I was feeling and what my feeling was evidence of, before I'd felt it. I do this to patients' families when I'm tired. It's handling. A trusts me to put the pages down on my own.

**What B does better**

Several things, and I'd want them back.

*The hinge into Limbo.* This is the biggest. In A, Limbo is announced with "There's something strange going on here—and the easiest way to show it is by example," and afterwards, "That's the strange thing from before: I was in limbo," and I had to page back to find what was strange. B asks an actual question first: "How do I show him something I don't (and *cannot*) know about myself, and moreover, *that* I don't know it?" and then answers it on the far side: "The answer to the question *how did I both know and not-know what I was doing* is, of course, limbo." (I'd lose "of course.") With the question in place, Limbo has a job. That's the main seam I named in A, and B doesn't have it.

*The confession inside the monologue.* B has this and A has lost it:

"And truth be told, something deep inside knows they're right. But I absolutely cannot afford to admit it, least of all here. I don't even want to believe I have that kind of darkness in me—the kind that would forsake a fellow being just to prove I'm right about them."

"Forsake a fellow being just to prove I'm right about them." That's the line in B that stopped me. It's the whole book in a clause and it's in his voice, mid-squirm. A's monologue is funnier and more alive ("He thinks *I'm* the bad guy. *Ha!*", "Not that I'd take any pleasure in that", the mind-reader bit), and it has a real man in it rather than "them." B's speaker never even tells me who he's talking to, and then "they" silently becomes "he" at "My target recognizes," which is a seam of its own. So: A's monologue, with that one passage from B put back.

*"Because it lives in me, too."* B's naming begins: "I'm seeing genuine darkness in them. That part is accurate. And I know the harm it can cause in the world. Not for some abstract reason, but because *it lives in me, too.*" A opens the same section with "A dark part of my mind—a part that *needs* to see him as bad—is *revealing* itself to him," which is where I felt the whiteboard come out. B's way in is warmer and it's how I actually know what I know about the agents: I recognise it.

*The plain recap.* I didn't care for "the stack" as a word, or for being told "You watched the stack get built." But the sentence inside it sorted out the beat I couldn't follow in A: "So I call it an invitation. He sees through that, so I call it being fierce. He still won't let it go, so I call it projection." That's the monologue in three moves. A never gives me that, and I needed it.

*First person in "How you sent it."* B: "In my case the aim was the giveaway. I couldn't have placed it so well if I hadn't known what I was doing, and I couldn't have held their reaction against them if I had." A turns this into "If you were fully unaware, you couldn't aim it so precisely..." and follows it with "Your target can see the state you're in even though, in an important sense, you don't. Because you don't," which I couldn't untangle. Here B is the one going first, and B is clearer. Likewise B's "If I knew I'd planted the evidence, not only would it not exonerate me; it would implicate me" is cleaner than A's paragraph ending "That's not what we usually mean by 'unconscious,' is it?", a question that isn't really one.

*Two small bridges.* B opens "What is doing this" with "I was talking about it as if it had a stake in the matter. I meant to," and opens "Why" with "My darkness needs enemies so that I never look in here. Here is what that was like from the inside." Both tell me why the section is there. A starts those sections cold.

**What's the same in both, and still wrong**

Everything I said in my first letter about the back half of Chapter 1 and the repetition in Chapter 2 holds for both. B is, if anything, more of a lecture ("There is nothing *to* turning away except knowing and not-knowing"). Both have "That's a major thesis of this work," the footnote, "collective shadow," "Chapter 4 takes that up." Both give my kind of contempt, the cold kind, one paragraph and then build everything for the hot kind. Both bury the people we love near the end.

**If I were handing one to a friend tomorrow**

A. And the first thing I'd change is the join around Limbo: put B's question in before it and B's answer after it, so the chapter stops clearing its throat. The second, the same afternoon, is to put "forsake a fellow being just to prove I'm right about them" back into the monologue. Then I'd start cutting Chapter 2 for the things I've by then been told three times, and move the passage about the people we love up to where he first invites me to pick a case. That passage is where most of us actually live.

Tell him thank you. I mean that. I was short with my husband on Thursday and watched his face and, for once, didn't pretend I hadn't seen.

With love,
Renata


## Paul: 61, retired finish carpenter; eleven years of Soto Zen, nine of Dzogchen, twenty-two years sober

### Stage 1

Jim,

I read your friend's pages over three mornings, at the bench, with coffee, and I did what he asked me to do. You know I'm slow. I'll tell you what happened and you can pass along whatever is useful.

I used my brother. You know the story. For the stranger's eyes at the start I used a man I see at the transfer station, and the first part worked: the face stops being a thing and somebody is in there. Then he says run it again with the atrocity, and I put Danny's face on it, and the thing went out like a pilot light. So he had me inside the first page. "This is all you are" is the right sentence. The men's group took a year to get me to those five words and he had them on line twenty.

Then right away he lost a little of what he'd earned. "For the rest of us: notice that instead of seeing someone whose wellbeing matters, now there is only something rotten." I hadn't noticed yet. He noticed for me. Same with "Something in you wants this verdict to land" followed by "Can you feel that?" That's backwards. Ask me, then shut up. And "Usually the latter," about how the other person reacts. He asked a good question and answered it himself before I'd put my coffee down.

**The monologue.** Is it what a mind does? Yes, the moves are right. "It's really more like an invitation" is dead on; in the rooms we'd call that holding somebody accountable with a knife behind your back. "Not that I'd take any pleasure in that" made me laugh, because I have said it. But the sound of it isn't my mind. "Classic." "Ha!" "he's bad, dammit." That's a man acting out his thoughts for an audience. Mine never talked that loud. Mine said, "I'm just done with him," very calmly, for twelve years. And I couldn't see the scene. "A political opponent." Who? His brother-in-law at a cookout? A man at work? What had the fellow just said? He tells me he can see it in the man's eyes, so they're in a room, but there's no room. He asks me to bring somebody real and he brings a category. If he gave me one kitchen and one sentence the man said, I'd believe everything after it more.

My kind is the cold kind, and the cold kind gets one paragraph. It's a good paragraph. "A politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them" is me with Danny exactly, and "they feel it just the same" is true; he felt it at the funeral and I made sure. But then the whole rest of the chapter is built on the hot case, with gazes and eyes and defiance, and I had to keep translating. A lot of people our age with some practice behind them have no hot contempt left. We have the cold, and it passes for equanimity.

**Where I left.** The stretch after "I'll just go ahead and name what's happening." It gets clever. "The (possibly unique) case where revealing my self-deception actually benefits me." "We know they know we know." "Limbo contains knowledge of itself, in limbo." I don't doubt he's seen something. But I was no longer looking at Danny, I was following a man doing a proof, and once I'm following a proof, I'm not in my own case anymore. He even says "You don't have to trace the logic." Then don't put it there, or put it after the hall-of-mirrors feeling, which I did recognise.

**Where I came back.** Limbo. That section is the fourth step, and it's cleaner than the Big Book. I used a job in '98, a widow in Barre, hours I padded while I was still drinking. When I finally wrote it down for my sponsor it was not news. "Something you had always known, but somehow hadn't let yourself know." Yes. And then this, which is the sentence I'm carrying out of the room: "Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" I've heard rationalisation described a thousand times and never that simply. The part that knew never made a case. The part that padded the hours had three. I also did the one where you picture the person in front of you and try to use them with no excuse in hand. It won't go. That's a real exercise. It's worth more than the two pages of argument on either side of it.

The "little grace" part says the right thing. It is the difference between a fourth step and a beating. But "let's give ourselves a little grace" is a facilitator with a flip chart. And "Nobody ever looked honestly at something in themselves while being told they were worthless" is a big "nobody ever" from a man who mostly is careful to say "in my experience."

**Seams.** You asked if it's one voice in one sitting. Mostly one voice. Not one sitting. What I tripped on:

- "Okay, back to the scenario. That's the strange thing from before." I had to page back to find what strange thing. Limbo and the grace section are dropped into the middle of his case, and you can see the saw cut on both sides.
- "We just checked that darkness is essentially a kind of turning away." We didn't check. He said "I can't prove this to you" and asked two questions. Then the cheating example shows up there, late, when it belongs back in Limbo where he first mentioned cheating.
- The paragraph about being on the receiving end of a dehumanizing gaze. "Doesn't the person feel somehow absent." Which person? I read it three times before I understood he means the one doing the looking.
- In the second test: "Think of two times you were caught," then "imagine two different people catching you." Remember, or imagine? Two times, or one? And inside it he goes "What did you do? What I did was dig in," and later "I left a little more myself. It weakened the grip of your darkness." I and you are trading places sentence by sentence.
- The interlude opens "So far it's been my case." Chapter 2 opens "Chapter 1 was my case." But it wasn't only his case. He had me bring my own person on the first page, again for the pill, and then Chapter 2 asks a third time as though it's the first.
- Chapter 2 announces "I will use another word for what I've been calling darkness: evil," makes a speech about why, and then three sections later says "I've been calling it the darkness, and I'll keep the name." One of those was written on a different day.
- "We'll come back to why," "Chapter 4 takes that up," "the two parts from the Limbo section," "That's a major thesis of this work," "If this book works." Every one of those is him stepping out from behind the page to show me the blueprint. And the footnote about the attribution error: he doesn't need a psychology textbook to vouch for him.
- "The light" walks in at the end of the interlude as if we'd been introduced.

**The three parts.** The interlude earns its place more than anything else here, and I'd tell him that first. The pill: I tried it with Danny and found that what I want isn't for him to see. I want him alone at the end, the way Mom almost was. That's not justice and I knew it sitting there. "The small wait, after you've said it, to watch it land." I did that to apprentices for thirty years. And the ticket machine, the "small, pleasant lift. I'm not that." Jim, that is word for word what happens in me when a new person at the centre asks Rinpoche's translator about chakras. No wrongdoer, nothing to punish, same flavour. He's right that it's the same thing, and nobody in nine years of teachings has put it to me that small. The "who can really say?" shrug is good too.

And the two people who catch you. My first sponsor, Walt, took me apart in front of the Tuesday group for making an amends "with an invoice attached." Harsher than anybody has ever been with me. I walked out taller. A senior monk at the Zen place once corrected my bowing in a perfectly mild voice and I carried it for a month. "Almost the same words. Opposite outcomes." That's true and it's the heart of the thing. "What were they serving? Could you have defined it?" is a real question and he lets it stay one, for about four lines.

Then: "It is the light. We already know how to do this, because we are it." Speaking as somebody who's had that pointed out by a person qualified to do it: I don't think it's wrong. I think it's cheap there. In my tradition you don't say that sentence until the person is looking at it, and then you hardly need to. Here it comes as a closing flourish on a test about getting caught cutting a line. It asks me to agree, and I'd only just started to see.

**Chapter 2.** Parts of it felt like being told again. "How you sent it" is the Chapter 1 argument with "you" written over "I." "What it is" mostly the same. The executive and his spreadsheet is answering somebody who wasn't me.

But there's one thing in it that isn't in Chapter 1 and it got me harder than anything but the ticket machine: "I was working hard to keep him there, which only makes sense if part of me thought he could leave." I held that against twelve years of making sure Danny knew he wasn't forgiven. If he were only a thief, why would I need him to know anything? You don't send messages to a stump. That stopped me. And then, the next line, in bold, he tells me what I just found: "we only treat people as irredeemable because part of us knows they're not." The question had done it. The bold type took it back out of my hands.

Other things I'd keep if it were cut hard: "The thing we're turned away from is also the thing that warns us when we are turned away from it." That is said better than my tradition usually manages, and it's exactly the problem. The paragraph on conscious versus unconscious, with "we're nice to the perpetrators, when what we should be is kind." The sigh at someone you love and "then you went back to loving them as if nothing had happened." The "why the fuck shouldn't I," which I recognise from the receiving end; in my drinking years I got that look from good people all over town and that is just what I concluded. His admitting he still falls into it. The little prosecutor with his files. The last paragraph. That's maybe a third of the chapter. With the interlude doing what it does, a third would be enough.

The "sacrifice" message, "the dumping ground for our collective shadow," is workshop language and I flinched. But I'll be straight with you. I held it to my case anyway, and what came up is that I took money out of my mother's purse in 1989. I made that amends years ago. Danny's been carrying it for me since. I don't think that's all of it; what he did was real and my anger at it is clean. But the need for him to stay a thief is not clean. So the idea found something even though the wording put me off.

**Told rather than asked.** Besides the ones above: "Nobody, underneath, believes their contempt is righteous." "If I know that, why do I still do it? That's the wrong question." (It was my question. Don't tell me it's wrong, show me the better one.) "As long as punishing evil in the dark sense still has that draw, the darkness is operating in you." "It's a stance, and you have now watched the alternative recruit you." "If this is horrifying, notice why: because you don't actually want to be doing it." I wasn't horrified. I was tired and sad. He does have a clause for that, "some people find tiredness there instead, or grief," and I was glad of it, though it reads like it was glued in afterward.

And "I want to be crystal clear" and "in my absolutely deepest moments of introspection." When a man tells me how deep he went, I trust him less. The plain version, "In my experience, my darkness doesn't survive my looking directly at it; not its righteousness, anyway," I trust completely. That's what practice shows, and the little "anyway" is honest: the habit survives the looking, the righteousness doesn't.

**As a practitioner.** "All harm begins by turning away" is close to what we'd call not recognising, and he's honest that he can't prove it. Fine. Giving the darkness a will, "takes control," "commandeers," is what AA does with "my disease is doing push-ups in the parking lot." Useful, and also a place to hide, and he knows it, because he says straight out that we're fully responsible. Where I think he's thin is the ending: "As long as we aren't fully aware of our own nature, we have to locate the darkness in someone else." I had my nature pointed out nine years ago and I went right on despising tourists. It took six men in a church basement to show me that, not rigpa. I've known teachers with real realisation and plenty of contempt. Seeing what you are and cleaning out the habit are two jobs. "To the degree that we've uprooted this confusion" in his last line is more careful, and he should be that careful all the way through.

What's working: the exercises, and any sentence where he's reporting rather than proving. What isn't: the proving, and the number of times he reaches over and turns my head.

I'll read the other version tomorrow.

Jim,

I read the other version this morning. Short answer: the first one I read (with the interlude) is the better one, and it isn't close. If I were handing one to somebody at the centre tomorrow, it's that one. But the second has a few boards in it that the first one threw out and shouldn't have, and it explained two of the saw cuts I complained about yesterday.

**Why the first is better.**

The interlude. The second version doesn't have it, so it doesn't have the pill, the small wait to watch it land, the ticket machine and "I'm not that," the "who can really say?" shrug, or the two people who catch you. Those were four of the five places where something actually happened in me. Without the second catcher, "fiercely kind" in Chapter 2 is two words with nothing under them. With him, I've got Walt standing there. And in the second version "the light" first appears in the sentence "the darkness wins by masquerading as the light," with no introduction at all.

The first version asks where the second one tells. Put them side by side:

- Second: "If you look carefully, you'll find that limbo contains knowledge of itself." First: "If so, limbo contains knowledge of itself."
- Second: "My darkness cannot survive my looking directly at it, by definition." First: "In my experience, my darkness doesn't survive my looking directly at it; not its righteousness, anyway." "By definition" is a man winning an argument. "In my experience" and that honest "anyway" is a man who looked. It's the difference between the two drafts in one line.
- Second: "Everything I can accomplish with the darkness can be accomplished better without it." First puts "As far as I've seen" in front. Good.
- Second: "Is that true in the case you picked? If it is, then you *know* they're redeemable, and you're blocking their way back." First: "If it is: what did part of you believe about them, to need to work that hard?" The first one is the question that stopped me cold about Danny. The second one would have had me arguing with him.
- Second: "This was all there to see, if you had looked... you turned away from theirs." First keeps that whole passage on himself: "It was all there to see, when I finally looked." Same with "It's a tremendous relief" against "For me it was a tremendous relief." He has no business telling me what I'd have seen. He can tell me what he saw all day long.
- Second: "The horror is evidence that the darkness is not your nature. And through the common knowledge we've just traced, some part of you already knows that it cannot be anyone else's, either." First: "The horror is a hint... And if it isn't yours, it's hard to see how it could be anyone else's." The first leaves me room. The second has "traced," "already knows," and "cannot" in one sentence.

The second version also has the "common knowledge" business: "everybody knows it," said twice, and then in brackets "this is a hint about the nature of awareness itself. I say more in Chapter 3 and Appendix 1." A claim about the nature of awareness, in a parenthesis, in the middle of a paragraph about a dirty look. Where I sit that's the biggest thing a person can say, and you don't say it with a page reference. The first version cut all of it and is better for it. Same for "Sit with that for a moment," which is workshop talk, and the paragraph telling me what my horror is for before I've said I have any.

And the second version's stack: "A whole stack of things," "at the bottom of the stack," "the bottom-most thing." That's the clever stretch made longer. I was gone by the second layer.

**What the second does better, and what the first has lost.**

1. The joins. Yesterday I griped about "Okay, back to the scenario. That's the strange thing from before." So when he comes back, "The answer to the question... is, of course, limbo" has something to answer. The first version cut the question and kept the return, and that's the scar I tripped on. He doesn't need the stack back. He needs one plain sentence of question before Limbo. Same with the cheating example: in the second it follows "There is nothing to turning away except knowing and not-knowing," which sets it up. In the first the order is scrambled, with "We just checked," then the cheating case, then "And turning away entails," so the example sits between two halves of one thought. And the second's opening of "What is doing this," "I was talking about it as if it had a stake in the matter. I meant to," is a better door than "There's a strange thing here." So is "I called the first level of hiding malice, and the second contempt. In one line:" where the first version just starts talking.

2. This, from the second version's monologue, is the truest thing in either one, and the first version dropped it:

"And truth be told, something deep inside knows they're right. But I absolutely cannot afford to admit it, least of all here. I don't even want to believe I have that kind of darkness in me—the kind that would forsake a fellow being just to prove I'm right about them."

"Least of all here." That's it exactly. Not in front of him. Anywhere but in front of him. The first version's monologue is louder and funnier ("Ha!", "Classic.") but it never once lets the man feel the floor go soft, and mine always did, for half a second. Put those lines back.

In the second it's said in Chapter 1 and then said again. So: keep it out of Chapter 1, and put that one sentence in Chapter 2, right after the question, in place of the bold line about the great irony of contempt.
