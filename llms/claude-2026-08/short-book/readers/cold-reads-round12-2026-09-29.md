## Helena (53, mediator, former litigator, secular atheist; runs cold) — compared against the long book's Chapter 1

### Stage 1

Aditya,

I read it through once and did the exercises as they came, with real people. Here is what happened, in order.

**Before I got to you.** The epigraph is from the Gospel of Philip, and I flinched. I'm a secular reader, and the first thing on the page told me I'd picked up a religious book. The line itself is fine. A hidden thing is strong until it's recognized: any litigator could have told you that. But I read your first page braced, and I think you should know that.

**The opening.** The first sentence is long and I had to take it in two runs. It was worth it, though. It's a real claim and you make it about yourself: my conviction that I'm nothing like the bad people makes me act like them. I can work with a man who starts by indicting himself.

"I'm going to make some claims about your mind and what you know. I realize this can sound presumptuous." Did it help? Partly. It read like an opening statement: here's what I intend to show, reserve judgment. I respect that. But the promise to let me "decide for yourself" didn't hold for the whole chapter. By the time I reached "This, I believe, is the great paradox of evil," the tentative voice was gone. Then "For me, seeing this revealed a surprising truth about the nature of evil." I don't use the word evil, and a "surprising truth about the nature of" anything makes me reach for my wallet. You'd have lost me less if you had said what you saw and let me decide whether it deserved the word.

**Limbo.** I did find it, and it's the part of the chapter I'll remember longest.

Twenty-some years ago I was a second-year associate on a commercial lease case. There was a memo in our production, an internal one, that hurt us. I put it in box eleven of fourteen, between two runs of invoices, and coded it as responsive to a request it technically answered. I knew opposing counsel's team was two paralegals and an exhausted partner. They missed it. I told myself it was produced, so it was disclosed, so it was fine.

Your question: "The knowledge that it was wrong: did it feel like new information?" No. It never did. I knew in the moment, with the box open. And what I actually recognized came from your next questions. About a year later a senior partner, Walt, asked me in the hallway whether there'd been "anything hot" in that production. I said, "Nothing they didn't get." That was true to the letter. It was also a lie, and I built it on the spot, fast and clean. "If you did, did that happen passively?" No. It was the most active sentence I said all week.

So yes, limbo. The word works for me. Lawyers use "in limbo" all the time, and it fits: the thing was filed but never decided.

What I noticed in the method: your limbo questions are leading questions, and I write leading questions for a living. "Or like something you had always known, but somehow hadn't let yourself know?" The second option gets the italics and twice the words. "Looking back, did some part of you know you were doing that, even as you did it?" I answered yes, because it's true of me, so it didn't bother me much. But sitting between the questions is a statement: "It was right there to see if you had looked: fully available to consciousness, with you just turned away from it." That one isn't a question. You're telling me what I'll find, halfway through asking me to look.

**Better nature.** "I can't, when I can see myself in them." I don't believe that as stated. I spent twenty years hurting people I could see myself in. It was the job, and I slept fine most nights. I think what you mean is narrower, and I'd believe the narrower thing. "Better nature" I can live with, because Lincoln got there before you. Your asymmetry, that being helpful requires hiding nothing, made me stop and test it. I think it holds for hiding things from yourself, which is what you mean, though not for hiding them from others.

"Under the right circumstances, I'm capable of the worst acts humans have committed." Granted. I've read Browning's *Ordinary Men*. For me that isn't the hard part.

**The confrontation scene.** It isn't me. I don't confront, I don't do it with my eyes, and I don't say "dammit." So I read this section as a spectator, which is probably not what you want. Still, the mind in it is believable. The best move in it is the slide from threat to "it's really more like an invitation." I've watched clients do that across a conference table, and I've done it.

"And he can't prove I'm doing this. And crucially, neither can I." That line is deniability, built so that it's deniable even to the person who built it. I've drafted letters like that. It landed.

I skimmed from "And wouldn't you know it" through "Ha! Classic." The performed inner voice there felt like acting.

Then the point of view jumps: "He leaves the interaction more certain than ever that his enemies are evil—unable to own up to their own darkness." How does the narrator know that? It's the first place I felt you writing his mind for him. And "darkness" made me wince each time it appeared.

The line that caught me was this one: "There's a brief flicker of satisfaction, vindication, even relief at being proven right—just before I relabel it disgust or dismay." Three years ago I heard that Doug Brenner, my former partner, was in a fee dispute with a client he had taken from me. I said "that's sad, really" to the person who told me. It wasn't sad to me. It was relief. I was proven right, and I relabeled it within about a second. That's exact.

**"I'll go ahead and name what's happening."** This was mostly lecture, but it contained the most useful idea in the chapter, and I nearly missed it.

"It is sufficient to produce the outcome I need, and also necessary." As a lawyer I read necessary and sufficient as plain English. What slowed me was "it." I had to go back two sentences to find that "it" is my enemy reading my self-deception. Once I found it, the next two sentences were the payoff: "If it felt accidental, he might forgive or pity me instead. But if it were purely intentional, then it would read as overt malice—which would make me the bad guy."

That is my civility with Doug, exactly. For six years at bar events I've said "Hi, Doug," smiled and moved on. If I were warm, I'd be the fool who got robbed and forgave it. If I were openly cold, I'd be the bitter ex-partner, and people would feel sorry for him. So the civility is calibrated so that he knows and nobody else can say so. I didn't see that as calibration until your paragraph. But I only got there by translating your hot scene into my cold one myself. The chapter didn't help me do it until much later.

"So what exactly have I done to him? Nothing but show that I'm doing this to him." On first read it meant nothing to me; it sounded like word salad. On second read, with Doug in mind, it meant exactly this: I've done nothing to Doug. I've been perfectly civil. The only act is letting him see that the civility is the verdict. So the sentence is true, but it only works for a reader who has already made the translation. And the which-chain after it ("which I also knew, which he saw, which...") is clever in a way that asks me to notice it's clever.

"This, I believe, is the great paradox of evil." I winced. We "had no way of knowing," while they do it with "full awareness." That part I already knew. It's the actor-observer asymmetry, and I've read the behavioral economics. What was new, and what I'd want kept, is your claim that it isn't a bias at all. You say it's work, done on purpose and hidden. I haven't decided whether I believe that, but it's the part worth arguing.

"And in that gap, something uglier takes control." With the scare quotes on "thing," and then "More on that in Chapter 2," I got nervous. It smells like possession, and it sounds like the next chapter is where the devil shows up. If so, I may not follow you there.

"Believing I'm different and special is inherently self-deceptive." This is asserted, not shown. And "leaks out my eyes": the eyes again.

**The correction without contempt.** This worked, and it's the one place where the question really was open. When I was a fourth-year, Judge Marian Tolliver called me into chambers over a discovery motion I'd filed mostly to bury the other side in paper. She said, "Ms. Park, you're better than this motion." I withdrew it the next morning. I didn't dig in and I didn't want revenge. For a week I wanted to be the person she had addressed. So when you write "This kind of correction can feel like it's reminding you of something—that you're better than this," you weren't telling me something. I had the memory before you named it. "Check if you've had that before" was unnecessary, but harmless.

It sits oddly in the order, though. You close the mechanism, show the alternative, and then go back to the mechanism for the common-knowledge section.

**Common knowledge.** It opens with "Assuming you believe (or better yet, see) the sense in which this is intentional." I noticed the hedge. It's honest, but it also admits you aren't sure you've carried me, and at that point you hadn't fully.

I followed the idea. I know common knowledge from game theory; that part is familiar ground. The sentence "What I'm doing is violating that shared knowledge, and making him carry the knowledge that I am violating it, while I hide it from myself" lost me. I read it three times.

What the section added was this: "My justifications, on the other hand, are believable only to me (and my allies)." That's the Doug story. I have told it maybe forty times: medical leave, two clients, the Hendricks account. Everyone I tell it to nods. Of course they do. They're my allies, or they became my allies by hearing it.

Where I refused: "the real kind that everyone knows," and "The darkness leaks out of my skull, empowering his, because its meaning is universal." Everyone knows the real good? That's moral realism smuggled in as a subordinate clause. You may believe it, but argue it or mark it. Don't slip it past me in the middle of a sentence about something else. Opposing counsel does that.

**"I am speaking exactly to the part of him that I claim doesn't exist."** Found in myself, and it's the recognition I'm taking out of the room. You don't give the cold shoulder to a vending machine. The whole performance of my civility toward Doug assumes a Doug who can feel shame. If he really were what I tell people he is, a man with no conscience, my coolness would be wasted on him. So I don't believe my own story about him, or my behavior doesn't. That one sentence of yours did more than the three paragraphs around it.

"No wonder we, as a society, cannot admit that this is what we're doing to one another." That went from me to society in one step, too fast.

"If seeing this makes you want to hide, notice that's the same verdict, pointed inward. It feels like accountability. It isn't." Good. My therapist spent a year getting me to that, and you did it in three sentences. Keep it.

**Other kinds of contempt.** This is where I got caught, and it came late. For most of the chapter I'd been thinking "not me, I don't do the eyes." Then: "I just write the person off: a quiet filing-away, a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them." That's six years of bar events in one sentence. I'd push back on one phrase: there's a great deal behind the politeness. But you say so in the next sentence, so fine.

It may be that the hot scene let me lower my guard, and this is where you got me. But I nearly put the chapter down during the hot scene. A cold reader of my type spends seven pages as a spectator before you speak to her.

"The stupid, the slow and the weak get the same verdict, with better cover: I'm only being accurate." That's my voice, word for word. I say it about opposing counsel, and I've said it about co-mediators.

Then the loved ones, the hardest paragraph. "The look on their face that tells me it landed, which I pretend not to have seen, leaving them nothing to accuse me of." Last spring my daughter Mina, who's seventeen, came home at one in the morning after saying eleven. I said, "Okay. That's your choice. We'll talk about what it means tomorrow." I said it evenly. She said, "Can you just yell at me? Your calm voice is worse than yelling." I told her I wasn't going to yell because I wasn't angry. I was furious. And the calm voice gave her nothing to push against: nothing to accuse me of. She knew that, and I knew that she knew. I'd never had an explanation for why she's right about the calm voice. Now I have one, and I don't like it.

"Part of me knows it will spread the same way, too, and that's part of why I send it." I didn't follow this. Why would I send it in order to spread it? If you mean I want it to land, say that. As written it assigns me a motive I can't find.

"Everyone in the conversation learns that the verdict is safe to hold, and leaves a little more practiced at it." The Doug story again. It's polished now, with good timing and a laugh line about the medical leave. I've been training people.

Then the trees. "Part of us can already see that the world isn't inert; that seeing lives in limbo too." No. That's where the atheist gets off the bus. I don't think I'm hiding a knowledge that trees aren't inert stuff, and being told that part of me already sees it is exactly being told what I'll find. It's the only place in the chapter where you told me something about myself that I'm sure is false.

**Self-serving.** I've mediated a nursing-home case like the one you describe. "Each would sincerely say they're doing good, and each knows." Some did. I sat across from a CFO who I'm fairly sure didn't. "Each knows" is stated as a finding, and it isn't one. "It degrades the world through a causality no accounting shows" is vague in a way nothing else in the chapter is. The last sentence of the section is good.

**Checks.** "Forget about the 'right' or 'decent' answer." Fine. Doug, vengeance or the pill: honestly, part of me wants the vengeance button, and specifically the public kind. I want the story I tell to be true in front of everyone, in his presence. The pill would take that from me, and I noticed I didn't want it taken. That was worth finding. The checks feel appended, though, as if they came after the chapter ended.

**Last.** Was it obvious? A good deal of it. Self-deception, actor-observer, "we're all capable of it": I've read all that. What was new was the calibration. The coldness works because it's deniable to me and legible to him, and it's addressed to a conscience I claim he doesn't have. That turned two things I already knew, Mina's complaint and my Doug story, into things I have to do something about.

This week: there's a section lunch Thursday, and someone always asks about Doug. I'm not telling the story. And the next time Mina does something that makes me furious, I'll say I'm furious.

Would I pass it on? Not as is. I'd send my friend Dev, a co-mediator who does "I'm only being accurate" better than I do, the pages from "Other kinds of contempt" to the end, and the paragraph about the calm-voice logic. I wouldn't send it to my sister, who is as secular as I am. The epigraph, "the nature of evil," the darkness leaking out of skulls and the trees would have her closing it by page two, and she'd miss the part that's true.

Helena

### Stage 2

Aditya,

I've now read the second version. I'll give you the verdict first and then the evidence.

**Which did more, and which treated me better.** The second version did more to me, and it wasn't close. The first version treated me better. That's true page by page, though not for the whole length. The first is shorter and mostly says "I." It has fewer places where you answer your own question, and it keeps its metaphysics to a paragraph about trees that I could simply refuse. The second puts me in the chair and keeps me there. In its first half, that's exactly why it works. In its second half it keeps me there while it explains an agent to me for several thousand words, and by the end I felt held, not accompanied.

**What the second has that the first should recover.** This is most of the letter.

*1. The part that knows versus the part that proves.* This is the biggest thing, and the first version doesn't have it at all. From the second version: "Whatever it was, did it arrive before or after you'd reasoned through it?" Then: "Meanwhile, another part of you was busy providing reasons." Then: "Why did one side have to argue, and the other not? And which side did you listen to—the part that "just knew" or the part that could prove?"

I went back to box eleven. The knowing came first, with the box open, before any reasoning. The reasons came after: technically responsive, they had fourteen boxes, it's their job to read them. My whole career has been the part that proves. When I read that question I felt it in my stomach, not my head. No sentence in the first version did that.

The thread pays off later, which the first version has no way to do. From the second: "He overlooked the part that knew in favor of the part that can prove." And: "And the result is that you've learned to ignore the part that knows in favor of that which proves." That's an account of how contempt spreads that I can follow step by step. The first version gestures at the same thing with "His justifications simply become more persuasive in this rotten world," but it asserts it about someone else. The second shows me how it happened in me.

One caution. "I mean, obviously. Why trust something that can't even justify itself?" is sarcasm, and it nearly lost me. It's aimed at the reader, and the reader it's aimed at is me.

*2. "Unconscious" as the defense.* From the second: "We like to call such knowledge "unconscious"—as if we had no way of knowing—but that's not entirely accurate." And: "Again, we normally call it "unconscious." Do you see what incentive we might have to call it that?" That's a good question and a real one, and it left me room. I've watched "he didn't consciously intend" do a great deal of work in a mediation. The first version gets to "We "had no way of knowing,"" much later, as a paradox about enemies. The second plants it in my own memory on page one, which is where it belongs.

*3. "Would the big ones be easier to recall, or harder?"* One line, and a fair question. It made me wonder what else is in box eleven, so to speak. Keep it.

*4. Me on the receiving end, and the funnel.* In the first version I watched you confront a man, and I was a spectator for pages. The second version makes me the one caught: "Deny it, and you're the kind of person who does. Get angry, and that's proof too. Stay calm, and you don't even care. Apologize, and it's too convenient." Then: "Every road funnels into the same conclusion."

I recognized it from both sides. I've built exactly that funnel in cross-examinations, for witnesses I had decided were liars. And I've been in it. In my divorce deposition, my ex-husband's lawyer ran it on me for an hour. "Stay calm, and you don't even care": I noticed which item on the list is mine.

Then: "You'd take that bet at any odds. But on what evidence?" That's a lawyer's question and a good one. The first version has nothing that puts the reader in the witness chair, and for a cold reader like me that's the difference between watching and being caught.

*5. The second stranger, stated harder.* Both versions have him. The second gets two things right that the first blurs. First: "Not more gently. Just that his anger was directed at what you did, and not what you are." The first version says "They might have been more fierce than I just was," which is close, but "Not more gently" rules out the soft reading before I can reach for it. Second, the open question: "What is that thing whose side you are both on; that two people who agree on nothing can both be serving? Hold that question." I held it. My honest answer about Judge Tolliver is that we both served the court, and that's the institution, not something metaphysical. But you asked; you didn't tell me. Keep it. The line before it, "Whatever he was standing up for wasn't his. It wasn't a rule, or a side," pre-empts my answer, and I'd rather have been allowed to give it.

*6. The harm, shown as something I learned.* This is the second most important recovery. From the second: "And the thing is, it won't feel like a change to who you are—it will feel like something you've learned about the world". And: "You don't notice that something has been taken from you." Later, from the receiving side: "And the thing is, it doesn't feel like a loss at all. It feels like you've gotten a bit wiser. A little more realistic about how this world operates."

After Doug, I left partnership practice. I've been solo for six years, and I have told myself, and everyone else, that I learned something about partners. I have never once framed it as something taken from me. The first version never makes me look at what my contempt's targets learn, or what I learned when I was the target. The second does, and it's the part that made the Doug story feel less like a story and more like a loss I've been calling wisdom.

*7. The record.* From the second: "The record will show that the second stranger was harsher—and so he should have been the one that made it worse." A litigator notices that sentence. The harm leaves no trace in the record, and the record points at the wrong man. The first version has "And he can't prove I'm doing this. And crucially, neither can I," which is the sender's deniability. The second adds what I'd call the evidentiary injustice, and I think both belong.

*8. The mens rea dilemma and the test.* From the second: "If he knew it fully, then he sounds like a mustache-twirling villain. If he didn't know it at all, then there's nothing to hold him responsible for." That's the frame I'd use for any claim about intent, and it sets up limbo as the answer to a real problem, not a label. Then the diagnostic: "If it were, he'd be embarrassed or confused if you pointed it out. Instead, it intensified in exactly the way needed to keep itself hidden". That's a test I can run on other people and on myself. The first version's "I can't sincerely hold against him evidence that I know I planted" matches the second's "You can't trust evidence that you know you yourself planted," so either one is fine there. The embarrassment test is the second's alone.

And one line: "He was judging you from the very state he was judging you for." It's clean, and it lands.

*9. A secular definition of conscience.* From the second: "That's what your conscience is: the part of you that keeps other people in view." I can hold that. There's no soul in it, no light, and it's checkable. The first version's "better nature" is fine, but it never tells me what the thing is. With this definition, the rest of your argument became something I could test without converting first. And "Try this: go out in public, find a stranger, and shoot them a dehumanizing gaze" is a real experiment. I'd keep the experiment and drop the "It's hard to do, isn't it?" I've done it across a deposition table and it wasn't hard. What was hard was doing it to a stranger who'd done nothing, which I think is your actual point.

*10. "Contempt is malice that believes it's righteous, and that's what makes it maddening."* This is the first version's "It is sufficient... and also necessary" paragraph in one sentence, and I understood it on first read. Along with it: "In other words, the particular limbo we call contempt functions simply by telegraphing itself to its target." That phrase, telegraphing itself to its target, is what I translated my Doug civility into by myself in the first version.

*11. Proven right versus right.* From the second: "And from the inside, being proven right and being right are the same experience. The only difference is who produced the evidence, and that's the part he can't see." Then: "Which is why it never mattered how small the thing you did was. It only had to be evidence." This is the best thing in either version about the Doug story. Every time Doug does something slippery, I'm proven right, and I have been producing the setting for it: the colleagues primed, the story polished. The first version's "flicker of satisfaction, vindication, even relief" names the feeling. The second explains it.

*12. The flash, in my own case.* The "When You Send It" section asks for a moment, and it's the one place in the second version where you ask the way the first version asks. From the second: "It felt like justice, a little sweeter than justice needs to be." That's the most precise sentence in either file for what cold contempt feels like from inside. At the bar association holiday party two years ago, I said, "Congratulations on the Hendricks renewal, Doug." Hendricks was my client. The sentence was perfectly civil, and it was a little sweeter than justice needs to be. Keep that line wherever the chapter ends up.

And: "We all know this is how it works. We have to, or else it wouldn't work." In the first version the common-knowledge section lost me for a paragraph. This is the same idea as an inference, in two sentences. Because it's argued, it didn't feel like being told. It's the one "we all know" in either version that earned itself.

*13. No bottom.* From the second: "A person who's out of view is a person anything can be done to. There's no bottom." And: "So a flash of it, even for someone you love, is a flash of the thing with no bottom." This is why Mina is right that my calm voice is worse than yelling. Yelling has a floor: I'm in the room, engaged, hot. The calm voice says she has been moved out of view. The first version explained the calm voice as leaving "them nothing to accuse me of," which is true, and I'd keep it. The second explains why it frightens her. That changed what I'll do more than anything in the first version.

*14. A definition of evil I can use.* From the second: "Not as a judgement, but as the very essence of what "evil" means: to turn away from the life in front of us in order to pursue our own ends." I winced at "the nature of evil" in the first version because it promised a revelation. This offers a definition, and a secular reader can test a definition. Put this where the first version has "a surprising truth about the nature of evil," and I would have read the first page braced for less.

And the cover story: "My darkness isn't real darkness—it's the kind all good people must harbor to keep bad people in check." That's the first version's "I'm only being fierce" in a form I recognized as mine.

**What the first was right to leave out.**

The agent, all of it. "But don't mistake the grammar for the claim: I am not saying there is a literal thing inside him." I believed you for about a page. Then came "It is an infection, and it just spread itself to you," and "So whose goals is it pursuing, exactly? His—or its own?" The grammar became the claim. A disclaimer you then ignore is worse than none. It tells me you know you're doing it. "It shows you a man with nobody home" is a good sentence and could survive on its own. The rest is Chapter 2 material at best, and the first version was right to leave it there with a one-line promise.

The Loop and The Tragedy. I skimmed both. They re-derive what The Tell and The Why had already given me.

The Taunt, mostly. The italic monologue is vivid. "I know you can hear this, but you can't prove it. Good luck." is close to what my "Hi, Doug" says. But "Which means that when I'm finally forced to do to you what I must" is violent, and for a cold reader it's a scene I'm not in. The first version's one italic line from the receiving side, "Don't pretend you're doing something noble here. We both know what you're actually doing.", does the job at a tenth of the length.

The smokescreen line. "who's to say what's really good or bad? If it stops bad people, it's by definition good!" and then "But the "who's to say" is evidence of the smokescreen." That's aimed at me, a secular reader with no settled metaethics, and it tells me my position is a symptom. It's the one place in either version where I felt cornered, not caught. The first version's "the real kind that everyone knows" smuggled in the same claim, which I objected to. At least that was only smuggling. This is an accusation.

The book talking about itself. "I'll give you the shape of the answer now, so you can keep reading, and earn it as we go"; "Why that should be, I'll come to later."; and "And it's what this book exists to provide you." The last one especially. Promising me safety sounds like a workshop brochure.

The answered questions. "Harder, almost certainly." "Of course not." "It's hard to do, isn't it?" "Haven't you ever watched an enemy squirm under your "righteous" gaze". "deep down he knows—as we all do—". The second version asks more real questions than the first, and it also answers more of them for me. The first version's "I" frame protected it from most of this.

The ending. "patiently waiting to be remembered." It's too soft, and it's the only line in either version that sounds like a retreat. The first version ends on the checks, which feel appended, but at least they're mine to run.

**Order and length.** The second version runs about three times as long, and roughly the last half repeats the first half in a larger voice. The order of its first half is better than anything in the first version. It goes: limbo, then how I knew, then me caught by the first stranger, then the second stranger, then what the first one took. It goes from receiving to understanding, and only then turns to sending. By the time it turned me into the sender, I had already felt the funnel, so I recognized it instantly.

The first version runs the other way. You as sender come first, and me as sender arrives only in "Other kinds of contempt," near the end. The second version has no cold contempt at all, though, and that's the first version's great advantage for a reader like me. There's nothing in the second like "I just write the person off: a quiet filing-away, a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them." There's no "The stupid, the slow and the weak get the same verdict, with better cover: I'm only being accurate." There's no "Everyone in the conversation learns that the verdict is safe to hold, and leaves a little more practiced at it." In the second version, my contempt only shows up through "Congratulations on the Hendricks renewal" because I brought it. The first version went and found me.

If I were putting one chapter together from the two, my reading goes like this. It's what happened in me, not an edit. Take the second version up to "The Why." Keep the first version's "I am speaking exactly to the part of him that I claim doesn't exist." It's still the sharpest single sentence across both, and the second's "That's what he's in limbo about: the fact that you're merely in limbo" is its weaker twin. Then the second's "When You Send It," with the first version's cold, loved-ones and gossip paragraphs folded into it, and the "no bottom" passage. Stop before the agent.

That chapter would have done to me everything both of these did, in about the length of the first. And I'd send it to my sister.

Helena
