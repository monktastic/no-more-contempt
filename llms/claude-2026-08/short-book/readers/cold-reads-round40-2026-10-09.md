## Naomi (54, philosophy professor, moral realism and moral epistemology; secular Jewish)

### Stage 1

Dear Aditya,

I read the chapter at the kitchen table on a Tuesday night, with David watching the Guardians lose in the next room. I did the exercises, which I almost never do when a book asks. I'm going to tell you what happened in order, and I'm going to be a philosopher about it in places, because that's what I am and I can't read any other way. Please take the philosophy as a sign that I was engaged. I don't do it to books I've stopped believing.

**The opening.** You ask me to hold the claims "tentatively, until you see the picture they assemble," and then you give me the central one: "contempt is the claim that you're a different kind of thing than me, but for it to land, you must be the same kind of thing, and part of you must know it." My pencil went to the two "must"s. That's a transcendental claim, a claim about the conditions of possibility of contempt working at all, and those are the hardest kind to earn. I put a question mark in the margin and agreed to keep going. Tentatively, which is what you asked for. I appreciated "Or maybe not." It's a small thing, but it told me you weren't going to pretend the reader had no say.

**Limbo.** The memory came immediately, which surprised me. In 2003 I wrote a recommendation letter for a graduate student named Marcus Odom, who had embarrassed me in seminar the previous spring by catching a real error in my reading of Korsgaard, in front of everyone, and not graciously. I wrote that he was "solid" and "conscientious." Anyone in the profession knows what those words mean in a letter. They mean: don't. I knew it while I typed. I also knew, while I typed, that I was not thinking about why I was choosing those words. I told myself I was being "measured," that I didn't inflate letters, that his work really was uneven. He got a job anyway, at a state school in Michigan, and two years later he sent me a card thanking me for my support. That card is when I owned up to it. And no, it was not new information. Your word "limbo" is right. It was available, and I was turned away from it. I bought your first claim for my own case completely. Sartre and Fingarette would say the same; you've just said it more plainly than either.

I liked this a great deal: "If that doesn't match anything in your memory, the rest of this won't make sense, and I'd rather you know that now." That is a man staking his argument on my evidence instead of his rhetoric. It made me trust you.

Did I deflect? Yes. My husband asked me at the time why I was taking so long over one letter and I said something about the job market. That was not passive. Some part of me knew.

"Nobody ever looked honestly at something in themselves while being told they were worthless for having it." I marked "Nobody ever." It's a universal claim and you can't know it. But I believed it, because I've watched it in office hours a hundred times: a student who calls herself stupid has stopped looking at the problem. So I bought it as true and noticed you'd overreached in the grammar.

"Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" This landed. The reasons were busy. *He was rude in seminar. His work is uneven. I don't inflate.* The knowledge that I was punishing him didn't argue. It just sat there. I'd want to add that the quiet side might be a cached norm rather than anything deeper, but I couldn't make that objection stick while looking at my own letter. The quiet side didn't feel like a rule. It felt like Marcus.

**The cashier.** The woman at Giant Eagle on Saturday; her tag said Denise. I pictured her face. The smile and the thank-you went immediately. The sneer wouldn't go until I gave it something: she was slow, the line was long. So yes, the sneer needed a reason, and I noticed I had to invent one. I bought this, with one reservation. Imagining a sneer in a quiet kitchen is not the same as being tired at 6 p.m. with a cart full of groceries. I have been short with cashiers when I was tired, and I didn't feel I had a reason; I just had fatigue. But maybe fatigue is the reason. I let it go.

**The remorse asymmetry.** This is where I first refused, and I want to be precise about where. My mind went straight to Huckleberry Finn, who decides to help Jim and is convinced he's damning himself. Jonathan Bennett wrote a famous paper about it: Huck's conscience tells him he's doing wrong by being decent. That looks like your reversal. You've anticipated it, sort of: "Regret that it cost me, sure—anger at being taken advantage of, guilt at breaking a rule I was raised on, or feeling foolish." So Huck's feeling is "guilt at breaking a rule," not remorse. But then I worried remorse is being defined as the feeling that tracks the good, which would make "it only points in one direction" true by stipulation.

Then I did what you asked and checked my own life. I have never felt the sickening thing, the having-gone-against-myself, for an act of decency. Not once. I've felt foolish for lending money to my cousin Barry. That's a different feeling and I know it is. So I came back to the claim by my own route. "Maybe some cannot access it, but I doubt it's ever reversed." I'll take "I doubt." I'd have refused "it's never reversed." "Remorse is what turning back feels like" I bought outright. That is a good sentence.

"Under the right circumstances, I'm capable of the worst acts humans have committed." I'm a secular Jew whose grandmother left Lodz in 1937. I've read Browning's *Ordinary Men* twice. I didn't need persuading of this. What I needed was to be shown what I do with the knowledge, and you said you would.

**The Encounter.** Believable as what a mind does? Yes. Uncomfortably. I don't confront people who've been cruel very often, but I recognized every move from faculty meetings with our dean, who says "outcomes" the way other people say grace. The "invitation" paragraph I recognized as a thing I say to myself: I'm giving him a chance to be reasonable. "Plus, he can't prove I'm doing this, can he? No. And crucially, *neither can I*." That stopped me. That's the self-deception paradox in eleven words, and it's funny, and it's true. I didn't skim anything here. If I sped up anywhere it was "*Ha!* Classic." which felt a touch performed, but I knew it was the narrated mind.

**The cold version.** This is the one that's mine. "a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them." That is exactly how I am with Dean Pruitt. I'm scrupulously polite. I have filed him. And "someone slow, ignorant, or clumsy can get the same verdict, and I write it off as 'just being honest'": that's how I talk about certain students in the department lounge, and I call it rigor. The cold paragraph did more to me than the hot scene did.

**Integrity.** It came at exactly the right time. "So what am I supposed to do—be *nice* to evil?" was in my mouth already, and you asked it before I could. My corrector: in 1996, our chair, Tom Okafor, walked into the copy room while I was reading a sophomore's paper aloud to two colleagues, doing the voice. He waited until they left. He said, "She's nineteen and she's trying, and you're making her into a bit." That's all. He wasn't gentle. There was no wriggle room. And I didn't dig in, which is unlike me. I went back to my office and felt sick, and the sick feeling was mine, not his.

Your teacher story did something specific. "He was searching my face for something, some sign that I knew, and that made me look for it in myself." That's what Tom did, and I'd never had words for it. He wasn't announcing a verdict, he was looking for me. I read the hypocrisy sentence twice: "He wasn't being a hypocrite, and hypocrisy was what I'd always used to counter-accuse the people who tried to shame me for what I was." It's compressed. On the second reading I understood that you'd had a weapon ready and he gave you nothing to aim it at. That's good, but it arrives fast and I think it matters later more than it seems to here. "I don't want to make too much of that afternoon" was right. It kept the story a story.

"What is it that two people on opposite sides can both be serving?" I answered it before reading on: the good. Murdoch's Good, sovereign over both of us. I felt entirely free here. You asked and stopped, and I found my own answer, and then later found it was roughly yours.

**The Corruption.** Mostly insight, some of it read twice. "The signal is the pretense that there is no signal" I had to read twice and then admired. "Neither half alone would be enough to get me what I need: both his reaction and my innocence." This paragraph is clever, maybe too clever. It attributes a design to the process that I couldn't verify, and I bought it tentatively. But then "If you've been on his end" undid my reservations completely. "Deny it, and you're the kind of person who does. Get angry, and that's proof too. Stay calm, and you don't even care. Apologize, and it's too convenient." I was a woman in a philosophy department in 1991. I have been on his end. Every exit closed, exactly like that.

"My stories kept changing; my conclusion never did." Yes.

"From the inside, being proven right and being right are the same experience. The only difference is who produced the evidence." This is the best thing in the first half. I thought of the pundit I watch, Grady Holt, whom I watch precisely so I can be angry. Every night he produces the evidence for me and I feel I've been proven right about him. I never once asked who arranged for me to be there to receive it. And "So I didn't—and that *was* my role in causing it" is very fine.

**The two checks.** Is there something satisfying about seeing evil violently punished? Yes. David and I watched *Inglourious Basterds* when it came out and I was elated at the end, the theater burning, and then embarrassed by how elated I was. The buttons: for the dean, I couldn't be bothered; he isn't worth vengeance, which is its own kind of contempt, I see now. For Holt, yes, some part of me wanted the first button. I wanted him humiliated on his own show. I noticed that your "Imagine no one will ever know you were given this choice, let alone what you chose" is Glaucon's Ring of Gyges, and that it works for the same reason Plato's does. "And that lie is exactly what lets me act on it, in ways I rarely recognize until later." I accepted this. I watch Holt to act on it.

**Whose intention.** "It acts as if it has goals of its own, and it pursues them intentionally." I bristled. This is the homunculus problem, and it's what makes the self-deception literature so exhausting: you explain one divided mind with a little mind inside it. I was about to refuse. Then the next paragraph: "Don't mistake the grammar for the claim: I'm not saying there's a separate thing living in me." It came in time, barely. I heard it as a functional description, the way Freud's defenses are best read. I can live with that.

I did resist "That's intent, just like your deflection was in the opening." You're cashing in a concession I made a few pages back. I had made it, though. I'd said my deflection wasn't passive. So I paid.

"When I'm sure someone was fully aware of what they were doing, I've made them a villain. When I'm sure they had no idea, I've made them a machine." This is Strawson, the participant attitude against the objective one, and you've found something he didn't quite say: that both poles can be ways of putting someone outside the circle of people like me. And "What I find hard to hold is what I found in my own memory: that they knew, but didn't let themselves feel the weight of what they knew." That is Marcus's letter, and it's what I should be able to grant the dean. For me this was the most important paragraph in the chapter. I'll be thinking about it for a while.

Then I refused, partly. "And I only do that because, at a level I cannot let myself see, I fear I might be one of them." I don't know that about myself, and the phrase "at a level I cannot let myself see" makes it unfalsifiable. Then: "If you can find even one memory where it's plainly true of you, that's the one to look at." That's where I felt pushed. It presumes there's one to find. I looked anyway. I found a fear of being a fraud, which is adjacent, not the same. I could still follow everything after.

"Only a chump believes otherwise." This landed, and yes, I connected it. Earlier, in the hypocrite's voice: "I'd be a chump." Now it turns out that the lesson I teach my enemy is the same chump philosophy. That's Thrasymachus, and it's the first time I've seen anyone show how it gets taught rather than argued.

**The way out.** "What does "yourself" mean here? I'm not asking a philosophical question." That made me laugh, because of course it is one, and you knew I'd think so. I tried it. Back at the Marcus letter, what was I hiding the knowledge from? Not from my reputation. From the person who had been proud of Marcus when he caught my error, for about four seconds, before I was embarrassed. That person was me, the teacher I take myself to be. Korsgaard would call it practical identity. So I found something, but I'm not sure it's what you point at. I found a standard I hold, not a nature I have.

"Our better nature isn't one part among others. It's a description of what we *are* in the absence of all self-deception." I granted this as your claim. I didn't find it. It's Platonic, Augustinian, and very large, and in a philosopher's mouth I'd call it grandiose. In yours it didn't sound grandiose, because of "Not because it's nicer. Because it's the one that's home." Home is the right word. It's modest and it's exactly what remorse felt like. Did anything later change my answer? The remorse question did, partly: I only feel I've gone against myself when I go against that. That's evidence for your identification, not proof.

The gaze. Yes, I've been on the receiving end. An APA interview in a hotel room in 1989; the senior man never looked at me as though I were a philosopher. "Absent" is the word. What was he turned away from? I answered: from me, and from whatever in him would have had to notice I was a person asking good questions. "Why would he have to turn away at all, if what he is, is essentially bad?" I tried to answer and couldn't, and that's the point, I suppose. An essentially bad man would not need to look away; he'd look straight at me and not care. The look-away was effort. The objection I had ready was that he'd never looked in the first place. But the absence I remember wasn't vacancy. It was held. So the question caught me. "Is it possible that you look a tiny bit like this when treating him with contempt? I've discovered I do" also caught me, because I thought of the face I make at Holt.

The worst thing I know of anyone doing: Reserve Police Battalion 101, the men who shot the Jews of Józefów in 1942. Browning says they drank. Could they have done it with kindness in their eyes? No. And the drinking is the turning away, made chemical. I sat with this a long time.

The bad essence passages. Some of it read as heading off an objection: "When I try to make it a cause, I'm just recreating the problem one level deeper." That's asserted, not shown, and I wanted the argument. But "If darkness were an essence, I would be right to be afraid of it, and ashamed, and I'd have to keep proving it's in others and not in me. But if it's something I *do*, then I can stop." That caught me, because I've spent forty years half-believing my capacity for cruelty is temperament. Then "Like the *presence* of something, rather than the absence it actually is" is the first place you assert metaphysics without a hedge: "actually is." Privatio boni. I noticed it. Then you gave the room back: "If that doesn't match what you've seen, keep the question open." Thank you for that.

The integrity saying at the end of the section: it landed, mostly. The proverb itself is a little worn; I've seen it on a poster in our student center. "But someone always is: me." turned it, and "And I knew it was there the whole time" took me back to Marcus. It's a good place for it.

**Common knowledge.** This is my field, more or less, so I read it harder. "It was a charge of hypocrisy. It's as if he was saying: *you're doing the thing we all do to enable harm—turning away from yourself*." Here's where one person became everyone: "we all do." Your enemy's gaze accused you of hypocrisy. That it accused you of something we all do is your gloss, not his. I bought it tentatively.

The hall of mirrors: yes. David and I have had fights that turned into fights about the face one of us made during the fight, and then about the denial of the face. You and Lewis are describing the same regress. And "Nothing has to be computed" is right, and right in Lewis's way: common knowledge doesn't require anyone to run the infinite iteration. It requires a shared basis that both parties can see the other sees. Your basis is that we each know turning away from the inside. I didn't need to read that twice.

"Think of the last time you sent that look yourself." Two weeks ago, faculty meeting, Pruitt said "learning outcomes" for the third time and I looked across the table at Paul Reyes and let my eyes do it. When Pruitt glanced over I gave him a pleasant face. Did I know Paul would read it? Of course. "How did you know?" I knew because I've received that look, and because I knew what I meant by it. I had my out: I was only looking at a colleague. "Did it feel like justice, just a little sweeter than justice needs to be?" Yes. That question was beautifully exact.

"Try it now: imagine the same act without turning away." I imagined writing "solid and conscientious" while holding Marcus's face in view, and the knowledge that it was payback. I couldn't type it. I'd have had to write the real letter or no letter. "Does it surprise you that a stranger could count on that about you?" Not surprised. A little irritated at being counted on. But you were right to count.

"Contempt only works because we already know we're the same kind of thing: beings who must *turn away* to cause harm." Did each step hold? Mostly. My look at Paul worked because he knew from the inside what it was doing; I knew because I've had it done to me. So far you've shown that the sender and the receiver share the knowledge. "Same kind of thing" asks a bit more. And "must turn away to cause harm" still has that universal "must" from your first page. I'd say you've earned it for me and Paul and Pruitt and you. You haven't earned it for everyone, and you know that.

"It's why my message functioned as a taunt." I read that paragraph twice. The idea that the shared understanding was the medium for announcing its own violation is genuinely new to me, and it's hard to say in English. I got it the second time.

Then the surprise. "But it landed precisely because I hold the one he accused me of breaking, and his outrage in making it showed that he holds it too." I thought of Holt, who spends half of every broadcast accusing liberals of hypocrisy. I had always heard that as cynicism. But you can only be outraged by hypocrisy if you hold the standard being betrayed. Holt and I answer to the same standard. I didn't like realizing it, and I think it's true. "We were never disagreeing about what the ground was. We were fighting over which of us still stood on it." That's the line I'll quote to people.

"Love and contempt are addressed to the same place." Where someone's contempt reached in me: a senior colleague in 1993 who told someone, in my hearing, that I was "a very successful hire, from a certain point of view." It went straight past my pride, past my CV, to whatever in me knew I counted, and it tried to put that out. Your sentence named where it landed. I'd never located it before.

"I found this the way I found my own old wrongdoing." I believed you. I can't check it, of course, it's testimony. But it had the same shape as my card from Marcus, and that's the only evidence I could have had.

"Evil." "It is the thing I know as "evil"—cruelty for its own sake; the very thing I hate most in the world" I accepted as a definition. "It's why I don't think "pure evil" is a different kind of thing, but the same thing at full strength." I flinched. I'm Jewish; I don't hand out "same kind of thing" easily when the comparison is to Józefów. But I'd already conceded it two pages earlier with Battalion 101, who were ordinary men, and Arendt took the same flinch for the same view. So I accepted it with a flinch, and kept trusting you. "Of *course* I don't want to believe that" closed it a bit. It reads as though disagreement were reluctance.

"I couldn't have been argued into this: I had to taste that quality in myself." Yes, and that's the honest limit of the whole chapter. It's also why I did the exercises.

From "my allies are the entire rest of society" on: it followed. I already knew most of it. "Fighting evil is the place where my own darkness is most likely to come out, because it has the best cover" is something anyone who has watched a campus pile-on knows. The paragraph about contempt sometimes stopping misbehavior read as you heading off an objection. "It's possible for the fierceness to stay while the poison goes. What's left is fierce kindness." Tom Okafor, exactly.

Corrected someone badly: my son Eli at sixteen, caught lying about where he'd been. I said, "What is wrong with you? You're smarter than this," and I meant the second sentence as an insult. He went silent for a week. Three years later he dropped out of a summer program and I sat on the porch with him and asked what had happened, and I was afraid for him rather than right about him. What was different? I wasn't defending my verdict. I wanted him back.

"When someone treats others with contempt in front of you, do you trust them more, or less?" Less. Instantly. I thought of a colleague who mocks students' emails at lunch; I would not tell him anything I was ashamed of. That check was quick and decisive, and it does a lot of work for so few words.

**The close of "Common knowledge."** "If my enemy and I answer to the same thing": I took "the same thing" to be the good, or less grandly, the standard that persons aren't to be treated as nothing. The advisor passage was strong. "But it's not an advisor." I do treat it as an advisor; it's how I've handled every grading compromise and every committee vote I'm not proud of. "that I have the right to look at what is right and say *not today*" is Kant without the jargon, and better.

"We may never agree on a definition of "good." But we already agree on how to orient toward it, and part of us knows it." I partly found this and partly granted it. As a moral realist I want it to be true, which makes me suspicious of how easily I nodded. Holt and I don't agree on much, but I do think we'd both recognize the look-away for what it is. "And part of us knows it" is the third or fourth time you've said that, and it's starting to sound like a refrain.

It felt like an ending. "and yet I can't take credit when it does" is a quiet, generous last line. I thought the chapter was over. Then "The spread" started.

**The spread.** The first paragraphs I knew already. The quiet-contempt paragraph says again what the cold version said earlier. Then this caught me hard: "The sigh that tells them what I really think of them, dressed up to make me seem like the reasonable one." Sunday, dinner with the Hendersons, David got the year of our trip to Lisbon wrong and I corrected him and sighed. Small. "It only has to be *evidence*." That's what it was. When you ask whether it felt like me: yes, "the most reasonable version of me," your phrase exactly. With his face in view and no reasons: no. He looked at his plate for a second. I saw it and went on talking.

"*That wasn't the real me* can be used as an excuse, but I also think it's true." I half believed it. You keep the responsibility, which is what makes it not an excuse. But I've heard students say "that's not who I am" about plagiarism, and I don't believe them. I'm not sure I can tell the difference from outside, and I think you'd say I can't.

"Whether that number ever goes to zero is something you can discover for yourself." This is the freest sentence in the chapter. And "Sometimes it looks like me writing this book *at* people, instead of *for* them" disarmed me. I'd been half thinking it.

**The exercise at the end.** I did it with Denise from Giant Eagle again. It took a while. The recognition came, roughly as you describe; it's Murdoch's M and D, the mother-in-law looked at again until she's real. Wishing her harm from there didn't work. Then "the hood, the number instead of the name, the word for vermin." My grandmother's sister had a number. I stopped reading for a few minutes. Then I put Holt's face there. "For me the story is there before the face is." Yes. "Smug" arrived before his face did. I had never noticed that the word comes first.

Did it gather things up? It did. But it was one ending too many. The chapter had already ended twice for me, at "can't take credit" and at the face-test of the sigh. I'd have been glad if the exercise came sooner, or the spread were shorter.

**Did it seem to end before it did?** Yes, at the end of "Common knowledge," and again, faintly, at the integrity saying.

**Length.** If I had to cut a fifth: the quiet-contempt paragraph in "The spread" (the cold version already did it); the paragraph about contempt sometimes stopping people; "Here's what I've found in myself" and the one after it, about not being able to choose to see their better nature, which are true but a lecture; the first two paragraphs of "Whose intention," which restate the Encounter; one of the two "don't call yourself names" passages. The early one ("If seeing that makes you want to call yourself names, hold off") and the late one ("If seeing this makes you want to hide, notice that's the same verdict pointed inward") say the same thing. Keep the late one. "Part of me knows" appears so often it stops carrying weight.

**Words.** "Darkness" made me wince the first time and less after; it's a theologian's word and you're not writing theology. "Corruption," "poison," "inject": a lot of contamination imagery. "Here's the thing" and "crucially" sit in the narrated mind, and that's fine. "Mash that first button" is good. "Better nature" is Lincoln's and I was glad of it.

**Last.** Was any of it obvious? Some. That contempt corrupts the contemptuous, that crusades give cover to cruelty, I knew. What became newly consequential is narrower and more useful. The "proven right" line, Holt's hypocrisy outrage, and the sigh. This week I'm going to stop watching Holt. Not as a resolution, but because I now see what I watch him for. And on Thursday there's a faculty meeting, and when Pruitt says "outcomes," I'd like to not look at Paul. We'll see.

Would I pass it on? Yes, to my friend Ruth Kessler, who teaches high school civics and hasn't spoken to her brother since 2016. She'd do the exercises honestly, and she wouldn't be bothered by the "must"s the way my colleagues would. I would not give it to my department. They'd fight the transcendental claims and never try the cashier, and the cashier is where it works.

With real gratitude, and some pencil marks,

Naomi

Dear Aditya,

I read the second file the next morning, before my ten o'clock. For the first two-thirds I kept checking whether I'd been handed the same file twice. Then "Common knowledge" began and the two versions diverged, and from there to the end I read them side by side with a pencil in each hand, so to speak.

**Which did more to me, and which treated me better.** The chapter (1-chapter.md) did more to me. The second file (2-another-version.md) treated me better in "Common knowledge," and worse at the end. I didn't expect to split it that way, so let me explain.

That's where I found Holt's outrage at liberal hypocrisy turning out to be evidence that he and I answer to the same standard. Both versions have the sentence that did it, "his outrage in making it showed that he holds it too." But in the chapter the whole section is built toward it, and it hit harder there.

What treated me better was the second file's route to "same kind of thing." In my first letter I complained that the chapter's gloss on the enemy's gaze, "*you're doing the thing we all do to enable harm—turning away from yourself*," was where one person became everyone. The enemy accused you of hypocrisy. "We all do" was your addition. The second file doesn't make that move. It says: "Part of me was counting on him to see me go missing and know what it meant, because he's done it himself." That's grounded in him, not in all of us. That's an inference to the best explanation, honestly labelled as a question. I can say yes to it from my own case, my look at Paul across the faculty table, without signing up for everyone. The chapter's version asked me to sign first.

**What the second file has that the chapter should recover.**

"Part of me was counting on him to see me go missing and know what it meant, because he's done it himself." (second file) This matters more than anything else on the list. "Go missing" is better than anything in the chapter's version of this step. It's what I saw in the hotel-room interviewer in 1989: not hostility, absence. And "because he's done it himself" puts the shared knowledge where I can check it, in the person, rather than in "we all."

"And it had to show him clearly that I was doing it, and that I was doing it *to* him, so that it read as a signal and not a slip." (second file) The chapter says the same thing as "It needed him to recognize it *as* hypocrisy," but "a signal and not a slip" is the distinction I actually needed. My look at Paul was meant to be read as a signal. If Pruitt had caught it, I'd have claimed it was a slip. That's the out.

"I was publicly denying (even to myself) knowledge that we privately hold in common, and showing him that I was." (second file) As someone who teaches Lewis, this is the cleanest statement of the mechanism in either version. It's nearly a definition. The chapter spends two paragraphs ("I described it as a series of unfolding layers" and "Our shared understanding wasn't just the background") getting to roughly this, and I had to read the second of those twice. I'd keep "Nothing has to be computed," which is true and which the second file lacks, but I'd want this sentence too.
