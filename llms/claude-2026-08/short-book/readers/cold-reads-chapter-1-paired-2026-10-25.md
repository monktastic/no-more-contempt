## Dana: condensed Chapter 1 (m1)

### Letter 1 — after the preface and Chapter 1

Jeannie,

Okay. You asked what it did to me, so here it is, and I'm not going to be nice about the parts that bugged me.

The preface almost lost me. Second sentence he mentions plant medicines, and then there's "darkness spreads between us while masquerading as righteousness" and a blessing at the end, and I thought, here we go, a retreat-center book. What kept me was "It is not one that I can claim to be fully living myself." Somebody who says that up front, I'll give a chapter.

What happened in me. He starts by asking you to remember something you did wrong and knew was wrong while you did it. I went straight to the Camry job four years ago when I let Marcus take the blame for my bad color match. Did it feel like new information when I finally owned it? No. Not for a second. And then he asks if you deflected when somebody got close, and whether part of you knew you were deflecting. Rick brought it up at lunch once and I changed the subject to the Browns, and yes, I knew. I'd never thought of changing the subject as a thing I DID. That was the first hit.

The two strangers part got me with Walt. Remember Walt Nowak, who I apprenticed under? He tore into me in front of the whole bay when I faked a frame-pull sign-off, and I never dug in, I just went red and fixed it. The book says the second stranger "could have been *harsher* than the first person... and it still wouldn't have made you dig in." That's Walt exactly. Harsher than anybody, and I've loved him for it for twenty-eight years.

Then it went where I was afraid it would. You know the Gary bit I do at holidays. There's a list in the middle of the chapter, written from the side of the person getting the contempt: "Deny it, and you're the kind of person who does. Get angry, and that's proof too. Stay calm, and you don't even care. Apologize, and it's too convenient." I read that and saw Gary on my porch two years ago doing his ninth step, and me turning his apology into "Step nine, take two" at Thanksgiving. There is nothing that man can do that I won't make into a bit. I knew that. Nobody ever wrote it out in front of me.

And then this one, which I'm going to be thinking about for a while: "**Love and contempt are addressed to the same place.** One says *I know you're in there.* The other says *there's nobody in there*, and they both say it to the somebody who is." Right after it: "A child can't refuse it; he believes it, and carries it for years." I thought of Gary's dad first, the stuff Gary told me about growing up. Then I thought of my own boy, who is half Gary, sitting at that table hearing his mother say his father is nobody. I put it down for a while after that.

Where it peaked was near the end, a section called The Spread. He finally gets to the kind I do: "And I send it *about* people, to my allies, with nobody's face in view." And: "Everyone in the conversation learns that the verdict is safe to hold, and leaves a little more practiced at it." My sister has her own Gary impression now. I laughed at it at Easter. Then the small stuff we do to people we love, "The *what's wrong with you?*" and "The look on their face that tells me it landed, which I pretend not to have seen." I've said "you sound just like your father" to him twice. I saw his face both times. I pretended I didn't.

Then he asks if you've ever been the second stranger yourself, and I have. Every week at the shop. I'm Walt with the new kids. I'm just not Walt at home, and I'm not Walt about Gary. I don't know what to do with that yet.

Where it sagged. A big stretch after the main story, the sections called "The How" and "The Mechanism," goes over the same ground again in vaguer words. He'd already shown me with his own story (him telling himself he was "holding someone accountable" and then using the guy's reaction as proof). That story was good. Then he explains it again with "turned away" about every other sentence, and "the need," and "it accomplishes its goal," and I had to slow way down. And the stuff about "essence" and "an absence that behaves like a presence" near the end. That's poster-at-the-retreat-center talk. If it's the devil, say the devil, I was raised Catholic, I can take it. If it isn't, tell me what it is.

The ending sagged too. After the kitchen-table stuff, he closes on "an orientation" and being "*turned toward all of life*" and "for them and through them for the world." I was sitting in my own dining room and he took me back out to the whole world. Though there was one good line: "We were fighting over which of us still stood on it."

Where I was lost:
- "On the other hand, he can't prove I'm doing this, can he? No. And *neither can I*. So if he accuses me of it, he can only be projecting." Three reads. Still not sure.
- "I am in limbo about provoking him, and revealing that I am in limbo is itself the provocation. The signal is the pretense that there is no signal." Then he tells me if "the recursion tickles you, Appendix 1 takes it apart." I don't know what recursion is and I don't have Appendix 1. Felt like being left standing at a counter.
- "My enemy sees I'm turned away, even though I don't. *Because* I don't. And he sees it as *evil*, for the same reason I see him that way." What reason?
- He opens with "Hold them the way you'd hold puzzle pieces," which was fair warning. But the very first paragraph ("it only works because you're the same kind of thing, and both of us know it") didn't make sense to me until well past the middle, when he says "It works because we are the *same* kind of thing." That's a long time to hold a piece.
- He has a lot of names for what I think is one thing: the part that knew, better nature, inner knowing, the innocent part, "I," and at the end "an orientation" and "shared ground." I guessed they're all the same. I shouldn't have to guess.
- The preface says contempt is evil, then Chapter 1 says believing in "some alien quality called 'evil'" is the mistake. I worked out what I think he means, but I had to work it out myself.

Where I was handled. Not as much as I expected, honestly. He mostly asks. Some spots: "what you just recalled wasn't [unconscious]. It was right there to see." He was right in my case, but he told me before I told him. And "could you feel the sense in which the other person is somehow absent or 'turned away' from himself?" No, I couldn't. Parts guys who call me sweetheart aren't absent, they're smug. Then "Why would they have to turn away from anything, if what they are is essentially bad?" That question already has its answer in it. And "which would you keep?" at the end. Obviously the one that knew. I could see the answer he wanted from across the room.

Sounded like a machine or a committee: "a uniquely insidious expression of evil," "forsake a fellow being," "the realm of those who matter," "demote his inner knowing," "embrace their darkness," "transcends sides," "invite you to remember," "The great lie we tell ourselves." And the rhythm where it goes "It isn't this. It's that." Over and over: "It feels like accountability, but it's a verdict," "Whatever he was standing up for wasn't *his*. It wasn't a rule, or a side," "My excuses aren't there to convince *it*; they're there to convince *me*," "What I've been hiding isn't that I *could*... It's that I'm *already doing it*," "This inability to define goodness isn't a flaw." Once it's punchy. Ten times it's a habit. And "darkness." Six times on one page in the main story. I lost count after.

One thing I didn't buy: he says "pure evil" is the same mistake as his, turned all the way up, "Innocent at the root, and fully responsible." I read true crime. I'm not there. He says "I now think," so at least he owns it. But he gives me nothing to check it against.

One thing I want to say for him: "Even this book can carry it: sometimes I'm writing it *at* people, instead of *for* them." Most books like this would never say that. And "It must be fought, with everything I have." I was scared this was going to be a let-everybody-off book. It's not.

How it ended. It didn't leave me hungry so much as it left me with homework. The ending itself was flat, a sermon after the best part. But I'm carrying two questions, and they're mine, not his: How do I quit the Gary bit without pretending Gary didn't do what he did? And why can I be Walt with a 22-year-old who botched a weld, and not with the father of my son? If Chapter 2 touches either one, I'll read it tonight. If it's more about "orientation," I'll read it slower.

One more thing. I've got Thanksgiving in four weeks. I already know I'm not doing the bit. I don't know what I'm doing instead.

Dana

#### Letter 1 — after the preface and Chapter 1

Hey,

Okay. You owe me a beer, and I'm not sure if it's because this was good or because it ruined my weekend.

Chapter 1 is a different animal. He doesn't preach, mostly. He asks. And the questions worked on me, which I didn't expect.

What happened. He asks you to remember something you did that you knew was wrong. Mine came up instantly. Years ago, a bad winter, I put aftermarket parts on two cars and billed them as factory. I told myself the adjusters screw us on labor anyway. His italics — "*Everyone does it. It's not a big deal. Just this once.*" — were my exact words. Then he says picture the person in front of you. The cheap fender went on Mrs. Petrakis's car. She brings us cookies at Christmas. If she'd been standing in the bay, I couldn't have done it. I'd never put it that way to myself.

Then the second stranger, the one who catches you but doesn't treat you like garbage. I had one. My old lead tech, Ray. He looked at the part, looked at me, said nothing. "He was searching my face for some sign that I knew, and that made me look for it in myself." That's Ray exactly. I redid both jobs on my own dime and never told him.

The real recognition came at the part where the guy he's chewing out hardens and thinks "why should I be the lone exception? I'd be a chump." And then the book turns and asks: "Did some part of you feel you *deserved* something? That everyone does it, and only a fool plays it straight?" I had to put it down. I've got a story I tell about an adjuster named Brent who lowballed a single mom, and how I told him off. I'm the hero in that one. And I've got the fender story, which I don't tell. They're the same story. I cheated because I'd already decided adjusters were crooks. He never said that. He just asked, and I got there. That's the best thing in the chapter and maybe the best thing anybody's done to me in a book.

The second thing is harder. He says, almost in passing, "A child can't refuse it; he believes it, and carries it for years." You know I do Mike's voice at family dinners. It always gets a laugh. Danny used to laugh. He stopped about a year ago and now he goes to the basement. Mike's never in the room when I do it. Danny is. Danny's got Mike's laugh and Mike's walk. I don't know what to do with that yet, but I'm not doing the voice at Thanksgiving. The book didn't mention divorced kids or anything. I got there on my own, which is probably why it stuck.

Then near the end there's an exercise: think of a time you felt the nasty thing coming and didn't send it. Last spring Danny got a D and "you're just like your father" was in my mouth. I didn't say it. I sat down and asked. He told me about a kid who'd been riding him all semester. More in ten minutes than all year. So I've done the other thing too, once at least.

And a few spots go over the top: "I don't care if I have to smother his cries to do it" — nobody's smothering anybody. "Smother" shows up three times. "Darkness" maybe a dozen, and he never tells me what it is. I filled it in as "the part of you that can hurt somebody and feel fine about it," but that's my guess. Also lines 152 to 158 say again what 131 to 137 already said. I felt it the third time around.

Where I was lost.

- "a uniquely insidious expression of evil" (preface).
- "and the book earns it" (preface). Then earn it.
- "It only works if we already have a shared basis of understanding."
- "That itself is implicit in the communication."
- "It was intentional and consciously accessible, but done in limbo."
- "he just has to see that I'm listening to my thoughts, period, and not to something deeper." That's yoga class. If he means conscience, say conscience.
- "give them license to embrace their darkness." Goth poster.

How it ended. Short. "We were fighting over which of us still stood on it" is a good line, and it made Brent make sense. We were both acting like the one who cared about the customer. Then the last question, "What is it that two people who agree on nothing can both be serving?", is pretty much the same question he asked back on the first page of the two-strangers section. It felt like he asked it, didn't answer, and asked it again. The paragraph right before said contempt "doesn't stop at them," and I was holding Danny in my head waiting for him to show me where it goes, and the chapter just stopped.

Do I want Chapter 2? Yes. The question I'm carrying is his question, what's the thing we're both serving. I'd bet conscience or God, and that he'll be careful not to say God. The question I'm really carrying is what I do about Danny.

#### Letter 2 — after the Chapter 2 opening

Hey again,

Worth reading, but only the top half.

The first page or so is the best kind of thing he does: he hands me cases and lets me check. - "Meanwhile, the part that knows quietly keeps on knowing." Chapter 1 had "It kept on quietly knowing what it knew." Almost word for word.

Chapter 1 already said it better: "The part that knew might not have been able to say how it knew." Use that.

Big claim, sounds like "the devil's greatest trick." And I couldn't tell whose voice the second sentence was. His? Sarcasm? The lie talking?

So: the top half I'd hand to anybody. The bottom half I'd cut by a third, and lose "ineffable."

#### Letter 3 — after the cut passages

Hey,

Well, that explains the missing page.

4. "If I hadn't needed to prove myself good—if I'd seen, in that moment, that part of me already *was* good, in a way that couldn't be taken from me—I might have had the courage to act as the second stranger did." Four years of Al-Anon in one sentence. It goes in Chapter 1 right after "That in **no way** excuses me. I'm still fully responsible for all the harm I've caused from this confusion, and to stop doing it." He'd just said he turned away "out of the innocent need to know myself as good." This finishes that thought. It would also have made the end of Chapter 1 feel like there's a way out, not just a diagnosis.

5. Half of this one: "the part that proves can be pointed anywhere. Even when it's pointed away from goodness, cut loose from the part that knows, it can prove that it isn't." In Chapter 2, right after "And that itself is a big clue: *who's to say?*" That's exactly the explanation that line was missing. I read "who's to say" three times and couldn't tell what it was a clue to. This tells you. Leave off "transcends sides" and "It's an orientation." Those are seminar words.

Don't miss: "My excuses aren't there to convince *it*..." (out of context I don't know what "it" is), and "This inability to define goodness isn't a flaw..." (Chapter 2's last paragraph already says it, and "invite you to remember" is seminar).

Would they have spoiled Chapter 2 if they'd been at the end of Chapter 1? Some would and some wouldn't.

- The "Not a definition of good" bit and the "turned toward all of life" bit would have taken Chapter 2's ending. If I'd read them in Chapter 1, the last two paragraphs of Chapter 2 would have been a repeat. But the cut version is better than what Chapter 2 ended up with, so I'd move it into Chapter 2 rather than leave it out. And I'd put just the one line, "We may never agree on one, and we don't need to," or something like it, at the end of Chapter 1 so the first line of Chapter 2 has something to grab.
- The "worst thing I can do," the "ideal victim," and the "already *was* good" lines don't spoil anything in Chapter 2. They belong to his own story in Chapter 1. They're what Chapter 1's ending was missing. Cutting them made the ending feel like it stopped mid-sentence.

One thing I'll say plainly. The lines he cut are some of the most human ones he wrote. "I needed others to carry the darkness I was hiding from myself" is a man telling on himself. The lines he kept in Chapter 2's ending are a man explaining. I'll take the first kind every time.

Dana

## Theo: condensed Chapter 1 (m1)

### Letter 1: after the preface and Chapter 1

Dear Nora,

You asked what it did to me. I'll tell you, though not in the order it happened, because the order it happened in includes me putting the pages down and walking around the block in my slippers.

The preface first, quickly. The second paragraph is a real confession ("*Because* I was convinced of that" is a turn somebody actually heard), and the Gospel of Philip epigraph is a good choice; it isn't the Gospel I grew up on, which is part of why it works. Then the preface starts selling. "Uniquely insidious," "masquerading as righteousness," "patiently waiting to be remembered," and a request for collaborators that belongs in a cover email. And he tells me calling contempt evil is "not a value judgment," which it plainly is. I went into Chapter 1 braced.

Then Chapter 1 did the thing I didn't expect: it started with my wrong, not my enemy's. "Have you ever done something you knew was wrong at the time, but couldn't fully admit it to yourself until later?" You know the one I have. The line I took from a student and put in my second book. He asked whether owning it felt like new information or like something I'd always known, and the answer was so plainly the second that I felt caught before he'd accused me of anything. Then: "Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" The part of me that knew it was theft never made a case. The part that said "poets steal" never shut up. I'd never noticed the asymmetry.

The recognitions, in order of how hard they hit:

The two strangers, and specifically: "I'd bet on it at any odds. But on what evidence?" I invented a man to accuse me and had a whole file on him in ten seconds. That is my dinner-party act, exactly, and how Mark became "the careerist."

The two buttons. Vengeance for Jonah, or a pill that changes his heart. I hesitated over the pill, and what I found under the hesitation was this: if his heart changes, he might write an honest book, and it might be good, and I don't want that. Two paragraphs later the book said contempt provokes "my enemies into becoming more of what I need them to be." I'd got there a step before he said it. That's the only time in the chapter I predicted before I was told, and it's the moment I trusted him.

"A child can't refuse it; he believes it, and carries it for years." I teach sixteen-year-olds. I once called a boy "our resident Hallmark laureate" in front of the class. The class laughed. He didn't turn in another poem that year. I hadn't thought of him in years.

And "The Spread," which is where it found me properly. For most of the chapter his contempt is a hot glare, face to face, "unquotable," and I kept thinking: that isn't mine. Mine is quotable. Mine is a line said over wine to people who laugh. Then: "And I send it *about* people, to my allies, with nobody's face in view," and "Everyone in the conversation learns that the verdict is safe to hold, and leaves a little more practiced at it." Then the cold kind, "a politeness with nothing behind it," which is how I say good morning to Mark. Then "I send it to people I love," the sigh "dressed up to make me seem like the reasonable one," and "The look on their face that tells me it landed, which I pretend not to have seen." You know what Sarah told me last month, that she's started rehearsing what she says to me so I won't have material. I have seen that look. I pretended not to. That's where I went for the walk.

The sentence of the chapter, for me: "Love and contempt are addressed to the same place.
