## Dale (55, Lakota addiction counselor, 25 years sober; cold, the silence)

### Stage 1

Aditya,

I read it at my kitchen table over two mornings, coffee and the dog. I'll tell you what happened, in order, and I won't dress it up. You asked for that.

First thing. Your opening sentence is long, but you put yourself in it before you put me in it. "My conviction," "part of me knows." Most books like this start by telling me about people. You started with you. Then: "Maybe you'll recognize something of yourself in it. Or maybe not." That "or maybe not" bought you a lot with me. I've had a lot of people come to the rez with a book under their arm and a lesson for us. You didn't come in that way. And all the way through, you never borrowed one thing from my people or anybody's. No feather on it. I noticed, and I'm telling you because I'd have closed it if you had.

**The memory.** I didn't have to look far. 1999, I was drinking. I told Arlene I was at my cousin Leonard's helping him pull a transmission. I was at the bar on Main. Two of the kids had the flu. When I finally owned it, in my fifth step with Elmer in 2001, it was not new information. Not one ounce of it. I knew it while I was ordering the second one. That's the whole fourth step, really, and every man in my circles knows it. So your first claim I bought outright: "that knowledge was never unconscious." That's not theory to me. That's a Tuesday night.

"If you did, did that happen passively?" No. When Arlene came close I started fights. Picked one about money, about her mother, anything. I was good at it. I knew what I was doing while I did it. You got that right, and I'd say it to a room.

Then this: "If seeing that makes you want to call yourself names, hold off. That's not owning up; it's another way to hide." I stopped there. My daughter Renee told me two years ago I forgive everybody but myself and my dad. I thought she was being soft on me. You're telling me it's a hiding place, not a punishment. I didn't like that. I think it's right.

"Everyone does it. It's not a big deal. Just this once." That's the voice. We call it the disease talking. "Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" Good question, and you left it open. I sat with it. The side that knew never once made a case. It just sat there being right. The drink had all the lawyers.

**The cashier.** I used the girl at the Loaf 'N Jug on Haines who calls me "hon." Smile and thank-you went right out of me, no reason needed. The sneer wouldn't go. I had to make up a story about her first — she's slow, she's rude — before I could even picture it. So yes. Bought it, tentatively. I'll tell you though, the first cashier my mind went to was a white lady at a store downtown in 1988 who followed me up and down the aisles. The sneer went at her easy. But she'd done something to me, so she doesn't count for your test. I set her aside honest.

**Remorse.** This one I fought. I tried to think of remorse for being decent. I've loaned money to men in circle and watched it go to a bottle. Felt like a fool, felt angry. Not remorse. Fine. But "Can you imagine anyone feeling it in reverse?" — I thought of my father. Sober, some mornings, he'd be tender with us. Fix my bike, put his hand on my head. And the next day he'd be meaner, like he was ashamed of the day before. For an hour I thought, there it is, your reverse. Then I thought about where he learned that softness gets you beat, which was Holy Rosary, from the priests. What he felt wasn't going against himself. It was fear of what was done to him. So I came back around and bought it. "Maybe some cannot access it, but I doubt it's ever reversed." I doubt it too, now. But it cost me an hour, and I'd want you to know a man like my dad is the test case, not the lier-awake you picked.

"I'm capable of the worst acts humans have committed." Yes. I knew that before I was thirty. Not grandiose to me. Just true. Drunk men know it.

**The confrontation.** Believable. I had a man in mind: Curtis, in my Tuesday circle, three winters back. Shows up with sage and a story about his grandfather's medicine every time his PO is about to violate him. I named it in front of the circle. Hard. And I'll be straight, reading your monologue I could hear my own. "Surely that's not what I'm doing. I'm just being fierce with evil, right? Right." I said a version of that to my wife in the truck after. "Somebody has to protect the young guys from that game." Which is true. And it's also what I said.

Where I skimmed: the invitation paragraph and "It's still a net benefit to the world!" I got the joke, but I'd already got it. "Ha! Classic." felt a little cute. The rest held.

**The cold one.** This is where you got me first, and you got me good. "It's the same verdict, delivered by withdrawal, and it's meant to be felt: by them, or by whoever's watching." That's my silence. Twenty years of program directors, most of them white, most of them gone in three years. I sit there and say nothing, and they can't read it, and I mean them not to be able to read it, and I mean them to feel it. I've been proud of that silence. My uncles did it. I thought it was dignity. Some of it is. Some of it is exactly what you wrote. I read that line maybe five times.

**"I'll just go ahead and name what's happening."** Mostly insight. But I had to read the second paragraph twice. "Neither half alone would be enough to get me what I need: both his reaction and my innocence." Second time I got it, and it's sharp. "The signal is the pretense that there is no signal." Then you switched sides on me: "If you've been on his end, you know how it closes around you. Deny it, and you're the kind of person who does. Get angry, and that's proof too." Aditya, that's being an Indian man in an office in Rapid City. Angry Indian. Quiet Indian, sullen. Polite Indian, he's working you. I've lived inside that paragraph my whole life. You didn't write it about us and I'm glad you didn't. It was truer without it.

"My stories kept changing; my conclusion never did." Every drunk I ever sat with. Me included.

"I'd be a chump." This is where my family is. The church preached love and the school beat my father for speaking Lakota. "So I didn't—and that *was* my role in causing it." Good line. Short. It stays.

**The two checks.** Violent punishment of evil, satisfying? Yes. When one director got walked out in 2019 for fudging numbers I felt good for a week.

"I'll be honest: there is in me." That's the line that keeps me reading you. You went first again. In a circle that's what earns you the right to ask anybody anything.

I didn't refuse that, and I didn't hear anything spooky in it. I heard what we say in the rooms: the disease is out in the parking lot doing push-ups. It wants you. Then this one, and this is the one that'll stay with me longest: "When I'm sure someone was fully aware of what they were doing, I've made them a villain. I can tell you in the first week who's going to relapse and when, and I'm usually right. **The bad essence.** "I want to ascribe all this..." — the first paragraph read a bit like you heading somebody off. I felt the argument more than the man. But then: "Like the *presence* of something evil, rather than what I think it actually is: someone turned away from their own better nature." My father drunk looked exactly like the presence of something evil. To a ten-year-old it was. "Every enemy this part of myself helps me make gives that fear somewhere to point other than at me." Curtis. Sorensen. Him. Maybe that's what Renee was seeing.

"I think it's trying to protect me, and the getting away is a side effect." I tell clients that about drinking: it was medicine once. It's good to hear it said about the other thing.

**"Only a chump believes otherwise."** Landed, and I connected it straight back to "I'd be a chump" earlier. That's what I learned in my father's house: nobody's clean, boy, everybody's working an angle. That's the creed of the boarding-school families, not all of them but plenty. You said I taught it to my enemy. My father taught it to me, and the priests taught it to him. I didn't need you to draw the line. It drew itself.
