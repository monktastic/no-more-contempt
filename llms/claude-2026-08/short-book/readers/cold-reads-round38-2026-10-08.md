## Ingrid (46, philosophy professor, moral psychology and self-deception; Lutheran upbringing, agnostic)

### Stage 1

Dear Aditya,

I read it at the kitchen table on Sunday night after the kids were down, with a pencil, which is how I read everything, and I want to tell you what happened in order. I'll mark where I bought, where I bought on credit, and where I stopped paying. That is my trade. But some of this caught me in places my trade doesn't reach, and I'll try to be honest about those too.

**The opening.** Your central claim, "contempt is the claim that you're a different kind of thing than me, but for it to land, you must be the same kind of thing, and part of you must know it," struck me as a good sentence with a hinge I'd be watching: "kind." I could already feel it could mean something thin (we are both creatures who can do X) or something thick (we are moral equals, answerable to the same good). I bought it tentatively and kept my eye on that word the whole way through. I'll come back to it.

**Limbo.** The memory came at once, which annoyed me. In 2019 I refereed a paper, blind, that I was fairly sure was by a man who had taken my comments apart at the APA Central the spring before. Not unfairly, as it happens. Took them apart in front of a room. My report was careful and cited real weaknesses, and it recommended rejection, and it was harsher than the paper deserved. I told myself the standards were the standards. It took me about two years to say to myself, plainly, that I had been punishing him.

Your question was whether owning up felt like new information. It didn't. It felt like setting down something I'd been carrying with my arm locked. So "It was right there to see—fully available to consciousness—but you were *turned away* from it" matched my case. I should say that in my field this is a live dispute: Mele would tell you most self-deception needs no knowing at all, just motivated belief-formation, and Sartre and Fingarette would side with you. You've picked a side without saying so. For this memory, you picked the right one. I knew. I'd grant you the claim for my case and hold it as open for all cases, which seems to be what you asked for.

I liked "If that doesn't match anything in your memory, the rest of this won't make sense, and I'd rather you know that now." That is the most honest sentence a philosopher could write at that point, and most of us don't.

"Did that happen passively?" No. I remember rehearsing the reasons, especially the line about "methodological standards for the venue," which I'd never have written about a friend's paper. "Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" was the first question that did real work on me. My answer, sitting there: because the arguing side was arguing *against* something, and you can't argue against what you don't know is there. Sartre's point about the liar who must know the truth to hide it, except arrived at through my own referee report instead of a seminar. That was good.

One quibble I let pass: "Nobody ever looked honestly at something in themselves while being told they were worthless for having it." I was raised Lutheran. Every Sunday of my childhood I said I was a poor miserable sinner, and some people I grew up with looked honestly at themselves under that. But the confession came with absolution in the same breath. So maybe you're right after all: nobody looks while being told *only* that.

**The cashier.** Darlene, at the Hy-Vee on Madison Avenue, who calls everyone "hon." The smile went with no reason at all. The sneer would not go until I'd supplied one: she talks too long, there's a line. I caught myself reaching for it. So yes, bought, though I'd note the philosopher's itch: a sneer is a social act, and maybe it needs a context the way a toast does, not because something in me forbids it. I held both. It was a good check.

**Remorse.** This is where I expected to refuse and didn't. The first thing that came to me was Huck Finn, lying awake sure he's going to hell for helping Jim. That is exactly a person wracked by conscience for a kindness; Jonathan Bennett built a famous paper on it. Then I reread your list and found "guilt at breaking a rule I was raised on." That *is* Huck. Twain's whole point is that the rule and the sympathy pull apart and the sympathy is the one that's right. So Huck ended up on your side, which surprised me. I tried my own case. When I covered two weeks of Paul's classes last fall, unasked, I felt something like self-disgust: pushover. But that is "feeling foolish," also on your list, and it never had the sick quality of the referee report. So I bought the asymmetry, for me.

I did mark one thing. If "remorse" means "having gone against myself," and "myself" will later turn out to mean the better nature, then the asymmetry starts to be true by definition. I didn't think you were doing that on purpose. But "Maybe some cannot access it, but I doubt it's ever reversed" is carrying more than the evidence. "Remorse is what turning back feels like" I liked very much.

**The Encounter.** Believable as what a mind does? Yes, painfully, though not my mind. I'm not hot. I've never called anyone out across a room. I did skim the stretch from "Plus, he can't prove I'm doing this" to "*Ha!* Classic." It read a little like a sketch. But "Surely that's not what I'm doing. I'm just being fierce with evil, right? Right." got me, because the "Right." is the sound of a door being held shut.

Then the cold version, and I put the pencil down. "a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them." That is me and Paul, at every department meeting for six years. And "Someone slow, ignorant, or clumsy can get the same verdict, and I write it off as "just being honest."" Paul publishes three papers a year and, as far as I can tell, reads about one. My contempt for him isn't moral. It's intellectual, and I've always called it standards. I've said "I'm just being honest" about him to Rachel, more than once. So the hot version didn't catch me and the cold one did, which is exactly the move you anticipated, and I resented a little being anticipated.

**Integrity.** "So what am I supposed to do—be *nice* to evil?" was not quite the objection forming in me. Mine was: how do you know his "we both know" isn't your projection? But yours was a fair one, and I didn't mind it being raised.

The person who corrected me with integrity was my dissertation advisor in Madison, Helen Ostrander. In my second year I gave a reading-group presentation in which I made Fingarette say something stupider than he says, so I could knock it down. Helen said, in front of eight people, "You know this isn't what he says." Then she waited. It was harsher than anything I'd have done to Paul, and I didn't dig in, and I've never wanted revenge. Reading your teacher story I understood why for the first time. "He was searching my face for something, some sign that I knew, and that made me look for it in myself." That's what Helen did. She waited for me to find that I knew, and I did. She was angry at the strawman, not at me.

So yes, the section came at the right time. Right after the Encounter I needed some evidence that fierceness without the verdict exists, and I already had a case, and your story let me see what it was. "I don't want to make too much of that afternoon" bought you a great deal of trust. The sentence about hypocrisy, "He wasn't being a hypocrite, and hypocrisy was what I'd always used to counter-accuse the people who tried to shame me for what I was," I had to read twice. It's compressed. On the second pass it was true.

I should tell you what I realized about Helen and Paul only later in the chapter. What I did to Fingarette that day is what I despise Paul for: citing someone without having read him properly. I'll come back to that.

"What is it that two people on opposite sides can both be serving?" I actually stopped and worked on this one. My answer was Strawson's: the demand that we regard each other with good will, the thing all the reactive attitudes presuppose. Resentment only makes sense among participants. Contempt is strange on that picture, because it's a reactive attitude trying to pass as the objective one, treating someone as a thing to be managed while being furious with him as a person. I didn't know whether that was your answer, but it was mine, and it was a good question to leave me with.

**The Corruption.** Mostly insight, some lecture. I read the "He can see both halves" paragraph twice. "Neither half alone would be enough to get me what I need: both his reaction and my innocence" made sense on the second reading, and I granted it tentatively. Then "In brief: I am in limbo about provoking him, and showing him that I'm in limbo is itself the provocation" was the best sentence in the chapter for me up to then. "The signal is the pretense that there is no signal" too.

"Deny it, and you're the kind of person who does. Get angry, and that's proof too." I've been on that end, with my brother-in-law Dale, Erik's brother, who farms near Waseca and has opinions about professors. If I argue with him I'm the condescending academic. If I go quiet I think I'm too good to talk to him. I have always experienced that as *his* contempt. Reading it here, I had to admit that he gets the same thing from me, just in my register, and that we close it around each other every Thanksgiving.

"My stories kept changing; my conclusion never did" is the signature of motivated reasoning, and I'll probably steal it for class.

"It won't feel like a loss to him. It will feel like he's gotten a bit wiser, a little more realistic about how the world works." This landed, and not on Dale. On me. I have gotten "more realistic" about my department over the years, and it has never felt like a loss.

"From the inside, being proven right and being right are the same experience. The only difference is who produced the evidence." Good. "If I'd looked carefully, I'd have seen my role in causing it. So I didn't—and that *was* my role in causing it." Also good, but by now I'd heard the shape twice.

**The two checks.** Satisfying to see evil punished? Yes. I was in my car in a parking lot in April 2021 when the Chauvin verdict was read, and what I felt was not only relief that justice had been done. There was something hungrier in it. I've never told anyone that.

The buttons. I thought Dale would be my worst enemy for this, and he isn't; I'd give Dale the pill without thinking. Paul was harder, and what I found surprised me. I'd give Paul the pill, but only if he *knew* he'd been changed. Only if he woke up and understood that he'd been shallow and I'd been right. That is the vengeance button with a halo on it. "Is some part of you itching to mash that first button?" Not the first button. I'd made a third one. "And that lie is exactly what lets me act on it, in ways I rarely recognize until later" is true of me, and the "exactly" didn't bother me there.

**Whose intention.** This is where I first refused. "That's intent, just like your deflection was in the opening" I accepted. Behavior that tracks, counters and steers is goal-directed in the functional sense, and my referee report was that. But then: "I couldn't have masterminded a plan in which two aspects of my mind coordinate so precisely to get me what I need. In that sense, it is not "my" intention." I don't accept that. Lots of precise coordination happens with no planner; habits do it, so does evolution. That I couldn't have designed it doesn't make it someone else's. And "It acts as if it has goals of its own, and it pursues them intentionally" worried me, because the "as if" governs the first clause and seems to drop away in the second. I heard Sartre's objection to Freud's censor: a censor that knows what to hide is just bad faith moved down a floor.

But "Don't mistake the grammar for the claim: I'm not saying there's a separate thing living in me. I'm saying that, while I'm turned away, my own mind behaves as if there were" came in time. It came right after, and it gave me back my footing. "None of this lets me off the hook" mattered too. I could follow everything after it, and I did. I just kept the mastermind sentence off my books.

"When I'm sure someone was fully aware of what they were doing, I've made them a villain. When I'm sure they had no idea, I've made them a machine." That's a real philosophical point and I hadn't seen it put so cleanly. It's the dilemma behind the whole debate about whether self-deceivers are blameworthy, and you've made it a dilemma about contempt instead.

"Why does it matter so much to me that he's bad—unless some part of me suspects he isn't?" Here I felt pushed. The question has its answer inside it.

"If you can find even one memory where it's plainly true of you, that's the one to look at." I found one, and this is where Helen and Paul came together. My contempt for Paul's not reading is sharpest in the weeks when I've cited something I haven't fully read. I checked this against the last year and it's true. Last March I put a footnote to a Korsgaard essay I'd only skimmed, and that same week I was unbearable about Paul at lunch. So what I fear I might be is not "bad." It's Paul. The fraud who produces and doesn't read. That was not something I expected a chapter to do to me on a Sunday night.

"*Only a chump believes otherwise.*" landed, and I did connect it back to "I'd be a chump" in the hypocrite's voice earlier. The cynicism I taught my enemy is the same one I got taught.

**The way out.** "What does "yourself" mean here? I'm not asking a philosophical question." I laughed at that, because of course I'd been about to ask it as one. I tried it with the referee report. "Take a moment, and see if you can find what you were hiding it from." What I found wasn't a nature. It was the one who would have had to *do* something: write a fairer report, or recuse myself. I was hiding it from whoever would have been obliged. That's Fingarette's picture, refusing to spell out an engagement because spelling it out commits you. Is that what you point at? I think it might be the same thing seen from the side. I'm not sure. I didn't find nothing, and I didn't find quite what you named.

"our better nature isn't one part among others. It's a description of what we *are* in the absence of all self-deception." I granted it as your claim. I didn't find it in myself. My upbringing has a rival picture: take away all self-deception and what you find is a heart curved in on itself, *incurvatus in se*. Luther would say you've got it backwards. So it did sound grandiose to me, a little, though "Not because it's nicer. Because it's the one that's home" is beautiful and almost won me over in the moment.

Later I noticed something that changed my answer a bit. You deny that badness is an essence and then assert that goodness is "what we most essentially are." That's Augustine: evil as privation, good as being. It's consistent, and it's an old and respectable view, but it is a big metaphysical commitment, and it arrives in the voice of phenomenology. I don't think you need to apologize for it. I think you should know a philosopher will see it. And even Luther needs a conscience that can recognize the curving as a curving, which is your better nature by another name. So by the end I'd moved from "refused" to "granted, and I can see where the evidence would come from."

**The dehumanizing gaze.** At a conference in 2011, a senior man asked me a question after my talk while looking about a foot to the left of my face, and then talked to the person beside me about my answer. Could I feel he was absent? Yes. That was the strangest part: he was absent, not hostile. "Why would he have to turn away at all, if what he is, is essentially bad?" When I tried to answer, I couldn't make "essentially bad" work. If he were, there'd be nothing to turn away from, and the look wouldn't have taken the effort it visibly took. So the question went your way. It's also a question that presumes he turned away, which you'd just asked me to feel. It worked on me anyway.

"Is it possible that you look a tiny bit like this when treating him with contempt?" Yes. I look exactly like that to Paul. That's the foot-to-the-left look. I've learned it from the man who gave it to me.

"Could they have done it with genuine kindness in their eyes?" I thought of Danny Heinrich, who took Jacob Wetterling, which is the worst thing anyone in Minnesota knows. No. But I noticed "genuine" is doing the work. Groomers wear kindness. The question can't come out the other way, because faked kindness won't count.

**Bad essence.** Part of it read as heading off an objection, particularly "Here's what makes it difficult: when profound self-deception is operating, it looks and behaves exactly like you'd expect a "bad essence" to look." But "If darkness were an essence, I would be right to be afraid of it, and ashamed, and I'd have to keep proving it's in others and not in me. But if it's something I *do*, then I can stop." caught me squarely. Total depravity was an essence in my childhood, and I spent a lot of my adolescence proving it was in other people. That's where my politics came from, honestly.

**The integrity saying.** "Whenever I've broken mine, it was by telling myself that no one was. But someone always is: me." The referee report was *blind*. That was the whole point. No one would ever know who wrote it. So the saying landed with real precision, though at first it had the familiarity of something embroidered on a pillow. It closes The way out well. I'd keep it there.

**Common knowledge.** This was the section I read most carefully, because it's the argument. Here is where each step held and where one asked for more.

"Part of me was counting on him to see me go missing and know what it meant, because he's done it himself." This is a premise about recognition: you can only read going missing from the inside, having done it. That's plausible. It's simulation theory, roughly. But it isn't shown. You can recognize a predator's intent without being one.

"How could it do that, unless it already knew that he's the same kind of thing as me: someone who must *turn away* from himself to cause harm?" Here "kind" is the thin kind: beings who turn away from themselves. Granted.

"**Contempt only works because we already know we're the same kind of thing.**" Here I felt the thin kind quietly become the thick one. Same kind of creature isn't yet same standing. The step asked more than it had shown.

Then the hypocrisy argument rescued it for me. "But the charge only landed because I hold the one he accused me of breaking, and his outrage in making it showed that he holds it too." That's Strawson, and it's sound. Resentment presupposes a shared demand. That gets you from "same kind of creature" to "answerable to the same thing" honestly. "We were never disagreeing about what the ground was. We were fighting over which of us still stood on it." That one I underlined twice. So the gaze argument lost me slightly and the hypocrisy argument brought me back, and between them I'd say the conclusion held.

What surprised me was that the hypocrisy argument is the stronger of the two, and you put it second.

The hall of mirrors: yes. Erik and I, in March, about whether he'd called the plumber. We both knew the fight was about my mother's visit and neither of us could say so without sounding unhinged, because on the record it was about the plumber.

"Try it now: imagine the same act without turning away." I imagined writing the referee report while holding, steadily, that I was paying him back for the APA. I couldn't sign it. Then the philosopher in me noticed that if I *had* been able to, you'd say I'd found a story to hide behind, so the test can't come out the other way. But I still couldn't sign it. "Does it surprise you that a stranger could count on that about you?" A little pushy. No, it didn't surprise me.

Where someone's contempt reached in me: Dale saying "must be nice" about summers. It wasn't my pride. Pride I can shrug off. It reached the place where I'm the daughter of a man who worked the ore docks in Duluth and still feel I have to earn a chair at that table. "**Love and contempt are addressed to the same place.**" was the sentence of the chapter for me. "and they both say it to the somebody who is." I read it aloud to Erik.

The last time I sent that look: Thursday, Paul, department meeting, when he invoked "Frankfurt cases" to mean something they don't mean. I said "interesting." Did I mean him to know I meant it, while keeping an out? Yes; "interesting" is the out. "Did it feel like justice, just a little sweeter than justice needs to be?" Yes.

"I've laid this out as an argument, but that isn't how I came to it." I believed you, and partly for an unflattering reason: the argument has the gaps of someone who found the thing first and built the steps afterward. Someone who came to it by argument would have closed the gap at "same kind of thing." That's not an insult. It's why I trusted it.

"evil": the "be *nice* to evil" and "fierce with evil" sit in the monologue's voice, and fine. "It's why I don't think "pure evil" is a different kind of thing, but the same thing at full strength." I flinched. I thought of sadism, which seems to need the victim's pain fully in view, not turned away from. Then I remembered "Love and contempt are addressed to the same place," and saw that you'd already answered me: the sadist is speaking to the somebody who is, while denying him. You have the resources. I didn't stop trusting you, but it took me a minute.

From "my allies are the entire rest of society" on: it arrived a little suddenly. The comment section was vivid; I know it. "Contempt can sometimes stop people's misbehavior" I already knew, and the paragraph after, "My contempt might fool people watching from afar," I mostly skimmed. The question about correcting someone badly worked. Nils, my eleven-year-old, lied about screen time in August, and I said "I can't believe you'd lie to me," in the voice. He hid the iPad better. Two weeks later I sat on his bed and asked him what he'd been hoping to get away from, and he told me. "fierce kindness" made me wince a little as a phrase, though not as an idea. "Everything I've been able to accomplish with contempt, I've been able to accomplish better without it" is your report, so I'll take it. I'm not sure it's true of me yet.

**"Part of me doesn't want to believe."** I took "the same thing" to be what the hypocrisy charge appealed to: the demand for regard, the good we're oriented toward even when we disagree about its contents. "I treat it like an advisor offering an opinion, which I can weigh against other practical concerns and set aside if the price is right." "But it's not an advisor." That caught me. I teach the overridingness of morality every spring, and I treat it as an advisor every week. "I'm just another person who knew what was right, decided it was negotiable, and then looked for someone worse than me to prove I wasn't so bad after all." Paul, again. I'd found him already. The chapter seemed to me to end at "especially when I've been given every reason to abandon it." It felt like a last line.

**The spread.** "the next stranger he won't give the benefit of the doubt, the kid who watches him do it" caught me because of Nils. "And I send it *about* people, to my allies, with nobody's face in view." Rachel and I, lunch, Paul. "Everyone in the conversation learns that the verdict is safe to hold, and leaves a little more practiced at it." Do I trust Rachel more or less for how she talks about Paul? Less. I wouldn't tell her about the referee report. And it follows she trusts me less too.

The person I love: Erik, the dishwasher, the bowls on the top rack. "The sigh that tells them what I really think of them, dressed up to make me seem like the reasonable one." "For one second the verdict flashes through my eyes, and I make sure they see it, and then I go back to loving them as if nothing had happened." That was the hardest paragraph in the chapter to read. Did it feel like me when I sent it? Yes: "It felt like the most reasonable version of me in the room." That's exactly it. Looking back with his face in view, no.

"*That wasn't the real me* may sound like an excuse, and it can be used as one. But I also think it's true." I half believed it. Real-self views make me nervous professionally; they let people disown whatever they like. But you put "it's mine to answer for" right beside it, and that's the version I can believe.

"Sometimes it looks like me writing this book *at* people, instead of *for* them." Disarming, and I think honest.

"If seeing this makes you want to hide, notice that's the same verdict pointed inward." You said this already, at the start, with "If seeing that makes you want to call yourself names, hold off." The second one is better placed, and I needed it by then.

**The exercise.** I did it with Darlene, since she'd been my cashier. It took a while, and then she was looking out. It gathered things up; the cashier at the start was suddenly back. Then Paul's face. "For me the story is there before the face is. That's the turning away, live." Same for me. "Paul, who hasn't read the Strawson he's citing" arrived before his face did. That was the best demonstration in the chapter, because it happened on schedule. "the hood, the number instead of the name, the word for vermin" was strong; I thought of *inyenzi*.

But I'll be honest: by then I'd felt the chapter end twice, at "every reason to abandon it" and at "to be seen as more than what I do." The exercise was a third ending. It earned its place, but I arrived at it a little tired.

**Free and pushed.** Free: "If not, I have no argument to offer here. Read on, and see whether anything else fits." "Whether that number ever goes to zero is something you can discover for yourself." The early "I'd rather you know that now." Pushed: "Why does it matter so much to me that he's bad—unless some part of me suspects he isn't?" and "Does it surprise you that a stranger could count on that about you?" and the "genuine kindness" question, which can only come out one way.

**Length.** It dragged in three places: the second half of the Encounter, the back half of The Corruption from "Of course, I don't actually tell myself," and in Common knowledge from "It's difficult to describe the horror" through "how evil spreads while staying "off the record."" If I had to cut a fifth I'd cut there, plus "My contempt might fool people watching from afar," plus the paragraph beginning "I desperately want to call this behavior *unconscious*," which the villain-and-machine paragraph after it says better. Said twice: the "call yourself names" / "want to hide" pair; "If I'd looked carefully" after Limbo had already taught me that; "Part of me *knows* it's the same thing" in Whose intention, which Common knowledge then argues for properly; "doubled down" twice.

**Words.** "better nature" sounds like Lincoln on a commemorative plate, but I stopped hearing it after a while. "darkness" made me wince at first; by the end I accepted it as your word. "mash that first button," "chump," "Classic" are fine in voice. "fierce kindness," a little. "The Corruption" as a title is gothic for what's in it.

**Last.** Was it obvious? Much of it was familiar to me as philosophy: Sartre, Fingarette, Strawson, Augustine, Arendt, Browning. None of it was familiar to me as *me*. What became newly consequential: that my contempt for Paul is aimed exactly where I'm afraid of being Paul, and that the blind referee report was a case of no one watching. This week, at Thursday's meeting, I'm going to try not saying "interesting." I'll see what arrives in its place, and how fast. And I'm going to leave the bowls alone.

Would I pass it on? Not to colleagues; they'd stop at "Whose intention" and grade it, which is what I did, and then miss the rest, which is what I almost did. I'd give it to my sister Karin, a nurse in Duluth, who has spent five years in a cold fury at a certain kind of patient and has told me she doesn't recognize herself. She'd do the exercises. She wouldn't argue with the mastermind sentence; she'd go straight to the dishwasher paragraph. I notice I've just explained why my colleagues couldn't read it, in the voice I use about Paul. I'll leave that in.

### Stage 2

Dear Aditya,

I read the second file on Monday morning, in my office before my ten o'clock. I expected a different chapter and got the same one with perhaps a dozen sentences changed. So this letter is about those sentences, read by someone who had already done the exercises. That made me a harder reader. I knew where every change had been made because I knew where I'd been hit.

**Which did more, and which treated me better.** The chapter did more to me. It also treated me better, with one exception, and the exception is the one place the second file is clearly right. If they'd differed only there, I'd still choose the chapter.

**Common knowledge, the argument.** This is the change that matters most, so I'll start there.

The chapter has: "Part of me was counting on him to see me go missing and know what it meant, because he's done it himself." The second file has instead: "He didn't see it by accident. The part of my mind executing this trap counted on him recognizing what I was doing: turning away from what I knew so I could hurt him while feeling righteous, and doing it to make him turn away too, so I could condemn him for it."

Then the question. The chapter: "How could it do that, unless it already knew that he's the same kind of thing as me: someone who must *turn away* from himself to cause harm?" The second file: "How could it do that, unless it already knew that he's the same kind of thing as me: someone with a better nature, who causes harm by *turning away* from it?"

In my first letter I said "kind" slides from thin to thick at the bold sentence, and that the hypocrisy argument then earns the thick sense honestly. The second file puts the thick sense, "someone with a better nature," into the question itself. So the bold sentence follows. But that's because the premise already contains it, and nothing before the premise has shown that recognizing my turning away requires him to have a better nature. A philosopher would call that begging the question, and I think a lay reader would feel it as being hurried. The chapter's version is weaker and more honest. It claims only that he must turn away from himself to cause harm, which my own referee report had already shown me. Then it lets the hypocrisy paragraph do the heavier lifting. That's the right order, and the chapter has it.

The chapter also gives the reason the recognition works: "because he's done it himself." The second file drops it. That clause was the premise I marked as plausible but not shown. Still, at least it was *stated*, so I could see what I was being asked to grant. Without it, the step from recognition to kinship has nothing under it at all.

And "go missing" is the chapter's best image in that section. It's how I'd describe the conference man looking a foot to the left of my face. ### Stage 2

Aditya,

I read the second file the next evening. Short answer first: the chapter did more to me and treated me better. But the second file has three or four things the chapter needs, and one of them repairs the exact step I objected to.

**What each did, and how each treated me.**

The second file cross-examines the reader. I mean that technically. It's leading questions, one after another, each with the answer built in. "Harder, almost certainly." "Of course not." "I mean, *obviously*." "It's hard to do, isn't it?" "Haven't you ever watched an enemy squirm under your "righteous" gaze". If opposing counsel examined my witness this way I'd be on my feet saying "Objection, leading" every thirty seconds. I'm a hostile witness by trade, and by the end of the second section I'd stopped answering and started objecting.

The irony is that it describes the first stranger as a man who leaves no road open, "Every road funnels into the same conclusion.", and then does the same thing to me. Each road I could take had been answered before I took it. I wasn't caught; I was handled.

The chapter does the opposite. It speaks in the first person. It confesses before it asks. It says "Or maybe not." in the first paragraph and means it. Its questions are open: the chapter's cashier check ends "Did either one need a reason before it would go?" and lets me answer. The second file's version is "go out in public, find a stranger, and shoot them a dehumanizing gaze. It's hard to do, isn't it?" That's the same test with my answer already written in.

And the chapter got my 2011 email out of me. The second file wouldn't have. Its opening memory question is nearly the same, but it never comes back to say, as the chapter does, "If you can find even one memory where it's plainly true of you, that's the one to look at." That sentence is what connected my email to Brent. Without it I'd have read the second file as being about Chidi, the first stranger, and enjoyed it far too much.

So the chapter did more, and treated me better. They didn't differ for me.

**What the second file has that the chapter should recover.**

1. The step I refused. In my first letter I objected to the chapter's move from "he can read my signal" to "he must turn away to cause harm." The second file gets there by a better route, in The Why: "He has just shown himself that a person can override their conscience while genuinely believing it's righteous. So how does he know that you aren't doing the same thing? That you're not just like him? **He doesn't.**" That's evidence from his own conduct. He's seen in himself the thing he condemns, so his own case is the proof that the condemned person might be redeemable. I'd accept that from a witness. And the line that follows is the best sentence in either file: "*That's* what he's in limbo about: the fact that *you're* merely in limbo." I read it three times. It's Brent and me exactly. What I can't let myself know is that he's only doing what I did in 2011.

2. The inventory in When You Send It. "Now look at what you knew in that moment. You knew how it would land. You noticed that it landed. You knew they could recognize what you were doing. The chapter makes this argument with its own enemy: "Part of me was counting on him to see me go missing and know what it meant, because he's done it himself." The second file makes me prove it on my own look. That's much stronger. It's the same move as the chapter's "Try it now," which was the best moment in the chapter for me. Evidence from my own file beats evidence from yours.

3. The answer to my flinch about "evil." I wrote that the word was too big for a glance across a dinner table. And: "So a flash of it, even for someone you love, is a flash of **the thing with no bottom.**" And: "As though some part of you knows that the smallest morsel contains the structure of the whole."
