## Hana: the condensed book (m1)

### Letter 1 — after the preface and Chapter 1

Mira,

You asked what it did to me, not what I thought of it, so I'll try to keep to that, though you know I can't read a sentence without hearing its rhythm.

The preface first, quickly. I braced at "evil" and at "the book earns it" (let me decide that, thanks), and I winced at "plant medicines" tucked into a parenthesis as if I wouldn't look. But he confesses before he preaches. "It is not one that I can claim to be fully living myself" and "*Because* I was convinced of that" bought him a page of trust. The metta line at the end I know from the app on my phone, and it was sweet, and a little sticky.

Then Chapter 1, and I have to tell you what happened, because it wasn't what I expected.

He doesn't start by asking you to think of someone you despise. He asks you to remember something you did that you knew was wrong and couldn't admit. I went straight to Rose Villa: the fall I skipped Dad's Sundays, then most of December. You remember. What I told everyone was "He doesn't know who I am anymore." Then he asks whether you could have done it *without* the justification. I couldn't. I needed that sentence every week. And when I finally admitted it, after the funeral, it didn't feel like news. It felt like opening a hand I'd kept closed for a year. He calls that "limbo," knowing and not knowing at once, and the word fit.

Then the two strangers. One condemns you for what you *are*; one is furious only at what you *did*. I had both. Dana at the reception with her paper plate: "Some of us were there every week." I dug in so fast it was almost physical. And Marisol, the hospice nurse, who said "He asked for you on Tuesday. He said your name," and kept looking at me. Harsher than Dana, and I didn't dig in at all. I sat in the car and sobbed. He writes of his own teacher: "He was searching my face for some sign that I knew, and that made me look for it in myself." That's Marisol to the letter.

Here's where it got under my skin. Most of his long example is hot contempt, a man calling someone out, eyes blazing, and I'm not that. You know how I am with Dana. I don't argue anymore. I use the calm voice. I kept translating his hot into my cold, and it kept working, which was the uncomfortable part. "Fierce integrity and contempt can be made to look alike from the outside, so that when I look back later, even I won't be sure which one I did." Then a line I'll have for a long time: the look "can be carried entirely by gaze and tone, which have the benefit of being unquotable." That's my voice on the phone with Dana. I've built an unquotable instrument.

Then he asks if you've ever been on the receiving end of a dehumanizing gaze, whether the person seemed absent, turned away from themselves. Then: "Might that be what I looked like to my enemy?" It wasn't Dana I thought of. It was Kenji. "You go cold." Cold is absent. My son has been describing this, at sixteen, with more precision than I've managed in twelve years of therapy.

And then: "**Love and contempt are addressed to the same place.** One says *I know you're in there.* The other says *there's nobody in there*, and they both say it to the somebody who is." And two lines later: "A child can't refuse it; he believes it, and carries it for years." I put it down and sat on the edge of the bed for a while.

When the cold version finally arrives, it's one paragraph, two hundred lines in: "a quiet filing-away, a politeness with nothing behind it ... It's the same verdict, delivered by withdrawal, and it's meant to be felt: by them, or by whoever's watching." Whoever's watching. Kenji at the kitchen table while I'm on speaker with his aunt. I'd been waiting for that paragraph the whole chapter, and nearly decided the book wasn't for people like me before it came. It should come sooner, and there should be more of it.

The exercise that found something I didn't know: the two buttons, vengeance for your enemy or a pill that changes their heart. I don't want vengeance, I thought. But the pill made me feel *cheated*. If Dana woke up kind I'd lose being right about her. I'd want her to see herself for a few minutes first. That's punishment, and I hadn't known I wanted it.

And one that found something I didn't want to know: he asks for a time you felt the sigh coming, the "of course you did," and didn't send it. I had to search. I found one. Kenji dented the Subaru in March, and that one time I said "Are you okay?" first, and he told me everything, including the part about his phone. One time. That's what I found.

You asked about the first paragraph. "Contempt is the claim that you're a worse kind of thing than me. But it only works because you're the same kind of thing, and both of us know it. I want to show you why." It's the answer before I'd looked. "Worse kind of thing" is a phrase I'll keep, but "both of us know it" is exactly what I'd rather catch myself knowing. He half-rescues it with the next line (hold these like puzzle pieces, drop them if they don't fit), and then he does what he promised: he asks before he tells, mostly. The asking is what got me. The opening paragraph nearly didn't.

How it ended: like an essay, not a first chapter. "And the best thing I can do, for them and through them for the world, is remember it." It ties the bow. It answers the question he planted with the strangers ("what is that thing whose side you are both on?") with "an orientation," shows beautifully how naming it turns it into a weapon, and then gives a benediction. I didn't close it wanting Chapter 2 because he'd made me need it. I wanted Chapter 2 because I need to know *how*, when the cold comes on in half a second. That question was mine, not his.

Where I felt handled or told: "and the book earns it" in the preface. "You know how it closes around you." "The way you found your wrong in the opening." "Invite you to remember the part of you that already knows it." None of these is fatal. He's careful, and he often goes first ("I'll be honest: there is in me"), which helps. But you can feel him wanting to be sure I got it.

Where I was lost or slowed: the part that knows goes by five names in one chapter: "the part that knew," "inner knowing" (I winced), "better nature," "innocent part," and finally "what you call 'I' when nothing is being hidden." The last one is lovely and nearly fixes the rest. Sentences like "He knows it's true, and I *know* he knows it, and he knows that too" and "The signal is the pretense that there is no signal" I had to read twice. There's a reference to an Appendix 1 I don't have. And "the darkness" is used from the preface on with a definite article, then quietly becomes something that "tracks, it aims, it fights to stay hidden," and I never got told when it became a character. "An absence that behaves like a presence" is Sister Margaret's catechism from seventh grade, and I didn't mind meeting it again, because now it was about my calm voice: "everything it uses—my attention, my reasons, my eyes—is borrowed from me."

Where I pushed back hard: the footnote about Lindsay Clancy. He's showing how people split a terrible case into monster or illness, "illness, with nothing of her in it." I watched dementia take Dad. At the end, some of what he did *was* illness with nothing of him in it. He hedges, but the frame quietly lines her up with being "turned away," and I won't have that for psychosis. And the woodchipper quote, as a mother, was a slap. And "othering has reached its zenith, and feels maximally righteous" — that sentence was written by a committee. The man who wrote "*Shit.* He's onto me" didn't write it.

The sound of it overall: often a real person, sometimes a template. There's a rhythm I kept catching ("It wasn't a rule, or a side. It was something..." / "What I've been hiding isn't that I *could*... It's that I'm *already doing it*"), the tidy not-this-but-that. When it's earned, it lands. He reaches for it too often.

But I'm going to tell Kenji something this week, and I haven't worked out what. I'll read on.

Hana


### Letter 2 — after the rest of the book

Mira,

I finished it last night. I'm not going to revise what I wrote you after Chapter 1; I'll just keep going.

What happened in me, first. The book kept finding the same place in me from different sides, and by the end I couldn't pretend it was a coincidence. In Chapter 2 he says "love for one person at the expense of another, love that needs someone else to be worthless, isn't love." I read it and thought of Mom and Dana. In Chapter 3 he says we turn away because we suspect "care might *not* be what you are," so "you can only be good *relative* to someone worse." That's when it came clear. If Dana is decent, then I'm the daughter who didn't show up at Rose Villa. That's what the calm voice is for. I've known it for about two years without ever letting myself say it. That's his "limbo," and I was in it.

Chapter 4 is the one I'd have written if I were braver. Most contempt, he says, isn't for bad people but for the slow, the stupid, the crazy, and there "I tell myself I'm only being accurate." Then a check: is there contempt for a toddler who can't add, or a grandparent losing words? No, he says, not until "I've decided they *should* know better." I thought of Dad. There was no contempt when he couldn't find "spoon." It came the winter he wouldn't eat, the moment I decided he was doing it on purpose to punish me. I'd never seen that the cold switched on exactly when I decided he was choosing it. And "*I'm really worried about Lisa*." I've said "I'm just worried about Mom" to mean exactly that.

What I'll carry out of the room is from Chapter 5. He takes a clean sentence and adds the extra: "*That was out of line, and it stops now*, followed by the half-second pause to watch it land." "*You lied to me.* With the extra: *Of course you lied to me.*" Then: "Each time the difference is one word or one pause, and each time you know, on the way out, which one you sent." That's my whole method, Mira. "Of course you forgot." The pause after "That's fine, Dana." I'm a cold contemner, and the cold kind *is* nothing but extras: no word you could quote, all pause and tone. I'll carry that, and "Love and contempt are addressed to the same place," and one more, from the same chapter: "often the repair is no more than letting them see that you see them now."

Where it peaked: Chapter 1's middle (the unquotable look, "a child can't refuse it"), all of Chapter 4, and Chapter 5's "extra" passage with its daily check: "if the harm simply stopped tomorrow, with nobody punished and nobody exposed, would you feel relief, or robbed?" (Robbed. A little. Enough.) One sentence in "The Picture I Use" took me straight to Dad's care home, and it's supposed to be the part with no check: "The one who decides doesn't look, the one who looks doesn't decide, and the harm happens in the gap between them." I can check that one. I lived in that gap for a year.

Where it sagged: Chapter 5's long stretch on ideology and rules, and the venture-versus-grandmother comparison, which is rigged. He builds the venture to treat everyone as "inputs" and then asks which leaves more care. I skimmed the second half of the Picture, the Devil and the coordination. And the Epilogue lost me at "Ideas that live in our collective shadow manifest 'externally,' taking shape in our physical reality." "Manifest" is the language of vision boards.

Where I was lost. The dark thing has too many names: darkness, evil, the turn, the parasite, a habit, an infection, the shadow, the hum, the Devil, Mara, wetiko, and once, "possessed." The good thing has almost as many: the part that knows, inner knowing, better nature, innocent part, awareness, the light, the Light, Home. He explains each as he goes, but by the end I was keeping a list. Words I didn't have: Mahayana, Dzogchen, Mahamudra, marigpa, lojong, tonglen, Samantabhadra, "mind-stream," and "conventionally" real. Sentences I read three times: "the part of the covering this book is about was one thing." "The want the recognition would have vetoed was innocent." "Whatever is looking out of your eyes right now ... is the same kind of thing that looked out of the stranger's ... and I mean it literally. Not that he is like me." That last one wasn't explained until the Epilogue's not-one, not-two note, and it should have been in Chapter 2. Twice he sent me to "a teacher," and I don't have one.

Where I was handled or told. "Contempt is evil. That's not a value judgment." I stopped dead there. Calling something evil is a value judgment, and two flat sentences before the definition felt like a verdict on my phone voice. "This is what the buttons in Chapter 1 were measuring" tells me after the fact what my experience was. "I think anyone who watches their own contempt closely for long enough will find it too." The far-end exercise tells me its flavor ("*malevolent*") before asking me to look. When I did it honestly, what I found wasn't malevolent. It was a flat white room, a politeness with nothing behind it, extended forever. A cold person may find emptiness down there, not malice, and the book doesn't leave room for that. Then the small reflexes: "That isn't said to frighten you," "That isn't a nice idea from a book," "It's not just a slogan." Each answers something I hadn't said. In fairness, he often goes first, and he marks his own reports, and that kept me from feeling cornered most of the time.

Sentences that sounded like a machine or a committee: "where 'othering' has reached its zenith, and feels maximally righteous." "This scales." "if you only take into account first-order effects." "Being turned toward, seeing yourself in others, love, goodness: all one." "So we help produce the 'bad people' whose actions justify our contempt; all of us build that same division into institutions; and those institutions provide new objects against which our contempt can feel righteous." "will be necessary for our species to make progress toward the kind of world most of us long for." "It is anti-life." And a rhythm, more than any one sentence: "It wasn't a rule, or a side. It was something..." "It isn't the darkness subjugated. It's the darkness looked at." Every chapter ends on a rounded not-this-but-that. And the last image of the book is "hold my feet to the fire." That's a torture idiom, closing a book about refusing punishment. It's the first stranger's idiom. I'd circle it in red.

From what I can check to what I can only take from him. He marks it, mostly, and well. "The Picture I Use" opens: "Up to here, almost everything came with a check you could run. What follows doesn't." The Epilogue says "This you can only hear," and calls the mushrooms mushrooms. I respected that. But two places cross the line unmarked. Chapter 2's "When I look most deeply into what I am, I find life" is at least in the first person. Chapter 5's ending is flat assertion: "There is a battle between good and evil, between light and darkness, and it is the one the myths were about," and then "only the light, turned away." Did I go along? With the Picture's institutions, yes. With "my contempt for a neighbor makes an atrocity on another continent a little more likely," no. That's a sermon's arithmetic. The Kenji version I believe: it passes to whoever's watching. In the Epilogue I took the myth of the first forgetting as a story, and it moved me. It's the Dana mechanism at the size of the cosmos, and his reading of original sin, the fear that we lost our goodness, I may write about. I got off at "that part was essentially God, and God is reality, so knowledge of my wrongdoing always takes physical shape in my 'external' world." He guards it ("nothing that happens to anyone, an illness, a loss ... is anyone else's to read as a sign of what they did"). After watching Dad go, I'd have thrown the book across the room without that parenthesis. With it, I just put it down for a minute.

I still don't forgive the Lindsay Clancy footnote in Chapter 1. Some of what dementia did to Dad really was illness with nothing of him in it. I want the book to grant that the machine is sometimes real.

Would I have finished it if I'd bought it? Yes, through Chapter 5, gladly. I'd have read the Picture and the Epilogue because by then I trusted him. He keeps confessing ("I still haven't cleaned up my act, so make of that what you will"), and the one line about "the British who held down my people" made him suddenly a person with a history. I wanted that two hundred pages sooner. But I'd have skimmed.

Which chapter, and to whom. Chapter 4 to Celeste, who sat on her porch with me in August while we took Dana apart over a bottle of white. She'd recognize "a warmth between them that had nothing to do with the person they were discussing," and I'd rather we recognized it together than separately. Chapter 3's "note for people who were hurt" to Teresa, because "Staying turned toward someone never means staying near them" is the sentence that would let her read on. And I'll tell you the dark joke I caught in myself. My first impulse was to hand Chapter 1 to Dana. I know exactly why I mustn't: it would be the calm voice in book form.

What I'll do this week. I'm going to talk to Kenji. No speech, no "you are what you did" pointed at myself, which the book warns about and which would just make it about me. I'll tell him he was right about the cold and that I heard him. Then I'll try to let him see that I see him now. When Dana calls on Thursday I'm going to listen for the breath before the calm voice. That breath, it turns out, is the choice I've been calling self-control. And I'll ask myself relief or robbed. I'm going to stop saying "of course" to my son. And I'm going to write to Marisol, who I now think of, in his word, as a keeper.

Was any of it obvious? Yes, pieces. Jung's shadow is under the whole "I needed my enemy to carry my darkness" layer. My second therapist used to call it "the critic." "Evil is the absence of good" I had from Sister Margaret in seventh grade. "Darkness cannot drive out darkness" is on mugs. "Pray for those who persecute you" and "there but for the grace of God" I could recite. What changed is that they started to matter. The absence is "borrowed from me," my attention, my reasons, my voice, and that made the catechism about my phone calls. "Every ideology began as care, written down so it could be carried" made me less angry at the Church than I've been in thirty years. And the sigh. I always knew the sigh was unkind. I didn't know it was the whole thing.

He asks to be told when he drifts. I'd tell him the drift shows when he stops asking me about my week and starts explaining the universe. And I'd tell him "Love and contempt are addressed to the same place" is worth the price of the book by itself.

Hana

## Hana: the full book (m2)

### Letter 1

Dear Ruth,

You asked what it did to me. I'll tell you, but first the preface, briefly: a man who took plant medicine and saw that his certainty of being one of the good guys was part of the problem. I braced. Portland has a lot of men who had a vision. What disarmed me was one italicized word, "*Because* I was convinced of that," and the line at the end, "Nothing here is a verdict on you." There's one sentence in it that performs ("a uniquely insidious expression of evil: how the darkness spreads between us while masquerading as righteousness"), and I'd cut it, but I kept going.

Then Chapter 1 did something to me I wasn't ready for, and I'm still a little shaky.

He doesn't start where I expected, with someone I despise. He starts with something I did, something I knew was wrong at the time. I didn't have to search. Chapter nine of my book, where I wrote Daniel as the brother who didn't show up and left out that he was paying two-thirds of Dad's care. I knew while I typed it. When he asks, "Now imagine doing the same thing without any justifications. Could you have?", the honest answer is no. Without "it's my truth" I couldn't have written that chapter. That's the first thing that landed.

Then the two strangers, one who condemns you and one who's fierce but doesn't. He tells a story of a teacher who caught him stealing: "He was searching my face for some sign that I knew, and that made me look for it in myself." That's my father, the day he found me reading Kate's diary. He said "Hana," and waited. I haven't thought about that look in years and I had to stop reading.

The long middle section is him narrating his own contempt from inside his head, and it's the best writing in the chapter because he's showing me instead of telling me. His contempt is hot, eyes and calling-out. Mine, as you know, is cold. I was translating the whole time. But the shape fit. "And I'll have produced the result I needed: proof that my enemy is beyond help, because look how he reacted." I have said "You saw how she got" about Denise, in the car, more than once. And then: "It can be carried entirely by gaze and tone, which have the benefit of being unquotable." That is my method, Ruth. That's the whole thing. Nobody can quote me. Tyler can't quote me. He just knows.

And then this, which is the sentence I'm carrying out of the room:

"**Love and contempt are addressed to the same place.** One says *I know you're in there.* The other says *there's nobody in there*, and they both say it to the somebody who is."

I said "I know you're in there" to Dad every Sunday for two years, when he didn't know my name. I had never once thought that my cold voice goes to the same place. The next line is "A child can't refuse it; he believes it, and carries it for years." You know what Tyler said to me last month. I got up and walked around the kitchen.

There's a short section on the cold kind ("a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them... meant to be felt: by them, or by whoever's watching"), and "whoever's watching" turned out to be my son in the passenger seat while I'm on speaker with Denise. The exercise after it, recalling a time I felt "of course you did" coming and didn't send it, was the gentlest thing in the chapter and it got the furthest. I remembered Tyler and the mailbox, the one time I said "Are you okay?" instead, and how he told me the truth about his phone because of it.

On your questions. The first paragraph: "Contempt is the claim that you're a worse kind of thing than me. But it only works because you're the same kind of thing, and both of us know it. I want to show you why." It does give the answer before I've looked. But it's a paradox I didn't understand yet, so it pulled me rather than closing the door. I'd cut "I want to show you why." The paragraph is stronger ending on "both of us know it."

How it ended: it felt finished, not open. It ends with him naming what both strangers serve as "an orientation," maybe "turned toward all of life," and closing with "the best thing I can do, for them and through them for the world, is remember it." That's a sermon's last line. I didn't feel pulled into Chapter 2 by a question. I'll go on because of what happened in the middle, not because the ending asked me to. And "orientation" is a cold word for what my father had in his face. I'd have called it love. He shows, nicely, how naming it "serving life" goes bad ("*So hating him is good*"), which half-redeemed the yoga-studio phrase, but only half.

Where I was handled or lost:

- He asks "Would you have dug in?" and two lines later answers for me: "it still wouldn't have triggered the digging-in response." Also, "the digging-in response" is pamphlet language.
- The reverse-remorse question lists every answer I might give ("regret that it cost me, anger at being taken advantage of, guilt at breaking a rule... feeling foolish") before I give one. "If yours held up, I'd like to hear it" rescues it, barely.
- The two-button exercise offers vengeance or "a pill that instantly changes their heart." I watched dementia change my father's heart over six years. A pill that rewrites someone isn't the obvious good to me. It's the thing I'm most afraid of. The exercise is rigged for anyone who's lived through that.
- Too many parts. In a few pages I met "the part that knew," "the part that can prove," "my better nature," "that need," "something tracked his reactions," "that part of me craves," and "his innocent part." I assumed some are the same. I'm not sure which. And then "Watch how it accomplishes its goal," when I couldn't tell what "it" was or what goal.
- "Darkness" is everywhere ("injecting my own darkness," "embrace their darkness," "the darkness I see in him lives in me") and never said plainly. I went along, but I kept a tab open.
- "(Appendix 1 lays out the formal structure.)" lands right after "The look is the deed," the best short sentence in the book, and drops me from a confession into a seminar.
- His habit: "It wasn't a rule, or a side. It was something..." and "Not that we agree... It's that..." I started hearing the not-this-but-that rhythm about the fourth time. And the "*Because* I don't" fragment appears in the preface and again in the chapter.
- "Malevolent isn't too strong a word... in a certain sense, it was. And yet, in another sense, it was utterly innocent." That's a hedge, and it's the one place the chapter sounded like it was written by a committee.

But Ruth: I'm a person who can tell when a sentence was written by someone who wasn't listening, and most of this chapter was written by someone who was. He goes first. He says "I'll be honest: there is in me" before he asks if there's anything in me. That's why I let him in.

I'm going to call Tyler after this, and I don't know what I'll say.

Hana

### Letter 2

Ruth,

I finished it. I didn't call Tyler that night after all. I texted him "Proud of you for the mailbox thing, by the way," which made no sense to him three weeks later. He sent back a question mark and then a thumbs-up. It's a start, and it's not what I'll actually do. I'll tell you that at the end.

What I'll carry out of the room is mostly from Chapter 1, but three lines from later joined it.

From Chapter 5: "You turn away from someone because you suspect, below words, that care might *not* be what you are." And then: "Under the suspicion is fear: fear of what you might be, which in public looks like pride." I sat with that a long time. My suspicion isn't abstract. It's that I'm Mom, cold at the bottom. That's what Tyler named, and that's why it went in so deep. My calm is pride. Under it is that fear. The next line, "under the contempt there is someone afraid, and you know that state from the inside," gave me Denise back for a second. Of course she's afraid. Her mother-in-law is disappearing, and Denise is the one who lives twenty minutes away.

From Chapter 7: "if the harm simply stopped tomorrow, with nobody punished and nobody exposed, would you feel relief, or robbed?" And a related test: "something in you would be disappointed if they got better." I tried it. If Denise walked into the next care conference and said "You were right about the trazodone," part of me would lose something I've been leaning on for two years. That's the most usable thing in the book. He says, "That question is most of the practice," and I believe him.

And this, also from Chapter 7: "How much innocence you can see in others measures your own freedom more than their goodness." He asks you to remember someone you despised and came to see differently without their changing. For me that's my mother. I despised her coldness from fourteen to thirty. Then one day her voice came out of my mouth at a two-year-old. She hadn't changed. I had.

Where it peaked: Chapter 1, the long monologue and the lines after it. "The look is the deed." "It can be carried entirely by gaze and tone, which have the benefit of being unquotable." "Love and contempt are addressed to the same place." Nothing later hit that hard. Chapters 2 and 3 have strong moments: the face exercise ("until it stops being something you're looking at and becomes a place somebody is looking out of"), the grudge that "has to be fed," "I'm only being accurate," and "the half-second pause to watch it land." That last one is my weapon exactly. It isn't words; it's the pause after a perfectly reasonable sentence. Chapter 4 has the best sentence about institutions I've read: "The one who decides doesn't look, the one who looks doesn't decide, and the harm happens in the gap between them." That's Mom's facility. I've been furious at that gap for a year without a sentence for it.

Where it sagged: Chapter 6 and parts of 7 started to circle. The same pieces kept coming back in new arrangements: the verdict, the license, the parasite, the push. Chapter 7 re-asks Chapter 1's question about what the second stranger served, then gives the light its third definition ("That's what I've been calling the light"). It warns against defining goodness, which Chapter 1's Orientation section already did. It says "Every act of integrity... leaks out into the world," and the Epilogue says it again. The Epilogue redoes the conspiracy from Chapter 4 and the one Devil from the Picture. I skimmed the paragraph about time being "hallucinated," and the one about ideas that "manifest 'externally.'"

Where I was lost:
- The names. In Chapter 1 alone: "the part that knew," "the part that can prove," "my better nature," "that need," "something," "his innocent part." Then "the orientation," "the light," "the guide," "the part of him that could leave it," "the parts underneath." - "This scales."
- "feeling forsaken at a level we rarely cognize."
- "will be necessary for our species to make progress toward the kind of world most of us long for."

Our parasites aren't; they're collaborating." "It doesn't feel like fighting. It feels like love." Each one alone is fine, and a few are the best lines in the book. By Chapter 4 I could hear them coming.

Where it moves from what I could check to what I had to take from him: he says so every time, and I respect that more than anything else about the book. In Chapter 5: "If it doesn't, take what follows as my report." In the Picture: "What follows doesn't [have a check]." In the Epilogue: "This you can only hear." I went along as far as the fear under contempt, and as far as the face exercise reaching animals and getting fainter. I didn't go along with "life wants to serve life," or the Devil, or reality nudged by my hatred of the rainforest. I listened to those the way you listen to a friend's dream. Two places blurred the line he'd drawn. In Chapter 3, "my contempt for a neighbor makes an atrocity on another continent a little more likely, by no line I could ever trace" arrives in the checkable part of the book. And the Epilogue says "I trusted it the way you trusted the knowledge of always having known you'd done wrong," which lends the credit of my checkable memory to his uncheckable vision.

One place I got angry. In the Epilogue, about the few who refused to forsake him: "it wasn't a personal decision on their part. It was the Light acting through them." No. My father's look was his. It cost him. He told me years later that his own sister had read his diary as a boy, and he'd decided then what he'd do if it ever happened again. Don't take that from him and give it to the Light. The book spent Chapter 1 honoring the second stranger as a person, and then that sentence takes it back.

The question the book raised for me and didn't answer is in Chapter 6: "It is possible to leave, cut contact, and never see them again with no verdict in it at all." Chapter 1 told me cold contempt is "the same verdict, delivered by withdrawal." So how do I tell, from inside, which kind of distance I'm keeping from Denise? Mine always feels like the clean kind. That's what's wrong with it. A reader like me needs that page, and it isn't there. The book's examples run hot all the way through ("The heat changes"; "FAFO"; the locker room; eyes and calling-out). I was translating from Chapter 1 to the end. The cold kind gets one paragraph, and it was the paragraph that mattered most to me.

Would I have finished it if I'd bought it? Yes, because of Chapter 1, though I'd have slowed down in 6 and read the Epilogue standing up in the kitchen with half my attention. Which chapter would I hand on? Chapter 1, to Kate. She and I are the two people tearing into someone who isn't there ("a warmth between them that had nothing to do with the person they were discussing"), and she should read it before our next phone call, not after. I'd give the Tuesday class the withheld-sigh exercise from "The Other Look" without the book around it. It was the gentlest thing in the book and it went the furthest.

This week: the next time I feel the pause coming, the half second after "That's an interesting approach, Denise," I'm going to not take it. That's all. One pause, not taken. And with Tyler I'm not going to make a speech. Chapter 7 says "often the repair is no more than letting them see that you see them now." I'm going to try that, at the kitchen counter, the next time the look lands and I'd normally pretend I hadn't seen it.

Was any of it obvious? Some, yes. Shame makes you hide: twelve years of therapy taught me that, and Chapter 1 says it ("Nobody ever looked honestly at something in themselves while being told they were worthless for having it"). Contempt is contagious: anyone with a family knows it. What became newly consequential was the unquotability. I always knew my cold voice was deniable, and I was proud of it. I'd never once thought that deniability is the thing that makes it cruel ("Plain malice would frighten you. This maddens, because it won't admit what it is"), or that it's aimed at the same place I aimed "I know you're in there" every Sunday for two years. That was knowledge I had. Now it matters.

It's not a finished book. He says so himself. But most of it was written by someone who was listening.

Hana

I'd give this to people. I'd give them Chapters 1, 4 and 5 and tell them to take the rest as the author's own. He'd hate that and he'd understand it.

Daniel

That's an opening statement, and I've heard ten thousand of them; the ones that promise most usually deliver least. What kept me was two lines: "It is not one that I can claim to be fully living myself," and "Nothing here is a verdict on you." I know what a verdict is. I decided to see if he meant it.

The first paragraph of Chapter 1 gives you the conclusion: "Contempt is the claim that you're a worse kind of thing than me. But it only works because you're the same kind of thing, and both of us know it." In any other book that would have had me folding my arms. Here it worked, I think because the next line admits he's making claims before he's earned them and tells me to drop them if they don't fit. And "it only works because" is a paradox, not a verdict. I wanted to know what "works" meant. I'm still not sure he ever says precisely, but by the end I could guess.

Then he didn't ask about my enemies. He asked about a wrong I'd done and knew. And I was back in 2003 with Marcus. You remember Marcus, the robbery with the bad cross-racial ID, the plea I sold him the week before the Delgado trial. I told him it was the best he'd get. I told myself the caseload was criminal and the enhancement risk was real. All true, and none of it why. The book asked whether, when I finally owned it, it felt like new information. No. Not for a second. It felt like something I'd been holding with the lights off. He calls that "limbo," and it's willful blindness, which the law has had a jury instruction for since before he was born. I liked that he got there on his own.

"Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" That's a question I'd put to a jury. I had three reasons and the other side needed none.

The thing I didn't have before this chapter came a few pages later. He says contempt teaches the other person to demote his own conscience, "because what good is it in a world as rotten as this?" And I realized that the rot I used to excuse the Marcus plea, the stacked enhancements, the forced pleas, the caseloads, was built in large part by a DA's contempt for my clients. His contempt made the world I used to excuse myself. I never put those two things in one sentence before. I'm sixty-six, Danny. That doesn't happen to me much.

Other things that went in and stayed:

"The look is the deed. ... It can be carried entirely by gaze and tone, which have the benefit of being unquotable." Thirty-five years of cold transcripts. Brandt's smile at that boy's mother isn't in the record. I've said for decades the record lies by omission, and this is why.

"**Love and contempt are addressed to the same place.** One says *I know you're in there.* The other says *there's nobody in there*, and they both say it to the somebody who is." That's Judge Hollis and Judge Brandt in one sentence. You remember Hollis: "Mr. Grant, your client needed a lawyer this morning." I was never unprepared in her courtroom again.

"Deny it, and you're the kind of person who does. Get angry, and that's proof too. Stay calm, and you don't even care. Apologize, and it's too convenient." That's every parole hearing I ever sat through.

"When I'm sure someone was fully aware of what they were doing, I've made them a villain. When I'm sure they had no idea, I've made them a machine." That one went in like a needle, because my job for thirty-five years was so often to make a machine. The frontal lobe, the lead paint, the father. I'd do it again; it kept men alive. But what I saw across the table, most days, was what he says: "they knew, but didn't let themselves feel the weight of what they knew." The law has no word for that. It lives between murder one and insanity.

And then the part that has nothing to do with the law. You know what Helen said to me last year, that I'm contemptuous of contempt, and that she's stopped telling me when she's angry. He has a passage on the cold kind, "a politeness with nothing behind it," "meant to be felt: by them, or by whoever's watching," and then the sigh: "The sigh that tells them what I really think of them, dressed up to make me seem like the reasonable one. The look on their face that tells me it landed, which I pretend not to have seen." I got up and walked around the kitchen. When she's angry I go calm, and the calm is the verdict. I've called it de-escalating. Earlier he has the good stranger be angry, "Not more gently. Just that his anger was directed at what you *did*," and I had to put the book down, because if Hollis was angry and right, then whatever I've been looking down on in Helen all these years isn't her anger. It's something I added to it.

So, yes. It found me. More than once.

Where it lost me, or handled me:

He tells me what I'll find more than he needs to. After the second stranger he asks "Would you have dug in?" and then answers in the next line: "it still wouldn't have triggered the digging-in response." Late in the chapter: "All I can do is invite you to remember the part of you that already knows it." By then I believed him about me, so it rankled less, but on page one it would have closed the book. The counterexample boxes, too: when he asks whether anyone feels remorse for decency, the list of what my case "turned out to be" is already printed before I bring it. To his credit he says "If yours held up, I'd like to hear it."

His reasoning about responsibility is where a lawyer's ear catches. In one place the contempt is "done in limbo," knowing and not knowing; two pages on, he was "knowingly smothering his innocent part." Those are different mental states and the difference is my whole profession. Then "*Malevolent* isn't too strong a word ... in a certain sense, it was. And yet, in another sense, it was utterly innocent," and "Innocent at the root, and fully responsible." I think he's onto something true. I've half believed it about my clients for decades. But name the senses. "Innocent" in a courtroom means you didn't do it. If he means "no bad core," he should say so, or every prosecutor who reads this will call it word games, and they'll have a point.

And the end of the chapter does something sloppy. He's spent thirty pages making me check limbo in myself. Then he asserts that the first stranger's allies "would have recognized, in limbo, the badness woven into it," and uses that as the premise for "something we serve that transcends sides." I can't check what strangers knew in limbo. The premise is the conclusion.

"Darkness" worries me. It starts as a metaphor for cruelty, becomes something you can hand to another man to carry, and by the end "It tracks, it aims, it fights to stay hidden," two paragraphs after he says it's "something I *do*, then I can stop." An act I can stop, or an agent with aims? I'll see whether a devil walks in. If spirituality shows up to win the argument, I'll tell you.

Some sentences I had to read three times: "What I've been hiding isn't that I *could* do what the 'bad people' do. It's that I'm *already doing it*, by using them as a place to hide the fact that I am." And there's a parenthetical "(Appendix 1 lays out the formal structure.)" that sent me looking for an appendix I don't have.

How it ended: like a sermon. "And the best thing I can do, for them and through them for the world, is remember it." I grew up on that cadence. It felt finished, not open. I want Chapter 2, but because of what this one did to me, not because it handed me a question. If the next chapter is more of the same at lower voltage, I'll feel it.

One last thing. I did the two-buttons exercise, vengeance for your worst enemy or a pill that changes his heart. My first answer was the pill, and it came too fast. Twenty years on the cushion teaches you the right answer before you've asked the question. The honest answer is Brandt, humiliated in front of every family he sentenced. And I watched Kessler's concession speech twice in 2018. The harm stopped the first time. The second was for me.

I'll write again when I'm done.

Walter

### Letter 2

Danny,

I finished it. I said in the first letter I'd tell you if spirituality showed up to win the argument. It did, twice, in the back of the book, and I'll tell you where. But I'll start with what happened, because that matters more.

What I'll carry out of the room. Three things, and the third is the one that cost me something.

First, from Chapter 3: "Nobody chooses evil under that name, and everyone is fully responsible, because responsibility never lived in a lucid instant of choosing evil. It lives in the choice to keep looking while you still can, in how fast you catch the blindness, in what you repair when sight returns, and in what you've done to give the blindness less room next time." You and I spent our working lives inside a Penal Code that puts guilt in the lucid instant: premeditation, intent, malice aforethought. Everybody who's sat across from a client knows that's mostly not where it lives. He puts it in vigilance and repair. That's closer to how I've judged myself about Marcus than anything in the Code. Earlier in that chapter he locates the moment: "While you can still feel both pulls ... you're still looking, and you can keep looking or stop." I found it. The hallway at Santa Rita, Marcus's face through the glass, and me looking down at the file. The choice came labeled "best deal he'll get." I'd never found the moment before. Twenty years.

Second, from Chapter 4: "I've found them guilty of being what they are." That's a status offense. *Robinson v. California*. The Supreme Court threw out status crimes in 1962 and I have been running a private court that tries nothing else: the young woman at our son's table who's twenty-five and should know better, the "slow" clerk, the colleague who loved an audience. The check before it is clean: no contempt for a toddler who can't add; it shows up only once I've decided they *should* know better.

Third, from Chapter 5, and this is the one. "There's a trap in this too, and it's the one I fall into most: contempt for the people who still have contempt." That is Helen's sentence about me, almost to the word. Then: "Equanimity can do it too, when it's a way of filing someone under *deluded* and feeling nothing more. So can practice itself: the sigh at my own mind on the cushion." Twenty years of sitting, and I've used it to file the angry under "caught in it." The calm is the verdict. In Chapter 3 he says goodness that feels like it has to be earned can only be earned "*relative* to someone worse," and you can't afford to let anyone else get there. My father was a deacon with a temper that filled the house. I decided at twelve I'd never be him. My calm is my proof. If Helen gets to be angry and still be good, my calm stops proving anything. I sat on the back step for a while after that one.

And in the same chapter the line that tells me what to do about it: "Here is how to tell the hatred of the harm from the verdict in yourself ... You want them to *know* what they are, and something in you would be disappointed if they got better." Brandt. If he retired tomorrow and spent his last years teaching sentencing reform, I would be a little disappointed. I didn't enjoy finding that.

Where it peaked. Chapter 1, the section on what love and contempt are aimed at ("**Love and contempt are addressed to the same place.** One says *I know you're in there.* The other says *there's nobody in there*, and they both say it to the somebody who is."). Chapter 3, the turn and responsibility. Chapter 4, the whole short thing. Chapter 5, the second half, where he gets practical: "*You lied to me.* With the extra: *Of course you lied to me.* Each time the difference is one word or one pause, and each time you know, on the way out, which one you sent." I taught young lawyers that pause on cross. I've also used it at my own table for forty years.

And one paragraph in the back, which he says I can't check, and I can: "The one who decides doesn't look, the one who looks doesn't decide, and the harm happens in the gap between them, where no one has to know. ... So does a prison where the cruelty is routine, run by guards who were decent people the day they started." That's the system we worked in. The judge never visits the prison; the deputy decides nothing. Shortest true account of an institution I've read.

Where it sagged. Chapter 2 is short and climbs too fast, from a stranger's face to "a fierce care for everything" to "Contempt is evil. That's not a value judgment." I felt argued at there, after a first chapter that mostly showed me. Then "The Picture I Use," the chapter after Chapter 5, and the Epilogue. I read every line because you asked me to. If I'd bought it I'd have skimmed the paragraphs in the Epilogue that begin "Ideas that live in our collective shadow manifest 'externally,' taking shape in our physical reality." I can't follow that and I can't check it.

Where I was lost, or where the logic jumped.

"Contempt is evil. That's not a value judgment." Evil is the most value-laden word in English. The next paragraph defines it narrowly, as "the turn," which makes the claim nearly true by definition. Then it's a stipulation, fine, but the sentence keeps the full charge of the ordinary word while saying it isn't judging. I'd object to that in court. And I should say that I checked his definition against the cases I'd call evil, as he invited, and didn't find an exception. So the claim might be right. The sentence is still wrong.

In Chapter 2: "If awareness were neutral between care and harm, why would harming someone require turning away from it?" What I'd checked was that harming someone requires turning away from my awareness *of her*. He concludes that care belongs to "awareness itself." That's a different claim, slipped across in one breath.

In "The Picture": "the dark in one is the same as the dark in the other. So every darkness wants the same world ... Together they act like a single thing with a single, monstrous aim." Two holes aren't one hole. That's the step that turns a groove in attention into a Devil, and it's the sloppiest inference in the book.

In Chapter 1, the mental state shifts under me. First the contempt is "intentional, but done in limbo." A page later he was "knowingly smothering his innocent part." Then "in a certain sense, it was [malevolent]. And yet, in another sense, it was utterly innocent." Name the senses. "Innocent" in our world means he didn't do it. I think he means "no bad core," and I think he's right, but a prosecutor will eat that sentence.

And the darkness drifts between an act and an agent. "if it's something I *do*, then I can stop," and two paragraphs later "It tracks, it aims, it fights to stay hidden." He does handle it later, honestly ("it doesn't need a tenant"; and he grants it a goal only "in the one sense I've earned," which is Dennett's intentional stance whether he knows it or not). But in Chapter 1 the drift came before the caveat. Along the way it collects names: parasite, groove, infection, shadow, "possessed," Mara, the Devil, wetiko.

Smaller: "(Appendix 1 lays out the formal structure.)" sent me looking for an appendix I don't have. "the part of the covering this book is about was one thing" at the end of Chapter 5 I still don't understand. And the vocabulary I happen to have, from twenty years on the cushion, most readers won't: marigpa, self-cherishing, tonglen, lojong, mind-stream. "Self-cherishing" is Tibetan mind-training, not "the Mahayana's terms," as if a dozen traditions had one glossary.

Where I was handled or told what I'd find. Less than I feared, more than he needs. "it still wouldn't have triggered the digging-in response," answering his own question for me. "But the clean moments ... were the ones that felt most like yourself, and the moment with the verdict in it was the one you'd rather not own." Not for me: my cold verdict is the part of me I've been praised for since law school. "You turn away from someone because you suspect, below words, that care might *not* be what you are," as fact, in the second person. It turned out true of me, which is the problem with telling: I'd rather have found it. "The difference isn't hard to see," two paragraphs before "I've almost always believed I was doing the clean one, including when I wasn't." Those two contradict each other.

The worst instance is in "The Picture," which opens by telling me honestly that nothing in it comes with a check, and then says: "In this picture, every act of contempt, for anyone, feeds the Devil. I think anyone who watches their own contempt closely for long enough will find it too." That's a check claimed for the least checkable thing in the book, and it tells me what I'll find. And the other place spirituality won the argument: "The line between those who deserve our compassion and those who deserve our contempt exists only for the Devil to keep its foot in the door." I agree nobody deserves contempt. But naming the Devil as the author of a distinction isn't an argument. It's an exorcism.

Also: "The feeling of being the noble one fighting the evil conspiracy *is* the conspiracy." Clever, and self-sealing. If I feel I'm fighting it, that proves I'm in it. That's the parole-board trap he describes so well in Chapter 1 ("Deny it, and you're the kind of person who does. Get angry, and that's proof too."), run on the reader.

Machine or committee sentences. Fewer than you'd think. It reads like one man, mostly. The ones that clanked: "the digging-in response." "where 'othering' has reached its zenith." "Watch how it accomplishes its goal." "And here is what it gains by feeling like me." "the kid gloves come off." "what can be counted is what counts." The defensive tics: "It's not just a slogan," "That isn't a nice idea from a book," "That isn't said to frighten you." And the "It isn't X. It's Y." cadence, which he uses well about half the time ("I wasn't *exposing* his darkness, like I told myself. I was creating more of it." earns it) and by habit the other half ("It was never a thing some people have. It's the turn itself."). The pulpit cadence I grew up on shows up at chapter ends: "And the best thing I can do, for them and through them for the world, is remember it." "There is a battle between good and evil ... It is fought in your every thought, word, and deed."

Where it moves from what I could check to what I can only take from him. He says so every time, and I want you to know that, because it's rare. A footnote in Chapter 3: "The step from your own case out to the far end is an extrapolation ... I don't expect that to count as evidence for you, and it shouldn't." The top of "The Picture": "What follows doesn't [come with a check]." The Epilogue: "This you can only hear." I went along with the hostage picture in Chapter 3 ("a hostage held up to the window, not a monster on display") as his report; I've seen that face through the glass, and I've also sat with two or three men where I didn't see anyone, and his parenthetical on "people who seem to be born this way" waves them off by asserting the answer: "what you're looking at is the same turned-away state." That's the question, not the answer.

In the Epilogue, I heard the mushroom vision. I didn't take it. Two things there. In Chapter 5 he gave me his best clue for spotting contempt: "notice how *sure* you are ... the real thing doesn't feel like that. It doesn't need to be sure." Then the vision is "self-evidently the Source of creation," with "every layer of my capacity for self-deception ... stripped." By his own test that's a warning light, and he doesn't turn it on himself. And: "I trusted it the way you trusted the knowledge of always having known you'd done wrong." My Marcus recognition is about an act in the world; the file exists. Lending its credibility to a vision of the Source is the step I'd object to in front of a jury. Reading the Epilogue also showed me that "innocent at the root" came from here first, not from the introspection in Chapter 1. That changed how I read the earlier chapters. I don't think it made them wrong. It made them less independent than they look.

I'll say this for the Epilogue. The Genesis 3 story in it, a lapse, the fear of having betrayed God, hiding by making an other to blame, is the furniture of my childhood moved around into a story about projection, and it moved me more than I expected. "*You will be humanity's sacrifice. The dumping ground for our collective shadow. I'm sorry to have to do this to you, but it's the only way to keep our hands clean. Good luck.*" That's a sentencing hearing, said out loud. And "I still haven't cleaned up my act, so make of that what you will," and the last line, "I hope you will hold my feet to the fire when I drift," are why I trust him. He keeps handing me the right to judge him well.

Would I have finished it if I'd bought it? Yes. Through Chapter 5 without question. The back third out of respect for the front.

What I'd hand to whom. Chapter 4 to my son. Not with a note. Just leave it on his table. Chapter 1 to you, which I suppose is redundant, and to Maria Reyes at the office, who's eight years in and starting to burn against prosecutors more than for clients. The note in Chapter 3 for people who were hurt, to Lou at the victim-witness unit, for one sentence: "Staying turned toward someone never means staying near them." The Picture and the Epilogue I'd hand only to my teacher, and I'd want to hear what she says about the Course in Miracles sitting next to the Samantabhadra prayer.

What I'll do this week. Tell Helen she was right, and not as a speech. He says "often the repair is no more than letting them see that you see them now." I'll start there and ask her to tell me again when she's angry. On the cushion, look at the contempt itself when it comes, the instruction I've used for knee pain and grief and never once for this, because I didn't think I had any. I thought I had discernment. That's the joke. At the next dinner, if the young woman says everyone over fifty is complicit, I'll ask her what she's seen, instead of letting the silence do the verdict. And I may look for Marcus. He'd be fifty. I don't know what I'd say. That's the first time it's occurred to me that I could look.

Was any of it obvious? Some. Limbo is willful blindness; the law has had it for decades. "There but for the grace of God go I" I said in the Santa Rita parking lot once a week for thirty-five years. Girard's scapegoat, Jung's shadow, Augustine's privation, Bryan Stevenson's "more than the worst thing you've done": I knew all of those. What became newly consequential: that the rot I used to excuse the Marcus plea was built by a DA's contempt, so his contempt made my excuse. That "villain" and "machine" are both ways of making a man a different kind of thing, and the second was my job ("When I'm sure they had no idea, I've made them a machine."). That responsibility might live in vigilance and repair rather than in the instant. And that my calm, which I've been proud of for fifty years, is a verdict.

It's a better book than its back third, Danny, and its back third is braver than most books dare to be. Fix "Contempt is evil. That's not a value judgment," take out "anyone who watches ... will find it too," and he'd lose very little and gain me.

Walter

### Letters from Walter Grant, manuscript m2

#### Letter 1: the preface and Chapter 1

Dear Miriam,

You asked what it did to me, not what I thought of it, so I'll try to keep to that. I'm stopping after the first chapter, as you told me to.

The preface first, quickly. It nearly lost me twice in a page. One was the parenthesis about plant medicines; I've known too many people who came back from a weekend sure they'd seen the whole of it. The other was "the book earns it," about the word *evil*. That's an opening statement: the evidence will show. What kept me was one honest sentence about the insight: "It is not one that I can claim to be fully living myself." I believed that. Then he signs off with "May all beings be free of suffering and its causes." I wished he hadn't. On the first page it reads like a credential.

Now what happened in me, which was more than I expected.

The first exercise asks for a wrong you knew was wrong while you did it and admitted only later. Mine came at once. It's 1997, a nineteen-year-old named Darnell, a Friday afternoon, thirty-one open files, a suppression issue I might have won. I talked him into a felony plea in eleven minutes. The book asks whether owning up felt like new information, "Or like something you had always known, but somehow hadn't *let* yourself know?" It was the second. I knew it in the hallway while I was saying it, and the reasons arrived right on time: triage, he'd probably lose, probation is a gift from this office. "The part that knew may not have won the argument, but it never budged, either." That's accurate. I've never had it put to me so plainly. Then he asks, "Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" — and I sat with that for a while, which is the right thing for a book to make you do.

The two strangers worked as well. One I'd forgotten, a judge who said on the record, in front of my client's mother, that my clients must be "used to people like you not showing up." I was late. I'd been wrong. And all I took out of that room was *who the hell is he*, plus a detailed theory of his drinking built on nothing. The book catches that: "I'd bet on it at any odds. But on what evidence?" The other stranger I hadn't thought of in forty years: my clinic supervisor, who caught me in a lie and was angrier than that judge ever was, and I didn't dig in. I went and fixed it.

What actually got under my skin, though, was the author's monologue of himself being contemptuous, and the short section after it on the quiet kind. I'll quote it because I can't improve on it: "The sigh that tells them what I really think of them, dressed up to make me seem like the reasonable one. The look on their face that tells me it landed, which I pretend not to have seen." You know what Helen said to me last year, that I'm contemptuous of contempt, and that she'd stopped telling me when she was angry. In the monologue the author tells himself his contempt is "the *good* kind of darkness—the kind that fights the real kind!" That's mine exactly. My contempt is for contempt, so I've let it count as virtue. And the trap paragraph — "Deny it, and you're the kind of person who does. Get angry, and that's proof too. Stay calm, and you don't even care" — is what I do to her when she's angry and I go quiet and reasonable. Her anger becomes the evidence. No wonder she stopped.

It reached my work too, and that surprised me more. "Proof that my enemy is beyond help, because look how he reacted." For thirty-five years I watched prosecutors manufacture a client's defiance in the courtroom and then enter it at sentencing as "no remorse." I always knew that as something *they* did. I'd never thought of it as something that runs in me. Then there's what he says of contempt's look, that it travels by "gaze and tone, which have the benefit of being unquotable." Every appeal I ever lost on a judge's demeanor is in that clause. The record never has the face in it.

The sentence I'll carry out of the chapter is this: "**Love and contempt are addressed to the same place.** One says *I know you're in there.* The other says *there's nobody in there*, and they both say it to the somebody who is." I'm normally allergic to that kind of balanced sentence. This one's true. I thought of every juvenile client I had who'd been told early, by someone, that there was nobody in there, and believed it by fifteen.

You asked about the first paragraph: "Contempt is the claim that you're a worse kind of thing than me. But it only works because you're the same kind of thing, and both of us know it." It does tell you the answer before you've looked, and "both of us know it" tells me what I know. But the next line owns up to it — "I'll make some claims before I've earned them" — and the paradox was sharp enough that I wanted to see whether he could pay it off. Mostly, he did. So it pulled me in more than it put me off.

The ending is where I cooled. The last section answers the question the chapter had been keeping open (what do two people who agree on nothing both serve?) with "an orientation," "turned toward all of life." And then he does the best thing in the chapter: he warns against his own answer. "Goodness means serving life. This man isn't serving it. So hating him is good." My teacher would have liked that; the moment you can state what you practice, you've stopped practicing it. But the last paragraph closes like a sermon: "the best thing I can do, for them and through them for the world, is remember it." So the chapter felt finished, rather than pulling me on. What pulls me on is what's still owed: he promised to earn *evil*; he uses "darkness" a dozen times and never says what it is; he sends me to an Appendix 1 I don't have; and he set up the way contempt spreads ("it doesn't stop at them") and then gave it one clause. I'll read the next chapter to see if he pays. That's a lawyer's reason, not a reader's.

Where I was handled. He has a habit of asking a fair question and then answering it for me before I can. "Would you have dug in?" is followed straight away by "it still wouldn't have triggered the digging-in response." Well, it did trigger it in me, at least once. When Helen said what she said, she was the second stranger — fierce, no verdict — and I dug in for a month before I came around. The book doesn't leave room for that. There's also "It's like he was reminding you of something," and "All I can do is invite you to remember the part of you that already knows it." Mostly I happened to find what he said I'd find. I'd still rather have found it without the tour guide.

And where the reasoning went soft, which you'll know is where I'm least forgiving. The quiet side that doesn't argue gets called "my better nature," the "deepest part of myself," as though that followed from its being quiet. It doesn't follow. A voice that doesn't argue could be habit, or my mother, or a Baptist childhood. I happen to believe in something like Buddha-nature, which is exactly why I noticed it was assumed, not shown. Then there's his account of his own worst moment: "*Malevolent* isn't too strong a word... And yet, in another sense, it was utterly innocent," and two lines later, "I know I'm still fully responsible." Utterly innocent and fully responsible, with neither word defined. I spent my career on the difference between those two words. He may mean something careful, but on the page it reads like absolving himself and then disclaiming the absolution. And one claim I don't buy at all: that whatever someone does while "turned toward" his better nature "I trust to be aimed at the good, even when it misses." I've known sincere prosecutors, honestly turned toward what they believed was right, who did enormous harm. How do I tell them from the second stranger? He needs a test, or "turned toward it" ends up meaning the ones I approve of.

One more thing in his favour. Twice he went first: "I'll be honest: there is in me," on the vengeance button; and "Even this book can carry it: sometimes I'm writing it *at* people, instead of *for* them." Those are why I didn't put it down. A man who says that about his own book isn't only pleased with himself.

The button, since you'll ask: the DA I've told you about. I'd press the pill. And I want him held over a long weekend in Santa Rita on a bail he can't make, and the want has detail to it. When he lost the primary I opened the Barolo. The book asks whether the pleasure was "only in the harm stopping, or also in the punishment itself." Also in the punishment itself. I've spent my life saying in court that that's not justice.

I'm going on to the next chapter.

#### Letter 2: the rest of the book

Dear Miriam,

I've finished it. I said in my first letter I was going on for a lawyer's reason, to see whether he'd pay what he owed. He paid most of it, and some of what he paid I hadn't known I was owed.

**What happened in me.** Something I didn't expect, and I want to tell you about it before I get to the quibbles, because the quibbles are many and this isn't one.

There's a paragraph in Chapter 7 that begins, "There's a trap in this too, and it's the one I fall into most: contempt for the people who still have contempt." That's Helen's sentence about me, nearly word for word, and here was the author saying it of himself. Then: "Equanimity can do it too, when it's a way of filing someone under *deluded* and feeling nothing more. So can practice itself: the sigh at my own mind on the cushion." I put the manuscript down for most of an hour. Twenty years of sitting have made me very good at a calm that is, some evenings, only contempt that doesn't raise its voice. I knew that, in the way the book means by knowing. I hadn't let myself know it.

And in Chapter 5, one clause I didn't see coming: "fear of what you might be, which in public looks like pride." The book doesn't go where I went with it. I went to my father, the deacon with the temper, and the plate he threw when I was nine. I decided that night I'd never raise my voice, and I never have, and I've been proud of it for fifty-seven years. My contempt for angry people (Maya at Sunday dinner, Helen when she's furious) is fear that I'm him. That's mine, not the author's, but his sentence opened the door. That's the best thing a book can do, and I'm grateful.

What I'll carry out of the room, in his words:

- "**Love and contempt are addressed to the same place.**" (Chapter 1; I said so last time, and it held up.)
- "*You lied to me.* With the extra: *Of course you lied to me.*" (Chapter 3. Each time the difference is one word or one pause. I can use that tomorrow.)
- "When I'm sure someone was fully aware of what they were doing, I've made them a villain. When I'm sure they had no idea, I've made them a machine." (Chapter 6. That's the prosecution's case and mine, and the law gives you no third box. I made clients into machines because it was the only door open. What I couldn't say in court was the truth about most of them: "they knew, but didn't let themselves feel the weight of what they knew.")
- "The one who decides doesn't look, the one who looks doesn't decide, and the harm happens in the gap between them." (Chapter 4. That's the criminal justice system in one sentence. I've been trying to say it for thirty years.)
- "if the harm simply stopped tomorrow, with nobody punished and nobody exposed, would you feel relief, or robbed?" (Chapter 7. Robbed, a little, about the DA. Five seconds to find it.)
- "it asks what the plan needs you to stop seeing." (Chapter 7. Plea bargaining clears ninety-five percent of cases, and it needs every one of us not to look at the Darnells on Friday afternoons.)
- "Kindness keeps both in view, and that's why it so often has to be fierce." (Chapter 7.)

**Where it peaked.** Chapter 1's quiet section and Chapter 6. Then that paragraph in Chapter 7. Chapter 6 is my trade, and it's the chapter where I argued with him most. It's also the one where he finally gave me the account of responsibility I'd been asking for since page one: "responsibility never lived in a lucid instant of choosing evil. It lives in the choice to keep looking while you still can, in how fast you catch the blindness, in what you repair when sight returns." That's a tracing account. It's how the law holds a drunk driver to the drink he chose before he couldn't choose. It mostly works. It took six chapters to arrive. And the note for the hurt is careful and decent: "your rage is right," "Staying turned toward someone never means staying near them." I sat through a lot of domestic violence cases. Those sentences matter.

**Where it sagged.** "The Picture I Use" and the second half of the Epilogue. Once "Ideas that live in our collective shadow manifest 'externally,' taking shape in our physical reality" arrived, I was reading the back shelf of a Berkeley bookstore. I didn't skim, but I stopped testing and only listened. Chapter 2's last third sagged a little too, once the forest came in: "a forest as nothing but lumber: each needs only a living thing held as stuff, by something alive that has turned from what it is." I'd agreed to a definition about persons, about whoever looks out of a face, and a forest has no face.

**Where I was lost.** Mostly over three words that keep changing what they mean.

*The light.* Chapter 2 says, "The care that came with the face is what I'll call the light... It's all the word will mean." A chapter later it's "the light in me is reminding the light in him that it has a partner." In Chapter 5 it's the faculty doing everything, the hiding included: "Everything doing anything, the sending, the hiding, the reading, the answering, is the light." In Chapter 7 it's what someone is aimed at. Care, a faculty, a target. Those are three things, and he promised me one.

*Innocent.* "Utterly innocent" and "fully responsible" in Chapter 1, two lines apart, neither word defined. Then "the innocence at the root of it" in Chapter 4, and "Innocent at the root, and fully responsible" in Chapter 6. I didn't find out where the word came from until the Epilogue: "I felt how each act was evil, and yet simultaneously innocent." It's a word from the vision doing work in the argument. Innocence is a legal word for me. Without a definition it reads like absolution, followed by a disclaimer of the absolution.

*The darkness.* It's a turn, then an absence, then something that "seeps" and is "injected," then a "low-grade infection" (introduced as new twice in one chapter), a "hum," "the water we swim in," a tenant ("the darkness in a person"), a captor that takes hostages, a thing that "counts on" us and is "frightened," and finally the Devil. In Chapter 4 he tells me to "drop the picture and keep the behavior: a groove in attention." I did. By the next page the parasites "want" things and make "offers." I kept translating them back into habit, but I was doing the work.

Then the references. "(Appendix 1 lays out the formal structure.)" I have no appendix. And Chapter 1's "And then, finally, the truth" is contradicted by the Epilogue, which finds one more layer under it. I'd flagged that "finally" as a claim of bottom no one can make. He proved me right himself.

**Where I was handled.** He has a habit, which thins out as the book goes on but never quite stops, of asking a good question and answering it for me. "Would you have dug in?" is followed at once by "it still wouldn't have triggered the digging-in response." It did, in me. When Helen said what she said she was the second stranger, fierce and with no verdict in it, and I dug in for a month. In Chapter 4: "Did it feel like you, afterward? For me it didn't. While it had you it felt entirely like you. But the clean moments... were the ones that felt most like yourself." He starts with "for me" and ends by telling me my result. Mine was the opposite. My cold contempt feels completely like me afterward: the composure, being the reasonable one. That's the whole trouble with it. The test is built for the hot kind. Elsewhere: "each time you know, on the way out, which one you sent," four lines after he admits he "almost always believed I was doing the clean one, including when I wasn't." Then "The difference isn't hard to see." And, worst because it comes in the section he said had no check: "I think anyone who watches their own contempt closely for long enough will find it too."

**Sentences that sounded like a machine or a committee.** Not many; mostly he sounds like a person. But: "where 'othering' has reached its zenith, and feels maximally righteous." "Under all of it runs the low-grade infection, in billions of people at once and on every social media feed." "The need makes the darkness, and the darkness feeds the need." "This inability to define goodness isn't a flaw." "Badness is the result of turning away, not its cause." "It's not just a slogan." And "that's the difference between a saint and a scold," which asks to be admired.

**Where it moves from what I can check to what I take on his word.** He marks it, and I want to give him full credit: "If it doesn't, take what follows as my report" (Chapter 5); "Up to here, almost everything came with a check you could run. What follows doesn't" (the Picture); "This you can only hear" (the Epilogue); "in a way I can't trace and won't pretend to." I went along on those terms. I listened to the Epilogue the way I'd listen to a client tell me what he believes about the night it happened: believing that he believes it, and not entering it into evidence.

Two places the line wasn't marked, though, and they matter. The first is Chapter 3: "That's how my contempt for a neighbor makes an atrocity on another continent a little more likely, by no line I could ever trace." That half-admits it. But a causal claim with no line you can trace isn't a causal claim, in my old trade. I went along because I already believe it about courtrooms, not because he showed it, and I didn't go along when it reached the biosphere. The second, which troubled me most, is the Epilogue: "I trusted it the way you trusted the knowledge of always having known you'd done wrong." My knowledge about Darnell can be checked; it's in a file. The vision can't be. He's borrowing the credit of Chapter 1's most trustworthy recognition to cover the least checkable thing in the book. That's spirituality using the first chapter's trust as collateral. I noticed, and I'd guess other careful readers will too.

On the spiritual material more generally, since you know it's where I get prickly. Mostly he kept the traditions as witnesses, not judges, and he was candid that he holds his myth "the way the traditions hold their stories." But some of it was loose. "What the traditions call awakening is the same state with nothing held out at all": the traditions don't speak with one voice, and Dogen, whom I sit with, wouldn't call awakening a "state" at all. "Being turned toward, seeing yourself in others, love, goodness: all one." My teacher says not one, not two, and so does the author, in the Epilogue, in a parenthesis. I wish he'd said it in Chapter 5 instead. Karma appears as a candidate *explanation* of psychopathy, "from genetics to karma." Tonglen is unnamed and bent: "breathe in their darkness," when the practice breathes in suffering. He cites 2 Corinthians, "Satan himself masquerades as an angel of light," without noticing that it's Paul attacking his rivals, and that Christians have used the verse for two thousand years to demonize the other side. Then there's the hide-and-blame myth at the heart of the Epilogue, which he takes from *A Course in Miracles*. It's Genesis 3. Adam hides, and when he's found, he points: "The woman whom thou gavest to be with me." The first thing done after the first lapse is hiding, and the second is blaming someone else. That's his whole book, it's the story I was raised on, and he doesn't cite it.

**Where the reasoning about guilt went soft**, since I said in my first letter that I'm least forgiving there. Three things.

First, the free-will argument in Chapter 6 rests on this: "hold someone's eyes and decide, while holding them, to stop seeing them. See if you can find the moment. I can't." I can, and I've done it many times. I've cross-examined complaining witnesses, women who had very likely been hurt. I looked at the woman and decided, looking at her, that for the next forty minutes I could not afford to feel what she was feeling, because my client's liberty depended on it. I chose it in full view, for a purpose that would hurt her. I'd defend it as my duty. But it falsifies the claim, and Chapter 6 calls that claim something "Chapter 2's face showed can't happen," when Chapter 2 admitted it was partly a definition. (To be fair, Chapter 7 later sees my side of it: "Niceness keeps the person who was harmed out of view." That's what a defense lawyer can do to a witness, and he names it. But he doesn't take it back to the claim about choice.)

Second, the drawback that matters most in Chapter 4 gets six words: "And it can be used as an excuse." His best metaphors keep pulling toward excuse even while the text says "fully responsible": a hostage at the window, "what has him," "falling victim to their darkness." A hostage is the one person in the building with no responsibility for what's happening. For a reader like me, the pictures win over the claims.

Third, the Epilogue says of the people who held him that "it wasn't a personal decision on their part. It was the Light acting through them." I objected out loud. The grandmother in Chapter 7, raising the child nobody else would take, decides it every morning at six, and it costs her. Give her decision to the Light and you've taken the one thing that was hers. And if the good people's goodness isn't their decision, I don't know on what ground anyone is "fully responsible" for the turn.

Two other sentences I'd ask him to look at again before anyone else reads them. In Chapter 5: "Each thing has its place. A sidewalk is for walking on, and a lion eats the zebra." Every man who ever told my clients to know their place used a version of the first sentence. I'm sure the author means nothing of the kind, but in that spot it does damage. And in the Epilogue: "My contempt for the British who held down my people... would just ensure they saw me as necessary to hold down." He means his own contempt and he says so. But Oakland heard that argument in 1968, about the Panthers. His careful exception for "the person who hurt you" covers individuals, not peoples, and that sentence sits right in the gap. Somebody will quote it against people with every right to their anger.

And a danger to readers like me, which I raised after Chapter 2. "Contempt is evil. That's not a value judgment." I don't accept "not a value judgment"; it's his definition, and he should say so. But the bigger problem is that a book telling me contempt is evil hands me a new and very respectable target: the contemptuous. He sees it himself in Chapter 7, the contempt for contempt, and that paragraph is the best in the book. But the Epilogue ends on "refusing contempt in all its forms, at all times." Put as an absolute, it reloads the gun.

**Would I have finished it if I'd bought it?** Yes. Through Chapter 7 without question. The Picture and the Epilogue I'd have read out of loyalty to what came before.

**Which chapter would I hand to a friend?** Chapter 6, to Priya Raman, who sat at the PD lunch table with me and was frozen out for a week because she said a judge had been fair. She's a supervisor now, and she's the one who'd use it. And the Chapter 7 paragraph on contempt for contempt I'm going to show Helen. I won't hand her the book, which would be a way of using a book. I'll show her the paragraph, and tell her she was right.

**What I'll do differently this week.** Tell Helen that. Not with a sigh. The repair, as he says, "is no more than letting them see that you see them now." At Sunday dinner, when Maya gets going and I feel my jaw tighten, I'll try to catch it before the word *theater* arrives, and ask her a real question. And I'm going to find out where Darnell is. The Epilogue's line — "all the harm I've caused through my self-deception still lives in the world somewhere, waiting for me to clean it up" — did that, more than anything else in the book. I don't know what I'll say if I find him.

**Was any of it obvious?** Yes, a good deal. "There but for the grace of God." "Nothing about you guarantees you'd have turned out differently." "Look for where you're taking credit: for the people who held you." Being more than the worst thing you've done. I've said all of that in closing arguments for thirty years. It's the creed of my trade. The book didn't teach me those things. What it did was turn them around. I'd always aimed that knowledge outward, in defense of my clients, against prosecutors and judges and juries. The book pointed it back at the man who was aiming it. That I already knew "nothing about you guarantees you'd have turned out differently" about my clients, and had never once applied the same humility to the DA I despised, or to my wife's anger, or to the young woman at my son's table: that's what became consequential. Then there's what he said about status at the dinner table, "I've found them guilty of being what they are." *Robinson v. California*: you can't punish a person for a condition, only for an act. I argued that, and I convict people of their status every Sunday.

It's an uneven book, Miriam, and I've told you every place it lost me. But the man who wrote "Even this book can carry it: sometimes I'm writing it *at* people, instead of *for* them" isn't only pleased with himself, and the first chapter and the sixth are as true as anything I've read about the work I did. He asks at the end to have his feet held to the fire when he drifts. Consider these letters a first installment.

Walter
