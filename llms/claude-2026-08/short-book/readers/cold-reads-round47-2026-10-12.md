# Cold reads, round 47 (October 11–12)

*Five writers who practice, on the whole short book after the cross-reference repair (snapshot short-sent-round47.md), in the same three stages as round 46, each ending with a ranked list headed BEFORE YOU SEND IT TO YOUR SANGHA: must fix, should fix, leave alone. Midway, Aditya added that the book should reach a general audience, not only practitioners; that reached each reader before the third letter, so every list marks its items sangha, general or both, and earlier letters are practitioner-framed. Quotes checked against the files; the ones that don't match are the readers' own people and sayings. Letters verbatim.*

## Ruth (58, memoirist in Portland, Maine; Karma Kagyu)

### Stage 1 (preface, introduction, Chapter 1)

Dear Aditya,

Steady through the preface, a drag through the introduction, and then I devoured Chapter 1 from "Have you ever done something you knew was wrong" to the end of the Encounter. My attention went slack in two places: the introduction, and the middle of "The Corruption," the stretch where you explain what the Encounter had already shown me. The Way Out I read twice, the second time because I wanted to, the first because I had to.

I'll tell you what happened in me first, in order, because that is the part you can't get from anyone who doesn't do the exercises.

**The limbo check.** The wrong that came up was not one I'd have chosen. In my first book there's a chapter about the night my brother Erik's wife, Lisa, left him. I wasn't there. I wrote it from Erik's account and my own resentment, and I shaped it. I gave her one line of dialogue, the coldest thing he remembered her saying, and none of the four years before it. I knew at the time I was shading it. I didn't let myself know it until the book was out. And the justifications were exactly the ones you list, only in a memoirist's dialect: it's my truth, memoir is one person's side, everyone who writes about family does this, it's one scene. The part that knew never budged. Your line "The part that knew may not have won the argument, but it never budged, either" is true of me, and I'd underline it if this were paper.

"Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" stopped me for a good while. I had no answer except yours.

Then "Did that happen passively?" Yes. When my editor at the time asked, mildly, whether Lisa had seen the chapter, I changed the subject to permissions for the epigraph. I knew I was doing it. So: bought, and it cost me something to buy it.

**The remorse-in-reverse check.** Here I pushed back, and I'll tell you how it came out. I have felt sick over something decent. The first time I refused Erik money, after years of Al-Anon telling me I had to, I felt for weeks like I had betrayed him. It felt like remorse. Then I read your list, and I'm honestly not sure. "guilt at breaking a rule I was raised on" covers it uncomfortably well: in my family, Norwegian Lutheran, you take care of your own, and I had broken that. So I bought it tentatively. But a lot of people in your sangha will have done Al-Anon or something like it, and every one of them will reach for this exact case. The list is doing the work. Make sure it stays.

**The two strangers.** I have a first stranger who is real. After my first book came out, a one-star review appeared under a name I didn't recognize. It was not about the prose. It said, in effect, that I was the kind of woman who feeds on her family's wreckage. Within a paragraph I knew it was Lisa. And it did exactly what you say: it made it harder, not easier, to look at the chapter I'd shaded. I spent two years composing replies to it in the shower. I never once thought about the chapter during those two years.

And here is what your book did that I didn't see coming. Reading "The Encounter," I realized I had been her first stranger first. In print. In a book with my name on it. Her review was contempt, but my chapter had handed her a verdict too, *this is all you are*, in 4,000 words, to strangers. I don't know yet what to do with that. I'm telling you because it is the effect you're after, and it happened.

The second stranger: my sponsor in Al-Anon, years ago, who told me flatly that I was enabling Erik because I needed him to need me. It was harsher than anything Lisa wrote. I didn't dig in. You have that exactly right: "he could have been *harsher* than the first person ... and it still wouldn't have triggered the digging-in response." (I'm eliding; the full sentence is in "The Two Strangers.")

**The two buttons.** Lisa. I'll be honest, since you were: I wanted the first button, and not as a flicker. Then something odd. I've spent twenty years wishing for the second button for Erik, a pill that changes his heart, and in Al-Anon you learn there isn't one and you stop wishing. So for Erik the exercise had no charge at all. For Lisa it had all of it. That difference told me more than the exercise was asking. You might know that for readers with an addict in the family, the pill will read as the oldest fantasy they have.

**Cold contempt.** There is an editor in Portland, who runs the books pages I would most like to be reviewed in, and who once said to me at a reading, smiling, that he "doesn't really do spiritual books." I filed him away that night. I have been polite to him since with nothing behind it. "a quiet filing-away, a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them" is a sentence that knows me. I had never counted that as contempt, because I thought contempt was hot. Bought.

**The sigh.** "The sigh that tells them what I really think of them, dressed up to make me seem like the reasonable one." My husband, Paul, last Tuesday, about the dishwasher. I'd have put the book down here for a minute if it were a book.

**Where I refused, or held back.** "The darkness leaks out of me and empowers his, because its meaning is universal." I don't know that its meaning is universal. I know it was universal between me and Lisa. You're claiming something about everyone in a chapter that has been careful to ask me about my own case. And "Evil behavior is unconscious in me, and fully aware in my enemies" is good, but it comes after a lot of explaining I didn't need.

---

**As a writer.**

What's working is the questions. You ask, and then you stop, and you let me go look. "Does that make it *easier* or *harder* to confront what you already know?" is a question I answered with my whole body. The voice in "The Encounter" is the best writing in the file: "he's **bad**, dammit," "*Shit.* He's onto me," "it's the *good* kind of darkness—the kind that fights the real kind." That's a person, caught, telling on himself. I trusted you completely in that section, because you were the one being exposed. Keep it exactly as it is.

"I'd take that bet at any odds. But on what evidence?" Perfect.

"**Love and contempt are addressed to the same place.**" That is the sentence of the chapter, and the paragraph before it earns it: I went and felt for where Lisa's review reached, and it wasn't my pride. It was the place you name.

"a hostage held up to the window, not a monster on display." The image does more than three paragraphs of argument around it.

What isn't working:

1. **The preface is out of order.** "This version is for friends who sit," then the warning, then "So this leaves out a great deal." That "So" has nothing to follow from; it belongs after the paragraph about leaning on assumptions. As it stands, the warning interrupts a thought, and the reader feels it.

2. **The preface spends too long on what this isn't.** A full book I don't have, that's better than this, that works differently from this, "though only chapter one feels finished for now." By the end of the preface I've been told four times that I'm reading the lesser version. Say it once.

3. **The introduction is a drag.** It announces. "Now for the strange part, which this book exists to show." "By the end I'll be claiming something stranger still." "One consequence will matter to readers who trust reasoning above all." It tells me what's coming three times before anything comes, and it promises things ("common knowledge, in a sense Chapter 1 will make plain") that Chapter 1 never names. I looked: Chapter 1 has "we both know" and "and so on," but never the phrase, so a reader waiting for the plain sense won't find it. Then Chapter 1 opens with its own paragraph that does the introduction's job better: "Contempt is the claim that you're a worse kind of thing than me. But it only works because you're the same kind of thing, and both of us know it." That's the opening. The introduction could be half its length.

4. **"I know that's a heavy word, and it's not meant as a judgement or attack. The book tries to earn it carefully."** Defensive. A memoirist learns this the hard way: when you apologize for a word before using it, you make the reader wary of it. Use it and earn it.

5. **The middle of "The Corruption" explains the Encounter.** Paragraphs like "My enemy sees I'm turned away, even though I don't. *Because* I don't." are good, but the run from "Part of me refuses to believe" through "Evil behavior is unconscious in me" is a commentary on a scene I just lived through. Scenes earn claims; then trust them. About a third of that section could go and I'd lose nothing. The "If you've been on his end, you know how it closes around you" paragraph should stay: that one gives me something to check, not something to agree with.

6. **"In brief: I am in limbo about provoking him, and showing him that I am in limbo is itself the provocation. The signal is the pretense that there is no signal."** Clever, and it went right past me. After "Love and contempt are addressed to the same place," this reads like a lecture note.

7. **"The Way Out," first paragraph,** is the densest in the chapter and the most important. "the turning is the departure, not a second thing underneath" took me three reads. The torturer sentence is very good. Give it room: break that paragraph in two at "Not out of sight."

8. Small: "see whether using them will go" is an odd phrase and I didn't know what you meant on first reading. "uniquely insidious" in the introduction is the kind of adjective you don't need.

---

**What a Mahamudra practitioner will trip on.**

1. **The plant medicines, in the first sentence of the preface.** I don't care. Some in your sangha will, and some lamas care a great deal; it touches the precept on intoxicants, and in our lineage it touches a long argument about whether drug experiences are recognition or a counterfeit of it. Putting it in parentheses in your first sentence means it's the first thing they'll discuss, before they've read a line of the argument. If it matters to the honesty of the book, give it a sentence of its own later. If it doesn't, cut it.

2. **"The mind is intrinsically compassionate."** As the first sentence of the introduction, flat. Our teachers say emptiness and compassion are inseparable, that compassion is the unobstructed display of the mind's nature. Your sentence is close to that, but stated as a bare assertion it will make the careful ones in your sangha ask "says who?" before you've shown them anything. Chapter 1 shows it; the introduction just asserts it.

3. **"Evil."** Not a Buddhist word, and a Lutheran-raised reader like me hears it in a Lutheran key. You define it, which helps: "That turning, in however small a way, is the only thing this book will mean by 'evil.'" But "darkness" and "the light" and the Gospel of Philip epigraph will make some practitioners wonder what tradition they're in. I liked the epigraph. I'm telling you it will be noticed.

4. **"What you hid it from is what you call 'I' when nothing is being hidden."** A Mahamudra student will bristle: when nothing is hidden, there's no "I" doing the not-hiding. You do follow it with "I'm not claiming there's a thing in there you could point to," which helps, and I think what you mean is recognition, not a self. But the word "I" will snag. "What's left when the hiding stops" is the phrase that will land with us. Lead with that.

5. **"it matters to see the mechanism by which straying from recognition creates samsara."** That's a large claim to put in a preface for people who've been told by their teachers that samsara's mechanism is laid out in the twelve links. Say it's one way of seeing part of it, which is what your next sentence nearly says.

6. **Recognition "on the spot."** In our lineage, the nature of mind is pointed out by a teacher. Some readers will feel a book offering recognition is overstepping. I don't think you are; your exercises point at something ordinary, not at the nature of mind. But say "assumes ... that recognition is available on the spot" carefully, or someone's lama will read it.

None of this should stop you. The chapter worked on me, and not in the way I expected. I came for the review and the editor. I found the chapter I wrote about Lisa.

Ruth

### Stage 2 (Chapters 2 to 4, "The Picture I Use")

Dear Aditya,

Steady, with two stretches I devoured and one I'd have put the book down in. I devoured the opening of Chapter 2 (the face) and almost all of Chapter 4 from "Here is how to tell them apart in yourself" on. I read "What I'm claiming" out of duty and skimmed the end of it. I skimmed the locker room. And I'd have put the book down, or at least closed it for the night and not been sure I'd come back, at the conspiracy paragraph in "The Picture I Use." More on each below.

**How the chapters hand off.**

Chapter 1 to 2 is clean. "Chapter 1 held a hint: you couldn't have done it if you'd seen it clearly." I knew where I was and why I was going on.

Chapter 2 to 3 is fine but slack, because Chapter 2 doesn't end on a question. It ends on a list of claims, and the list ends on King. Chapter 3 then opens on a good question ("why do they turn away in the first place?") that Chapter 2 never made me want to ask.

Chapter 3 to 4 is broken by "The Picture I Use." Chapter 3 ends: "So the question isn't whether you're in this. It's what to do from inside it." That's a real hand-off, and I wanted Chapter 4 at once. Instead I got the Devil, the institutions and the conspiracy. Then Chapter 4 opened on "Chapter 2 claimed there is one guide," which answers neither Chapter 3's last line nor the Picture. If the Picture has to be in the book, put it after Chapter 4, where it can be what it says it is, a picture held at arm's length, and not a speed bump between a question and its answer.

**Where it repeats.**

- "What I'm claiming" is Chapter 2 again, compressed, and then several of the claims are made a third time in Chapter 4 ("love at someone else's expense isn't love," the one guide, compassion versus wisdom). The list says "Here are the claims, stated flat." A practitioner reading slowly doesn't need them twice. Either make the list the only place they're stated flat, and cut the flat statements around it, or cut the list.
- Self-contempt as false accountability: in Chapter 1 ("It feels like accountability. It isn't.") and again at the end of the note for people who were hurt ("It feels like accountability."). The second time I noticed I'd been told.
- "Nothing is confined to your skull" (Chapter 4) after "It never stays confined to your skull" (Chapter 2).
- The second stranger is called back, I counted roughly, eight or nine times, and somewhere along the way he becomes "the second person." I thought at first you meant someone new. Pick one name.
- The buttons get called back three ways: "This is what the buttons in Chapter 1 were measuring," then "Chapter 1's pill in a form you can use daily," then "the flicker from Chapter 1, the one that reached for the first button." Each is good. Three is a tic.
- The book naming itself: "That's the claim at the center of this book," "It's why this book was originally titled *Contempt Is Evil*," "It's also why the book opened with one person." I'd keep none of them. The old title in particular will put people off: it sounds like the preachy book this mostly isn't.

**What landed.**

The face. "Imagine looking into a stranger's face until it stops being something you're looking at and becomes a place somebody is looking out of." I did this with a woman at the next table in the coffee shop where I was reading. Then with Lisa. With Lisa the story was there before the face, exactly as you say, and it was the review, word for word.

The upkeep paragraph: "A grudge has to be fed." I have fed this one for nine years. "It feels like setting something down." Yes, I know that from Erik, the year I stopped being angry with him. I didn't have to do anything; I just put it down.

The parasite. I had a strong reaction here, and you should know why, because a good part of your sangha will have the same one. In Al-Anon and AA we have had this word for fifty years: *the disease*. "That's the disease talking." "The disease wants him dead, and it wants you crazy." It is exactly your parasite: it lives off a host, it hides, it argues in the person's own voice, it defends itself when seen. And we learned your drawback the hard way too. "That's your disease talking" is one of the cruelest things you can say to an alcoholic, because it's contempt in a fresh coat, your phrase, and it's true. So when you wrote "This picture has real drawbacks, and you should know them," I trusted you more. You might say, in a sentence, that recovery people have been using this picture for a long time. It's evidence that it works, and evidence of how it fails.

"Staying turned toward someone never means staying near them." That is "detach with love," the hardest thing Al-Anon ever taught me, and you said it better. The note for people who were hurt is the most careful thing in the book. I'd show it to women in my old meeting.

Chapter 4's checks. These are what change my week.

"if the harm simply stopped tomorrow, with nobody punished and nobody exposed, would you feel relief, or robbed?" Lisa's review is still up. If it simply disappeared tomorrow, and no one ever knew she wrote it: robbed. I want her exposed. I want people to know. I've told myself for nine years that what I want is the review gone. What I want is her caught. That's the finding, and I didn't want it.

The examples of "the extra": "*You lied to me.* With the extra: *Of course you lied to me.*" I have said "Of course you lied to me" to my brother, in those words, more than once. I know which one I sent.

"And pity, which passes for compassion and is contempt with a soft face: it needs them to stay below me." This one I'll be carrying a while. Twenty years of being the sister who got her life together, and I have been pitying Erik in a voice that sounded like love. And the sangha forms: "The friend who 'isn't there yet.' The beginner's question, heard with a patient smile." I have been doing ngöndro longer than half the people in our Tuesday group have been sitting. I know that patient smile. I've worn it.

"Your sister's ex." I laughed out loud, not happily. It's my brother's ex. You couldn't have known.

"look at the contempt itself, not the person. Where is it? What's it made of?" This is plain Mahamudra, looking at the klesha rather than its object, and it's the moment where your book and my practice became the same thing. I tried it in the car with the editor. It didn't survive, the first time. The story came back within a minute, as you say it will.

**What I refused.**

"No amount of turning away ever produces a turning back." My brother's last relapse ended the day his son told him, with real contempt, that he was a waste of a father. Erik went to detox the next morning and has been sober six years. You'd say he turned back through the awareness the contempt was attacking, not through the contempt, and maybe that's right. But I noticed that the claim is built so that no case could count against it. A writer who spent a book on her brother's addiction will think of that, and so will others in your sangha. You hedge it ("It can frighten someone into stopping, and now and then into thinking again"), which helps, but then "The only thing that has ever turned anyone back" takes the hedge away again.

"I can't find a hot atrocity that didn't begin there." Maybe true. It's a claim about history in a book that has asked me, so far, only about myself.

The Hitler paragraph. Nobody in the room raised Hitler. It reads as if you're arguing with someone off the page.

"every system of extraction and destruction on Earth rests on that." Too big. The tree and the arch are lovely and they work: I felt it before any story. The sentence before them asks me to sign for the whole world economy first.

The far end. The footnote about the mushrooms is the second time drugs come up, and this time they're holding up a claim: that the most lost person carries more knowledge, not less. You say it shouldn't count as evidence, and I respect that. But a footnote on a mushroom vision about the inside of malevolence, in a book for a Kagyu sangha, will be the thing some people repeat about it. The exercise after it is good, and is enough.

**"The Picture I Use."** The note at the top is honest, and I believed it. Then the text doesn't keep to it. The Devil has already appeared before the note, in Chapter 3: "The background hum of the Devil, if you like the name." And inside the Picture, the darkness starts to have wants: "It doesn't care who you hate, as long as you hate," "Its purpose is to provoke the darkness on the other side." That's a lot of intention for an absence. Then the institutions: "At some level, we need them to remain." And the conspiracy archetype. Here I closed the book. Not because I think you're wrong; the paragraph is careful, and says real conspiracies exist. Because I know exactly how "where the 'evil global conspiracy' archetype comes from" reads to anyone whose brother-in-law sends them videos. It's the one paragraph in the book I'd be embarrassed to have a friend find on my nightstand. And it isn't needed. Everything true in it is already in "When we gather with allies to foster contempt."

**Where the book ends for me.** At "So here is the measure, and it's the one this book opened with: *this one is like me.*" Everything after that is good, and I read it gladly, but it's coda, and it ends four times: "The only way to end it," "Every act of integrity," "This is what the traditions were for," "So yes: there is a battle." The last paragraph is the strongest, and I'd keep it as the last word. Cut to it sooner.

**As a writer.**

Structure: Chapters 1 and 4 are the book. Chapter 2 is an argument that Chapter 1 already let me feel, plus a list. Chapter 3 is the best thinking in the book and the hardest to read, because the long paragraphs (the far-end paragraph especially, which runs from the smallest wrong to the factory-farm line to the most lost person) carry four turns each. The factory-farm worker arrives in one sentence and leaves in the next; he needs either a scene or nothing.

Pacing: the questions slow me down in the right way; the explanations slow me down in the wrong way. When you ask, I stop and look. When you explain, I start to read faster, and that's a sign.

Voice: you are best when you're the one caught. "I have said" is your strongest tense. The places I trusted least are the ones where the "I" disappears and the claims get large ("Every decision made while the people it touches are held out of view belongs to it"). The flat-Earthers parenthetical is the right kind of joke, because it's on you.

And one sentence I'd hang on the wall: "From outside, fierce and dark look alike, and it counts on our not wanting to tell them apart." Except I'd hang the next one: "But we can tell, from the sending end, if we're willing to look."

Ruth

### Stage 3 (the appendices, and the list)

Dear Aditya,

Read dutifully, on the whole: Appendix 1 a drag after its first section, Appendix 2 read with pleasure, Appendix 3 gobbled. Unasked, I'd have read Appendix 3 first, before the book, and it would have made me trust the book more. I'd have read Appendix 2 eventually. I would not have read Appendix 1, and "For the lawyers among you" I skimmed even asked.

**Appendix 1.**

The trap section is clearer than anything in the chapters about how the look works, and the seven-item list (the turn, the intent, the wanting, and on down to the license) is good: I could check each against Lisa's review and they held. "It's why malice is frightening and contempt is maddening." Yes. That distinction is worth more than the whole of section 2.

But a lot of it is Chapter 1 again, sometimes word for word. "**In contempt there is no separate deed.** The look is the deed, and there is nothing else." is in Chapter 1 already. So is the cutting-in-line passage, down to "The smallest morsel carries the whole, because there's nothing in it but the turn, and the turn has no size." So is "Take two things you checked," and, all but word for word, "That's all 'absence' means here." A reader who just finished the book will notice. If the appendix is for the reader who wants the mechanism taken apart, then it should take it apart, not repeat it.

"What I think is really happening" is the most honest writing in the appendices, and I liked being told plainly where belief begins. "I know how that sentence sounds." Good. Keep that.

Overclaims: "I've never met anyone who was surprised by their own wrong when they finally saw it." I'd believe "I've rarely," and I'd believe "I wasn't." And "The con man's warmth is the most reported case there is" I simply don't understand.

The line at the top, "The three appendices were written with Claude's help," will start a conversation in your sangha that you should be ready for. It's honest and I'm glad it's there. But put only on the appendices, it makes a reader ask about the chapters. And the appendices do sound different from the chapters, Appendix 1 especially ("That's a fixed point: the recursion never contains anything but the turn"). If the chapters also had help, say so once, in the preface. If they didn't, the note is fine where it is.

**Appendix 2** is generous and clear. "Most of what this book concludes is old. I want to say that plainly, because a reader who knows the territory will otherwise spend the whole book waiting for me to admit it." That was me. I did spend the whole book waiting. Move that paragraph, or a sentence of it, into the preface, and your sangha will read the book differently from the first page.

"I only got to the other thing because it was done to me and I had, for once, no way of not noticing." This is the best sentence in the appendices. It's a scene you haven't written. The chapters have you as the first stranger; this says you were the target once, and saw it. I'd want that story.

Two small things. Murdoch "is the closest of all of them," and then a few paragraphs later "**Projective identification** is the closest of all." Pick one. And the closing research note says "It shows that readers already believe goodness is more fundamental than evil": you mean people, not readers.

**Appendix 3.** For your sangha, this is the appendix that matters, and most of it is right. Marigpa, the adventitious obscurations, the clouds and the sky, "the nature of thought is dharmakaya," the Uttaratantra, Shantideva's stick and hand, lojong, tonglen: it's the view as I was taught it, and you hold it loosely and correctly. Your hedge on pointing-out instruction is exactly right and I'd fight to keep it: "I don't mean the two are the same thing; one is a specialized transmission and the other was an ordinary moment between two people." Without that sentence someone would be upset. With it, I was moved, because my sponsor did do something like that for me, and I'd never put the two side by side.

The Samantabhadra passage and the paragraph after it, holding the blank first and the fear second, answered the thing I'd marked in Chapter 3 ("I cannot give an account of why the first turn happens"). That was satisfying. "If that's wrong, the mistake is mine and not the traditions'." Good.

"What's mine, and a caution" is the most trustworthy section of the whole book. "The road starts from your worst moment rather than your best": that's your contribution in one line, and it's real: every teaching I've had starts from the good moment, from the glimpse. You start from the wrong I hid about Lisa, and it got me somewhere the glimpse never has.

What will trip people:

- The Eckhart block quote. I couldn't place it, and I was raised close enough to that world to have tried. "The devil is none other than the will turned away from God" is set off like scripture with no source. If you can give the sermon and translation, do. If you can't, cut it. One shaky quote in a section about honesty will make people distrust the good ones around it.
- A Course in Miracles, and Angelo DiLullo, beside the Samantabhadra prayer and Shantideva. You're honest that the Course is your road, and I respect that. But for a Kagyu sangha, the Course sits in a different section of the bookstore, and "Put that beside Eckhart below and the two traditions are saying one sentence" is a large claim to rest on a contemporary teacher. The DiLullo paragraph adds nothing the Shantideva paragraph doesn't already give. I'd cut it.
- "Every tradition that looked at this noticed the same thing about where it hides" and "That is the book's paradox exactly". You don't need the volume. The correspondences are strong enough said quietly.
- Trungpa. You mention him in passing and kindly. Some in a New England Kagyu sangha will flinch at the name for reasons that have nothing to do with your point. Leave it, but know it.
- "soldiers asked whether it's wrong to desecrate an enemy's corpse say yes at once": is that a study? If so, cite it. If it's your impression, say so.

What belongs in the book: the Mahamudra and Dzogchen correspondences, or a short paragraph of them, in the preface for this sangha. You wrote the preface for "friends who sit," and then hid from them the one section that shows them you know what they know. And the reverse: the institutions, the conspiracy and the Devil in "The Picture I Use" belong back here, with the other things marked as belief, not between Chapters 3 and 4.

---

**A postscript, now that I know who the book is for.** I've just heard from you, through the person who sent me the files, that this isn't meant only for practitioners: the sangha is your first readers, and you want the book to reach a general reader with no practice at all. I've left everything above as I wrote it. But I went back through all three files with my other readers in mind, the ones who come to my memoir readings, and the women from my old Al-Anon meeting who have never sat a day in their lives.

The good news first. The body of the book is almost there already. Chapter 1 asks only for memory: a wrong you hid, a stranger who caught you, an enemy, a button. None of it needs a cushion. The face in Chapter 2, the checks in Chapter 4, the note for people who were hurt: my non-practicing friends could do every one of them, and I think the note for people who were hurt would reach them sooner than it reaches us. The practice words in the body are few, and each comes with its meaning: "In the Mahayana's terms" in Chapter 3 explains self-cherishing as it goes, and Mara appears beside the Devil. I checked: "bodhicitta" and "samsara" are in the preface and nowhere else.

The bad news is that the front door is built for the sangha. The preface's second paragraph tells a general reader, in so many words, that this is not their book: "This version is for friends who sit, and it assumes things a general reader might not grant". Then it sends them to a full book that, it says, only has one chapter finished. Then the introduction opens on "The mind is intrinsically compassionate. Evil is nothing but a mind turned away from itself." A practitioner will nod at that. A reader with no practice will be asked to sign for the conclusion before they've seen a single case, and the reader I'm thinking of, my sister-in-law, a nurse, sceptical of anything with "mind" in it, will close the book there. You can see from Chapter 1 that you know how to do better. Chapter 1's own first paragraph asks her for nothing but her attention.
