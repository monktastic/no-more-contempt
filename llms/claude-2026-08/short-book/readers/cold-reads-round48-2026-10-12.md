# Cold reads, round 48 (October 12)

*Five wise, analytical readers from different traditions, told that the round-47 revision (Fable's edits, uncommitted) came from Mahamudra practitioners and that Aditya holds the Devil, the conspiracy archetype and the mythic battle to be authentic perceptions; told also that they need not agree with him. Stage 1: the short book as revised (snapshot short-sent-round48.md, with Claude's two new paragraphs ending Chapter 1, before Aditya's later rewording of them). Stage 2: the appendices. Stage 3: all 46 round-47 edits, before and after (`edits.md` in the round-48 scratch folder), with RESTORE / KEEP / NEITHER per edit and a ranked list. Letters verbatim.*

## Samuel (66, Lutheran systematic theologian and former prison chaplain, Minnesota)

### Stage 1 (the book)

Dear Aditya,

Steady, with one stretch I devoured and two where I went slack. I read Chapter 1 from "The Encounter" to the end of "The Way Out" in one sitting, at the kitchen table, and forgot my coffee. My attention went slack in the "What I'm claiming" list in Chapter 2, which reads like a set of theses nailed to a door (I am a Lutheran; I know the genre, and I know it is better at starting fights than at changing anyone), and again in the last third of Chapter 3, from the locker room to the thousand-year tree. By then I had been told the mechanism several times and the book was widening the circle faster than it was deepening it. "The Picture I Use" woke me back up, and so did the end of Chapter 4.

A word on who is writing. I taught systematic theology for thirty years and was a chaplain at a state prison for twelve of them. Your preface says this version is for people with bodhicitta aspirations. I don't have those, not in your vocabulary, but I have spent a working life on the question of why people who know better do worse, and you are working on the same question. I read it as that.

**What happened in me**

I did the Limbo exercise honestly. After my wife died I drank more than I should have for about two years. I wanted to test your claim, the one in "The Corruption", that "in the absence of justifications, it's what I naturally desire." My first thought was that this was Romans 7, plain akrasia: "the good that I would, I do not." I knew it was wrong, I didn't need a story, and I did it anyway. That would be a counterexample. Then I actually remembered the evenings, and there was a story every time. *Just tonight. She'd understand. I've earned one thing.* Your exercise held, and I didn't expect it to. I still think Paul's chapter names something your account files too quickly under turning away, the knowing that cannot make itself act. But on my own evidence, the story was there.

The buttons. I have two people I hold in contempt, and I'll name both, since you asked for real cases.

The first is a former bishop of my synod who moved a pastor he knew was abusing boys, twice, and wrote a letter of recommendation the second time. I have met three of the men those boys became. One of them is dead. When I put the bishop in front of the two buttons, I didn't want vengeance exactly. I wanted the pill, but only after a public reading of the letter, with him standing there. Your Chapter 4 question caught me cleanly: "if the harm simply stopped tomorrow, with nobody punished and nobody exposed, would you feel relief, or robbed?" Robbed. Without hesitation. The harm did stop; he is retired and the pastor is dead. What I want now is not protection for anyone. It is for him to *know what he is*. That is your second heat in Chapter 4, almost word for word, and I have been calling it a passion for justice in sermons.

Then your Picture section went further than I wanted it to. You say these institutions and their visible evil "keep our own darkness located somewhere outside ourselves." In 1994, at a synod assembly, a youth worker said something to me over bad coffee about that pastor being "too hands-on with the confirmation boys." I said something like "he's always been a hugger," and I didn't follow up. I had a book manuscript due. I hadn't thought about that conversation in years until your sentence about the bishop's kind of evil giving us cover for our smaller betrayals. My contempt for the bishop is real, and it is also a place I have kept that coffee. I am not sure I would have seen that without the book. I am fairly sure I didn't want to.

The second is my nephew, who is twenty-nine and calls my faith "a coping mechanism," usually at Thanksgiving. I don't argue with him any more. I write him off politely: a smile, a question about his job, nothing behind it. Your passage on the cold kind ("a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them") describes my side of the table exactly. And the flat-Earth parenthesis in Chapter 3 caught me the way it was meant to: I have contempt for his contempt, and his is the same move as mine. He thinks I'm slow. I think he's shallow. Your sentence "Something in me is sensing who it's okay to look down on" stung, because I have been telling myself that with him I'm being patient. Your line about pity being "contempt with a soft face" took that alibi too.

What landed hardest was not about either of them. It was a man I'll call Ray, whom I sat with weekly for six years. He beat a man to death outside a bar. Early on he told me, "I saw his face and I kept going." I took that for years as the counterexample to everything you say: here is a man who did harm with the person in plain view. Your sentence in "The Way Out" is the one I would have pointed at him: "Not out of sight: a torturer has his victim in plain sight." What goes, you say, is not the body but "the face as a place somebody is looking out of." Around year four Ray said something different. He said that by the time he kept going, the man "wasn't a guy anymore, he was the whole night." I think that is your claim, and I think Ray found it himself, slowly, in a room with no windows.

**What I refuse, or half refuse**

"So all evil is innocent, in one exact sense." I understand the sense, and the paragraph defines it carefully. But the word will be read before the definition is, and in my world "innocent" means "needs no forgiveness." The thing you describe, a will that cannot see the moment it stopped seeing, my tradition calls *bound*. Luther's picture is a beast of burden that goes where its rider goes, and the rider is either God or the devil. That is your parasite, five hundred years early, and it doesn't need the word innocent. Captive is the word the book itself uses for the hostage. Use it.

Second, the sadist. Ray turned out to fit the book. Two other men I knew did not fit it so easily. Their whole pleasure required the other person to be very much *there*, inside, feeling it. You half answer this with "Love and contempt are addressed to the same place", and that answer is right: the cruelty is aimed at the somebody who is in there. But then "out of view" is the wrong picture for that case. It isn't that the person is out of view. The person is in view, hated for being there. That is Tillich's demonic: it needs the holy present in order to distort it, and it has more energy than a privation should. I'd like the book to say plainly that the worst evil keeps the person in view in order to attack what it sees. It strengthens your thesis rather than weakening it, because it is Chapter 1's own claim about where the signal lands.

Third, and this is the theologian talking: the book has no grace from outside, and its own best evidence is grace from outside. Near the end of Chapter 4 you say, "The only way to end it is by turning people back toward, and the only way to do that is by turning toward yourself." But the scene that converts the reader in Chapter 1 is the second stranger. Nobody turned toward themselves there. Someone *else* spoke a word that reached the part that knew, and it worked because it came from outside you. You have the whole structure of the external word in that scene and then you take it back at the end. I don't need you to call it grace. I need you not to contradict your own best page.

**The Devil, the conspiracy, the battle, the parasite**

The parasite: said with about the right force in Chapter 3's first paragraph, then deflated in the next. "A habit that lives by going unseen will push back when seen. That's all a habit is, and it doesn't need a tenant." I think that is too timid, and I think it's false to what you described. A habit doesn't move from one person to another through a look. Your own Chapter 1 has it tracking, aiming, steering around the other man's reactions, and leaping between people. That isn't a groove in attention; it's what Paul called the powers. I don't need a horned figure either. But "only a habit" undersells what I watched in a cellblock, where a single sentence from a guard could reorganize forty men's evening. Keep the warnings about the name. Drop the reassurance.

The Devil: in "The Picture I Use" you say the traditions that named an adversary "were seeing something real", and then you say "it isn't real, and it must be fought." That second phrase is sloppy, and I think you know better. Augustine never said evil isn't real. He said it has no being of its own, and that it is real the way a wound is real. Say that: it has no being of its own, and it must be fought. And then the 2 Corinthians quotation is the best thing in the section. "And no wonder, for even Satan masquerades as an angel of light." That is the oldest and most exact description of contempt I know, and it's better than anything around it. I would give it more room and the hedging less.

Is the Devil a true perception? Here is what I am sure of. In the prison, and in the church I served, there was something that coordinated harm across people who never coordinated: the guards, the inmates, the parole board, the chaplain who didn't follow up. It reliably produced one result, a darker place, and it reliably wore the clothes of duty. That perception is true. Whether there is a personal will behind it I hold open, and I think the book is right to hold it open. "It doesn't care who you hate, as long as you hate" is as good a one-line demonology as I've read.

The conspiracy archetype: the paragraph that closes "The Picture I Use" is right and well said. "What's being sensed is what supplies those rooms with people willing to sit in them, and supplies the rest of us with the certainty that we'd never be among them." That is exactly what the abuse in my church was. There was no cabal. There were twenty decent men who each kept a boy out of view for a slightly different reason, and one bishop who signed. But I have to say one thing you don't: the "evil global conspiracy" archetype has a body count. In its historical form it was aimed at Jews, and my own Luther fed it in the worst book he ever wrote. Your version is the cure for that, because it puts the conspiracy in everyone, the reader first. But a reader who arrives with the old archetype will hear the first half of your sentence and not the second. The book should say, in one sentence, that the moment the archetype gets a people attached to it, it has become the thing it was sensing. That isn't softening. It is your own test from Chapter 4: if it needs someone out of view, it's the parasite.

The battle: said, but quietly, and only at the end. "That's why the traditions could tell you the darkness is empty and tell you to give your life to the fight in the same breath." That's a fine last line. But "Nice is worse than useless against evil" is in Chapter 4, and the reader has had three chapters of mostly inward work before that arrives. A man who has read Bonhoeffer reads this book and wonders, the whole way through, whether it would have let him join the plot. Your answer is in there ("If the choice really were between stopping a harm with contempt and letting it happen, stop it."), and it's the right answer. Bonhoeffer would add that the one who acts against evil takes guilt on himself and doesn't pretend to clean hands. Your picture of fierceness with "no malice" is right as an aim, and dangerous as a self-description. You do say we almost always think we're doing the clean one. Say it louder.

**The end of Chapter 1**

Does it name the direction? Half. The creed paragraph is the best thing in the last pages: "It's the direction turned into a creed, and it fails for the simplest reason: the man in front of me was part of the life I said I served." That belongs exactly where it is. It is the Pharisee in one sentence, and it sets up everything Chapter 4 says about ideology.

The naming paragraph does not belong where it is, or not all of it. "If I had to name the direction, I'd call it being in service to all of life." All of life is too large for a chapter that has earned one face. The reader has just met two strangers and an enemy. Then come the sidewalk and "nothing turns out to be just stuff," which belong to the end of Chapter 3, where you do the tree and the arch. "I don't mean feeling guilty for stepping on the sidewalk" made me laugh, and I don't think it was meant to. I would say the direction is toward the one in front of you, and that it doesn't stop at him. Christians call that the neighbor, and the parable answers "who is my neighbor?" by refusing to set a limit. I'm not asking you to use my word. I'm saying the direction is named better by the face than by "life," because the face is what Chapter 1 has actually shown.

**What a tradition might miss**

My own first. Christianity's besetting sin is the one this book is about. We built a whole vocabulary for holy contempt: the reprobate, the world, the heretic. Your sentence "But contempt is the one we still praise as *goodness itself*, as what a good person is supposed to feel toward bad people" is a description of a great deal of preaching I have heard, and some I've given. Lutherans will resist the opening sentence, "The mind is intrinsically compassionate", because we were raised on the bondage of the will and total depravity. But your evidence for it, that remorse never runs backward over decency, is Romans 2: the law written on the heart, "their thoughts accusing or else excusing." Your part that knows and part that justifies are Paul's accusing and excusing thoughts. We have the evidence and refuse the conclusion, because we fear it makes grace unnecessary. It doesn't. It makes grace a recognition rather than an import.

As for contemplatives generally, Christian or Buddhist: they treat ignorance as a private condition, worked on alone on a cushion or in a cell. Your anatomy of how it passes between people through a look, organizes crowds, and leaves everyone sure they were fighting it is the thing they will skip. And they will be embarrassed by the adversary. The desert fathers weren't, but their modern heirs are, and that embarrassment is a kind of complicity your Picture section names correctly.

With respect, and with the bishop still on my mind,

Samuel Achterberg

### Stage 2 (the appendices)

Dear Aditya,

The appendices, briefly.

**What a reader of the book needed from here.** Two things, and both answer objections I made in my first letter.

First, the sadist. Appendix 1 says, "All malice hides one thing from the one doing it: the sameness of the target. This is true even in the sadist." Then Appendix 3 gives three marks for "fully seeing," and the first is the one that settles it for me: "their objection would land on you, and you'd have to answer it rather than manage it." I ran my two men against it. Both of them managed their victims superbly. Neither ever had to answer one. So they were not seeing the person, only the place where the person's suffering showed, and your account holds. The book needs one sentence of this in the body, near the torturer in "The Way Out." Without it, the reader who has sat in a room with real cruelty closes the book at that line and doesn't come back.

Second, the external word. Appendix 3, on pointing-out instruction: "Recognition isn't produced by thinking; it's introduced, by someone who is in it, in a way that isn't an argument." That is what I said the second stranger was, and it's what my tradition means by the word that comes from outside. The appendix has this right and Chapter 4 has it wrong. Its "the only way to do that is by turning toward yourself" has to give way to this, or at least make room for it.

**What's true.** "It's why malice is frightening and contempt is maddening." That's the cleanest single sentence in Appendix 1, and I've already used it, out loud, on a retired colleague. The chain in "Why it had to be this way" is good, and the honest admission in "Where this stops" is better than most theology I've read on the same point. So is the line in the Christian section, "Augustine keeps the will and the guilt where I keep innocence and the catching of yourself." That is exactly the disagreement, stated fairly.

The test at the end of section 1 I can report on, because I'm one of "the people others bring their worst to." It's half true of me. I sat with Ray for six years and no verdict formed. I cannot sit with the bishop for five minutes. So the clarity isn't general. It's local, and it fails exactly where my own failure is involved: the coffee in 1994. That supports your account, I think, more sharply than you put it. The verdict forms where I have something of my own to hide.

**What's overclaimed.** "I've never met anyone who was surprised by their own wrong when they finally saw it." I have, many times, and you should hear it before you print that. Men learn at sentencing, from a victim's statement, what their act did: the girl who can't sleep with the light off, the store the clerk's family lost. They weren't surprised that it was wrong. They were surprised at its size. Say that, and the sentence is true.

The Eckhart line. You tell the reader you can't place it in the sermons and then use it anyway as evidence. A theologian reading that stops trusting the paragraph. Cut it, or find it.

And the section where you say what you believe. Most of it I can sit beside: the lapse, the fear, forgiveness as one act. But here you lose me entirely: "The appearance of separate people at all, me here and him there, is what the first turning looks like from inside." That makes the other person's otherness the fall. My tradition calls the separateness of persons creation, and calls it good. The neighbor's being *not me* is the whole gift, and it's what makes love love and not self-regard. Your Chapter 2 builds everything on a face that is somebody else's. This sentence, taken seriously, makes that face a symptom. It's also the old Gnostic move (your epigraph comes from that shelf), and the Church fought it for good reason. It always ended in contempt for the body and the world, which is the thing your Chapter 3 says contempt for the world is. You mark the sentence as belief, and that's honest. But I'd want you to see that it may undo the book's own best page.

**What's missing.** The Christian section is Augustine, a misplaced Eckhart, and Baudelaire. Luther is missing, and he's the closest thing in my tradition to your account. He defined sin as the self curved in on itself, and the bound will ridden by God or the devil is your parasite and your "no chooser in the middle." Romans 2:15, the law written on the heart with its thoughts "accusing or else excusing," is your two parts. And Bonhoeffer above all. His *Ethics* opens by saying the first task of Christian ethics is to overturn the knowledge of good and evil, because that knowledge is itself the disunion: it's the Pharisee's knowledge, the one who judges at every moment. That is your part that proves, as theology, eighty years ago, written by a man who then acted against Hitler and didn't claim clean hands. Appendix 2 should have him, between Arendt and Murdoch. Tillich's demonic belongs in Appendix 3. It is the best Christian account I know of an absence with more energy than an absence should have.

Samuel

### Stage 3 (the round-47 edits)

Dear Aditya,

Now the edits. I'll say first what I expected and what I found. I expected that five Buddhist readers would have sanded off the Christian-sounding parts, and that I'd spend this letter defending them. A few edits are like that, and one of them is the most serious cut in the list. But most of the edits are what good readers do to any manuscript: they caught overclaims, fixed numbers, and made you say "I've found" where you'd written "always." Those were right, and I'd have asked for most of them myself. The trouble is a smaller group, in which the book stopped saying something it believes and started saying what a tradition would approve of. Those are the ones I'll spend time on.

**By number**

**1, 2, 3 (preface).** Skipped as trivial, except one note. "This is one way to see part of it" is fine; it's modest and true.

**4 (introduction).** NEITHER. The added clause, "That is how the traditions I practice in put it," hands your opening claim to someone else. I doubt the traditions put it that way; Mahamudra teachers, as far as I know, don't use the word "evil" at all. And a reader like me hears "that's what Buddhists say" and files it. The other half of the addition is the best thing in the paragraph: "the rest of this book is me checking it in the one place I can: my own case." Keep that. Restore the two flat sentences of the Before, and attach the checking clause to them in your own voice. Own the claim; say how you'll test it.

**5 (introduction).** RESTORE (cut the addition). "If you practice, you'll recognize the turning as what your tradition calls not recognizing" contradicts edit 43, which now says "What I've been calling the turning-away sits one step after *marigpa*." The book can't say both, and 43 is the more careful of the two. It also tells the reader what they'll find before they look. And it tells every reader who doesn't practice that the introduction was written for somebody else.

**6 (introduction).** RESTORE in part. Two losses. The phrase "uniquely insidious" became "an insidious," but your own Chapter 3 earns "uniquely": contempt is the one wrong "we still praise as *goodness itself*." That's what makes it unique, so say so. The bigger loss is the cut paragraph that began "One consequence will matter to readers who trust reasoning above all, as I tend to". That paragraph announced the book's second great claim, that you can't calculate your way to goodness. Bonhoeffer built his *Ethics* on the same claim, and for a Western reader it's the one with the most bite. Contemplatives may not need to be told it, but almost everyone else does, and nothing in the introduction now promises it. Put it back, shortened if you like.

**7 (Chapter 1).** KEEP. "Plain malice would frighten you. This maddens, because it won't say what it is." That's exactly right, and it's the sentence that would have kept me from my sadist objection if I'd taken it seriously the first time.

**8 (Chapter 1).** RESTORE. This is the plainest case of doctrine overruling the page. The Before:

> What you hid it from is what you call "I" when nothing is being hidden.

The paragraph just above it has walked the reader through the grammar: you hid it from *yourself*. The Before closes that loop. The answer to "hidden from whom?" is *you*, the one you've always meant by "I." The After answers with "what's left when the hiding stops", which is vaguer, and then adds "or a self you could own", a disclaimer that belongs to a doctrine of no-self and has nothing to do with the reader's memory. I'm in no position to settle what the self is, and the book shouldn't try to settle it here either. But the reader's experience says "I got myself back," and the Before honored that. My tradition would say the same thing: the true self is the one God addresses. Yours may disagree. The page shouldn't take sides by inserting a doctrine into a moment of recognition.

**9 (Chapter 1).** KEEP. The forgotten birthday against the cutting remark is a good, plain check.

**10, 11, 15, 18, 19, 21, 28, 29, 32, 35 (wording, cross-references, "person" to "stranger").** Skipped as trivial; all KEEP.

**12 (Chapter 2).** KEEP, with one thing I'd add elsewhere. "to wield contempt, we have to already know that it's evil" is better than "evil in miniature," and the added "Its explicit message denies equality. Its successful operation presupposes it." is the best compression of the book anywhere. Cutting "originally titled *Contempt Is Evil*" was right; that's bookkeeping. But it took with it the only place the book said those three words flat. Somewhere, say them. Contempt is evil. It's your thesis, and it's the sentence a practitioner of any tradition will flinch at, which is why it should be on the page.

**13 (Chapter 2).** RESTORE. "This is not a call to be nice." Three edits (13, 17 and 23) each removed a sentence saying that turning toward is not softness. Taken one at a time, each looks like a trim. Taken together they look like a tradition's preference for gentle speech quietly removing the book's guard against being read as gentle. Chapter 2 is the first place "remaining unflinchingly *turned toward* them" meets an enemy, and it's exactly where a Christian reader hears "turn the other cheek" in its weakest sense. I heard it there. I'd restore this one and accept the cuts in 17 and 23, where "the harshest thing in the room" and "someone fierce" already do the work.

**14 (Chapter 2).** RESTORE. This one changed a claim into a definition. The Before, "I haven't found a second kind," was a finding that could be wrong, and invited me to look. The After, "since that's what the word means here, there's no second kind to find," wins by stipulation. I spent a paragraph of my first letter trying to break that claim with two men I knew, and the appendix's three marks answered me. That exchange happened only because the claim could be wrong. A definition can't be argued with, and it's the reasoning mind's move: certify the conclusion by owning the terms. The book warns against exactly that.

**16 (Chapter 2).** RESTORE the heading; NEITHER on Hitler. "Compassion is not wisdom" became "Caring is not knowing what to do," probably because "wisdom" means something technical in Buddhism. But Chapter 4 still says, "That check tells you only about the compassion, not the wisdom, which Chapter 2 said are different things." Chapter 2 no longer says it in those words. Restore the heading, and keep the parenthetical that warned off the technical sense. It was one line and it served both readers. On Hitler: "He's the perfect example of the opposite" went to "I think he's an example of the opposite." "Perfect" was too much, but "I think" is timid about the one case where the book should be surest. Say "He's an example of the opposite."

**17 (Chapter 2).** KEEP. "The only thing that has ever turned anyone back" was a claim no one can make. I watched men turn back after a stroke, after a daughter was born, in AA. Most of those turnings had someone in them who stayed turned toward the man. Not all, so the hedge is right.

**20 (Chapter 3).** RESTORE (cut the added sentence); KEEP "by comparison." "The traditions that looked at this longest say there's no account to give, and that the first not-recognizing was nobody's act." That's one tradition's answer presented as the verdict of all who looked longest. Mine looked at it for sixteen hundred years and said something subtler. Augustine says the evil will has no efficient cause, only a deficient one, and that looking for its cause is like trying to see darkness or hear silence. So he agrees there's no account to give, and he still calls the first turning an act. Your bare "I cannot give an account of why the first turn happens" was honest and stronger. If you want a pointer, point to both: Samantabhadra and Augustine, who agree there's no cause and disagree about whether it was anybody's act. "By comparison" is plainer than "in the *relative*," so keep that.

**22 (Chapter 3).** KEEP. "the only way out I've found" is the book's method speaking.

**23 (Chapter 3).** KEEP. "that's Stockholm syndrome" was a clinical label where the hostage image already does the work.

**24 (Chapter 3).** KEEP both cuts, with one wish. "You, of all people, deserve to know that" presumed a great deal of a reader who was hurt. "It's among the most effective forces in the history of people facing power they couldn't match" was too broad. Nonviolent refusal did not stop the regime Bonhoeffer died resisting, and he knew it. "A long record" is truer. But "my reading of why is the reason the second stranger worked on you" is a clumsy sentence. Fix it while you're there.

**25 (Chapter 3).** KEEP.

**26 (footnote).** KEEP, and I'd keep it if every other edit were reversed. "for those who dare" is an invitation I would never give a man in his first year inside. "if you have a teacher, do it with them" and the instruction to stop if it stays with you are what a chaplain would say. This edit was plainly right on its own terms.

**27 ("The Picture I Use", conspiracy).** RESTORE the order and the lost sentence, and add one. The Before led with the archetype and said plainly, "People are sensing something genuine." The After leads with a disclaimer, "Real conspiracies exist," then edges toward the archetype, and the plain sentence is gone. The reorder reads like a writer afraid of sounding like a crank. You have nothing to be afraid of here, because your version is the only one I know that turns the archetype back on the person holding it. Put the plain sentence back, first. Then add the sentence I asked for in my first letter: the moment the archetype gets a people attached to it, it has become the thing it was sensing. That isn't a softening. It's the one honest caution this archetype requires, because its history has a body count.

**30 (Chapter 4).** RESTORE "the covering was one thing" without the inserted clause. "And the covering, the part of it this book is about, was one thing" reads like a footnote stitched into the sentence to satisfy a tradition that counts its obscurations. If the book believes the covering is one thing, say so. Luther thought every sin grew from one root; I think you've found the same root from another side. If the book doesn't believe it, cut the sentence. The hedge in the middle of it is the worst of both.

**31 (Chapter 4).** RESTORE. This is the edit that matters most in the whole list. The cut paragraph:

> So yes: there is a battle between good and evil, between light and darkness, and it is the one the myths were about. It is fought in your every thought, word, and deed, and nothing you do is outside it.

"Thought, word, and deed" is the language of the general confession. I've said it every Sunday of my life: we have sinned in thought, word, and deed, by what we have done and by what we have left undone. The paragraph puts the battle exactly where the confession puts sin, and then it does the thing that makes it safe: "But the moment malice, contempt, or self-righteousness enter the picture, that is no longer fighting it. It is assisting it." It is the best paragraph in the book. It says the old myths were about something real, and in the same breath it takes the weapon out of the hand that would use the myth on a neighbor. Without it the book ends on "looking," which a contemplative will hear as a description of their practice, and stops there. With it, the reader is told they are in a war and told how not to change sides while fighting it. My guess is that this was cut because nondual readers dislike "good *versus* evil." But your next paragraph already answers them: there was never a second thing there, only the light turned away. A battle against an absence is still a battle. Ask any alcoholic. The new last line ("tell you the darkness is empty and tell you to give your life to the fight") is clearer than "give your life to it", which could be read as giving your life to the darkness. Keep that change, and restore the paragraph above it.

**33, 34 (Appendix 1).** KEEP. Narrowing "anyone who practices" is accurate; most Christians practice without being taught a "nature of mind." And "nobody I've found" is the right hedge. I've met men surprised by the size of what they did, as I said in my second letter.

**36 (Appendix 1).** KEEP. The reordering is fine. My objection to "The appearance of separate people at all..." stands, and no edit touched it.

**37 (Appendix 1 footnote).** KEEP, and it's braver than the Before. "Done knowingly, with a story, that is the same turn" extends the turning to people who avoid the dying out of grief. I have done that. I stayed away from a parishioner's bedside for three weeks in 2009 and told myself his family needed privacy. It was the same turn. Most readers will flinch at that sentence, and it's true.

### Stage 1 (the book)

Dear Aditya,

Steady, with a stretch I devoured. Chapter 1 had me from the limbo questions to the end of "The Corruption"; I read it twice, the second time slowly, on the cushion, as you suggest. My attention went slack in three places: the list in "What I'm claiming" in Chapter 2, which reads like a syllabus after a chapter that read like a confession; the middle of Chapter 3 between the flat-Earthers and the locker room, where the examples began to feel collected rather than found; and the run of checks in Chapter 4, which are each good but arrive like a set of exercises at the back of a textbook. "The Picture I Use" woke me up again, and so did the last page.

**What happened in me**

I did the buttons with the abbot. You won't know the story and I won't tell all of it: a teacher in my lineage, in whose monastery I spent some of my best years, who used "crazy wisdom" as a cover for sleeping with students and humiliating the ones who objected. When I put his face in front of the two buttons, my hand went to the first one before I had finished reading the sentence. Not for long, but it went. And your Chapter 4 check — "So the check, which is Chapter 1's pill in a form you can use daily: if the harm simply stopped tomorrow, with nobody punished and nobody exposed, would you feel relief, or robbed?" — produced "robbed," cleanly, at once.

I want to be careful here, because I think the check is half right. Some of the "robbed" was the thing you describe. I caught it: a small hunger to see him on his knees in front of the sangha, which had nothing to do with the women he hurt and everything to do with the years I spent defending him. That is mine, and I'm grateful to have been shown it. But some of the "robbed" was not that. In an institution that protected an abuser, exposure is not punishment; it is the only thing that protects the next student. "Nobody exposed" smuggles truth-telling in with vengeance. Your note for people who were hurt says accountability is owed, all of it, and I agree; but the daily check, as worded, would make a woman who wants the truth told feel she has caught a parasite in herself. Separate the two. Ask whether you'd feel robbed if the truth were known and he simply stopped, with no punishment and no humiliation. That I can answer honestly, and the answer is less flattering than "robbed" and more useful.

The thing that actually landed, and I'm still sore from it, was your sentence in the Introduction: "Every old picture of it grants it either too much substance or too little, and both mistakes feed it: the first breeds contempt, the second complicity." That is the history of my sangha in one line. For years we practised "pure view" about him — everything the guru does is the activity of wisdom — which is the "too little" mistake made into a doctrine, and it kept his victims out of view as surely as any hood. Then, when it broke, the same community turned on him with a ferocity that had nothing in it but the verdict, and some of the most ferocious were the ones who had been most devoted. Complicity, then contempt. Your book is the first thing I've read that explains why it was the same people.

My brother was harder, and quieter. He thinks my three years in retreat were a midlife crisis with incense. I don't feel hot contempt for him; I feel the cold kind. I have filed him. And when I read, in Chapter 4, "And pity, which passes for compassion and is contempt with a soft face: it needs them to stay below me," I recognised my own face at our last Christmas dinner: the patient smile of a meditation teacher listening to a man who hasn't sat. Your question — "would I be glad if they got there before I did?" — I can't answer yes. That is the most useful thing the book did for me.

What I refused. Two things.

First, the argument in Chapter 2: "If awareness were neutral between care and harm, why would harming someone require turning away from it?" As philosophy this assumes its conclusion, since the premise that harm requires turning away is what's at issue. As phenomenology — try it, see if the picture holds — it's better, and the picture didn't hold for me. But the hard case is the sadist, and you never face him squarely. The sadist doesn't put the person out of view; he needs the person in view, in order to degrade the one who is there. Your Chapter 1 actually has the better account of this — "I am speaking directly to the part of my enemy that I claim doesn't exist—his better nature—and trying to extinguish it" — and it doesn't sit easily with Chapter 2's language of something going "out of view." Limbo can hold both, I think: the person is seen and refused at once. But say so, because "out of view" is the weaker description and the sadist is the reader's first counterexample.

Second, the claim in Chapter 2 that "Every decision made while the people it touches are held out of view belongs to it, and since that's what the word means here, there's no second kind to find." "Every decision" is too much. I decide how to fund a meditation centre's roof without the faces of all the people it touches in view. You have the airport passage to protect yourself, and you should use it here. And "since that's what the word means here" is a stipulation dressed as a discovery. You need it to be a discovery, and the check you offer — does the definition fit the cases you'd already use the word for — is the right one. Lead with the check, not the stipulation.

**The Devil, the conspiracy, the battle, the parasite**

Too timidly, on the whole, and the timidity is worst exactly where the claim is strongest.

The parasite. Chapter 3 gives it a name and then spends three paragraphs taking it back. "I'll call it that from here, because the word fits the behavior, and for no other reason." Then: "If the word makes you picture a creature, drop the picture and keep the behavior: a groove in attention so old that its shape looks like intent." Then a paragraph of drawbacks. But two paragraphs earlier you wrote that it "pursued a goal you would never choose, a darker world, and while it had you it felt entirely like you." A groove doesn't pursue a darker world. You can't have both. Either the deflation is right, and the Picture's claim that separate parasites benefit from the same thing is a manner of speaking; or the teleology is real, and "a habit ... doesn't need a tenant" undersells it.

The honest middle, which I'd have given you in a seminar, is this. You can take what Dennett called the intentional stance toward a system without claiming there's a little man inside it, and the stance is warranted when it predicts better than any other description. Your parasite predicts. It predicts that the stories change and the conclusion doesn't; that it gets stronger when you come close; that it will find the one place I'm right and use that as the hook. Those are true predictions. That gives it the reality it needs. And my own tradition has a word for exactly that status: conventionally real, ultimately empty. Madhyamaka never said empty things don't function; it said the opposite. So you don't need to apologise for the parasite to a Buddhist audience. You need to tell them it has precisely the kind of existence they already grant to everything else, including themselves, and that they're using "empty" to get out of fighting it.

The Devil. The Picture says the right things and then hedges them in the italic note above it: "None of the checks in this book depend on it, and if it doesn't fit, leave it; the rest stands without it." I'd question whether that's true. The best sentence in the book about the Devil is this one: "It doesn't care who you hate, as long as you hate." That is Girard's Satan almost word for word — the mimetic process that casts out Satan by Satan — and it isn't an optional picture. It is a description of how rivalry propagates, and it follows from your Chapter 1. So I'd say: the Devil as a coordinating tendency with no centre, aimed at one thing (that both sides harden), is a true perception, and you should present it as the consequence it is. What is not a true perception, and you rightly avoid, is a Devil with a plan, a will of its own, a tenant. "So there's a paradox to hold with both hands: it isn't real, and it must be fought." I'd change "isn't real" to "has nothing of its own." "Isn't real" lets the Buddhist reader off.

You also left out the verse that says what you're saying better than anything else in Paul: that we wrestle not against flesh and blood, but against principalities and powers. That is the book's thesis, in the tradition that named the Devil: the battle is real, and the enemy is not the person.

The conspiracy archetype. Said well and too briefly. "What's being sensed is what supplies those rooms with people willing to sit in them, and supplies the rest of us with the certainty that we'd never be among them." That is true, and the second half is the bit nobody says. But you leave out the one thing a reader needs here, and it is your own mechanism: the conspiracy archetype's most famous use in history is the scapegoat. The perception is real — coordination without a coordinator — and the moment it gets a face, a people, a cabal, it becomes the purest case of the thing the book is about. If you say that, you earn the right to say the perception is real. If you don't, a careful reader will think you haven't noticed where it has gone before.

The battle. The last page is the strongest thing in the book: "That's why the traditions could tell you the darkness is empty and tell you to give your life to the fight in the same breath." That sentence is true, and I'd stand behind it in front of my own teachers. But it comes on the last page, and before it the battle mostly appears as practice — catching yourself, looking at the contempt. A reader could finish the book thinking the battle is a metaphor for mindfulness. "Nice is worse than useless against evil" is in Chapter 4, which is right, but it's one line. The battle should be stated earlier and plainly, as a claim: there is a real fight, it has stakes in the world, it is lost by people who think they are winning it, and it is won by people who won't add the verdict.

**The end of Chapter 1**

"Direction" is the right word, and the paragraph that introduces it is one of the best in the book. The hijacking paragraph is better still: "It's the direction turned into a creed, and it fails for the simplest reason: the man in front of me was part of the life I said I served." That belongs, because it closes the loop with the first stranger, who believed he was serving something.

Does "being in service to all of life" name it? Half. It names the scope, and that matters. But it sounds like a charity's mission statement, and it jumps ahead of the reader. Then the sidewalk: "That's easy to hear wrong. I don't mean feeling guilty for stepping on the sidewalk." In Chapter 1, with the reader still raw from the encounter, this is a swerve into a topic they haven't raised, and it reads as a joke at the worst moment. The thousand-year tree in Chapter 3 does that work properly. Cut the sidewalk here.

And I'd quarrel with one clause: "the second stranger wasn't exactly serving you." I think he was serving me, the me I'd turned away from, against my wishes. That's what made his harshness kind. If you say he wasn't serving me, you lose the bridge from the direction back to the person. There's also a small tension with Chapter 4, where the light is the thing you "can't define." Naming the direction in Chapter 1 and then saying it can't be defined in Chapter 4 is fine if the Chapter 1 name is offered as a pointer — "if I had to name" does that — but don't let it harden.

**What the book sees that a tradition might miss or resist**

Three things.

First, that ignorance travels between people, by gaze, and that it has an anatomy. Buddhism has an exquisite account of avidya in one mindstream and very little on how my not-recognising recruits yours. Lojong points at it — drive all blames into one — but as a practice for the individual. Your Chapter 1 is a two-person phenomenology of ignorance, and I don't know its equal in the commentarial literature.

Second, that confusion behaves like an adversary. My tradition's habit is to say evil is "only confusion," and the "only" does the damage. Confusion that aims, defends itself and gets stronger when exposed is not the same thing as the fog people picture when they hear the word. The old Tibetans knew this: the four maras, Milarepa's demons in the cave, Chöd. It's the Western convert version, my version, that flinches. And the flinch is not neutral. It is exactly what let "crazy wisdom" stand for twenty years in my old community: everything is the play of mind, so nothing is an enemy, so nobody is stopped.

Third, spiritual contempt: the patient smile, the friend who "isn't there yet." Every sangha I know runs on it, and every sangha calls it compassion. You name it plainly. Keep it.

Where I'd push back on you: your Introduction concedes the whole frame to the traditions in its first two sentences — "The mind is intrinsically compassionate, and evil is nothing but a mind turned away from itself." — and then the Devil arrives later as though it were a departure from that frame. It isn't. "Nothing but" is the tradition's reductive phrasing, and it's the very phrase your Picture has to fight. I'd let the Introduction say the absence behaves like a presence in the same breath as it says it's an absence. You do say it, three paragraphs later. Bring it up.

### Stage 2 (the appendices)

Dear Aditya,

The appendices, more briefly.

**What's true.** Appendix 1's distinction between malice and contempt is the best thing in all three: "It's why malice is frightening and contempt is maddening." I've watched it in survivor testimony. Survivors describe the SS man who simply shot people with a kind of numb horror. But what they come back to, decades later, voices shaking, is the guard who made a show of not seeing them while making sure they saw him not seeing. The corpse example is also exactly right: "desecrating an enemy's corpse does the dead man no damage, and everyone knows it's wrong". Every tradition of burial knows it, and Antigone knew it.

The best line in Appendix 3 is an admission: "Augustine keeps the will and the guilt where I keep innocence and the catching of yourself." The reader of the first file needed that sentence next to "all evil is innocent." In the book, innocence is put forward as a finding. Here you admit it's a choice that parts company with the tradition. Put the admission in the book, or soften the claim.

**What's overclaimed.** "If it holds, it's the thing all of the above are downstream of." No. It may be the thing they all missed, which is a real contribution, but missing something is not being downstream of it. Girard's crowd is not just your pair multiplied.

The sadist test is honest, and I respect that you invite counterexamples. But look at your first mark: "their objection would land on you, and you'd have to answer it rather than manage it". A sadist, by definition, manages it; that is part of the pleasure. So the mark rules out the case before you look. I've read the camp testimony about guards who enjoyed it. My honest finding is that they needed the victim's inner life to be there, and needed it not to count. Your theory can take that in; it's turning away of a strange, attentive kind. But say that the test is partly a definition, or a careful reader will say it for you.

Then the metaphysics: "The appearance of separate people at all, me here and him there, is what the first turning looks like from inside." Here I get off. You've marked it as belief, and fairly. But you should know what it does in a reader like me. If separate persons are what error looks like, then the six million were an appearance, and so were the eight hundred thousand. You don't mean that. But the sentence allows it, and the whole moral force of your first four chapters depends on the person in front of you being *real*, "a place somebody is looking out of." My tradition holds that persons are not the fall; persons are what love is for. I would not let this sentence stand unguarded in a book that asks people to look into an enemy's face.

**What's missing.** Three things, and the first two matter to me personally.

First, Judaism, which is absent from Appendix 3, though it says several of your central claims more sharply than anything you cite. The Talmud has Resh Lakish saying that Satan, the evil inclination and the angel of death are one. That is your unification of the Devil with the turning, said seventeen hundred years ago, and kept inside the person. Bava Metzia says that whoever whitens his fellow's face in public is like one who sheds blood. That is your "the look is the deed," as law. Sotah has Resh Lakish again: a person does not sin unless a spirit of folly enters him. That is limbo. And Berakhot has Beruriah correcting her husband Rabbi Meir, who was praying for the death of some thugs who tormented him. Pray that the sins cease, she told him, not the sinners. That is your "relief, or robbed" question, answered by a woman in the second century. It belongs in your book more than A Course in Miracles does.

Second, the Christian section is thin, and in one place wrong. You lean on a line of Eckhart's you can't place, and you conclude "there's no creature in it, only a direction." That is not the Christian tradition; it is a modern reading of one mystic. The Fathers hold that evil has no being (Dionysius says so flatly, Athanasius before him) *and* that the devil is a creature, good in nature, turned in will. That isn't a contradiction you need to resolve for them. It is your own paradox, held more carefully than you hold it. And the Philokalia's stages of temptation (provocation, coupling, consent, captivity, passion) are the nearest correspondence in any tradition to your limbo and your "one choice." The Fathers' word for catching it faster is *nepsis*, watchfulness. You use Augustine because he's the famous one. The Greek East is far closer to you, and it would cost you nothing to look.

Third, from my own field. Appendix 2 lists Browning in a bibliography and never mentions Primo Levi or Gitta Sereny. Levi's grey zone is the most important description of how turning away passes from oppressor to victim; it's your "license," lived. And Sereny asked Stangl why, if they were going to kill them anyway, the prisoners had to be humiliated first. He said, in substance, that it was to condition the men who had to do it, to make it possible. That is your Chapter 2 sentence about the hood and the number, stated by the man who ran Treblinka. Solzhenitsyn's line, that the line between good and evil runs through every human heart, is also absent. A reader will expect it.

**What the reader of the first file needed.** The admission about Augustine. The sadist test, or something like it, in the book, because the book's claim that harm can't be done in full view is the one I tested hardest against my archive. And some version of Resh Lakish. It would let a reader who is allergic to the Devil see that one ancient tradition already named him as your thing, without granting him a body.

Hannah

Dear Aditya,

Now the edits. I'll start with the one that changed how I read everything else.

In my first letter I said the battle between good and evil is said "only at the very end," and that it shouldn't have to wait that long. Then I reached edit 31 and found that you had said it outright, and that it was cut:

That is the best paragraph in the book, and its removal left a mark you can see in the text. The next paragraph now begins "Out there, fighting it means force if it takes force," and "it" has nothing to refer to. The antecedent was the battle. When a cut leaves a pronoun pointing at nothing, the cut was wrong.

So I came to this list suspicious. Some of the suspicion was earned. Much of it wasn't: most of these edits are honest corrections, and several are improvements I'd fight for. Here they are by number. I skipped 15, 18, 19, 21, 28 and 29 as trivial (wording and "person" to "stranger").
