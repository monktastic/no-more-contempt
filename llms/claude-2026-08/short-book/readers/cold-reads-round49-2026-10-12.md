**The exercises, briefly.**

My wrong: in the last month of my husband's illness I told my sister I couldn't take her calls because the hospice nurses needed the line. They didn't. I didn't want her voice in the house. I knew it at the time; I let myself know it a year later. Your description of limbo is accurate to the hour.

### Stage 2 (the earlier version)

Dear Aditya,

My first letter was sealed before your broader questions reached me. So here is the rest of what I have to say about the newer book, before I turn to the earlier one.

**What happened in me.** Limbo brought back my sister and the hospice phone, as I said. The Encounter brought back Thanksgiving. My nephew said "your nuns" over the pie, and I gave him a look. He said "What?" and I said "Nothing." That "nothing" was your plausible deniability, delivered over pie. He has been ruder to me since, and I've counted it as evidence. The beavers took me further than I expected. Three dinner tables this autumn learned from me that my neighbour is the kind of man who would. Then the relief-or-robbed check showed me I'd be robbed if he simply stopped. I didn't sleep well that night, and I'm glad of it.

**Bought, bought tentatively, refused.** I bought Limbo whole. I bought the remorse check: I have never once felt remorse over a decency, and I tried. I bought the two strangers. I bought "Love and contempt are addressed to the same place." I bought the one real choice in Chapter 3, the sigh, and the "extra." Those all rest on things I checked, and they held.

I bought tentatively the claim that the person at the far end knows more, not less. I ran your small-wrong, big-wrong test, and it held for me. But stretching it to the worst people rests on a mushroom vision, and to your credit the footnote says so. I bought tentatively "The mind is intrinsically compassionate." Julian of Norwich says there is a godly will in us that never consented to sin, and that is the same claim in my own tradition's words. I'd like you to know she's on your side. But your page asserts it, and the book earns it only later, partly.

My first refusal came at "Nobody ever looked honestly at something in themselves while being told they were worthless for having it." I've read too many desert fathers, and known too many old nuns formed under harsh superiors, to take "nobody ever." Some people did look honestly under contempt. It was rare and it cost them, but they did. Say "almost nobody," or say it of yourself.

The firm refusal came in "What I'm claiming": "Every decision made while the people it touches are held out of view belongs to it, and I haven't found a second kind." I chose the cheaper heating oil this morning with every person it touches out of view. Your airport paragraph knows the difference between background and turning away. This sentence forgets it, and it makes every purchase evil.

I also refused "And life, left to itself, wants to serve life." I watched a heron take a brook trout last week. Life left to itself eats life. What you mean is narrower: the life that looks out of a face, recognized. Say that.

**Earned and asserted.** Earned by what I checked: limbo, the remorse asymmetry, the second stranger, contempt aimed at the better nature, the choice, the far end's knowing, and relief or robbed. Asserted:

- "The simplest explanation is that care belongs to awareness itself." It's plausible, and it's offered as the simplest explanation without the others being weighed.
- "The darkness leaks out of me and empowers his, because its meaning is universal."
- "Badness is the result of turning away, not its cause." It's argued, never checked.
- Everything at scale, especially the institutions: "It needs them in power".
- In the Picture: "I think they were all looking at one thing". Wetiko, Satan and Fromm's love of death are not obviously one thing, and the sentence flattens three traditions to make the point.
- **"There is only one guide you can trust."** I've lived under a Rule for thirty years because the solitary conscience, however turned toward, deceives itself. So do I. Benedict gives the abbot discernment and gives the monks each other, and the book half-knows this ("a contract, a precept, a grievance procedure exist because people who were sure they had us fully in view didn't"). But the bold claim outruns it. So does "certainty is a mark of the other thing". The saints were often certain.

**Hand-offs.** Chapter 1 to Chapter 2 is the best seam in the book. It ends on a shared ground we both stand on, and Chapter 2 opens on a face. Chapter 2 to Chapter 3 breaks, because "What I'm claiming" sits between them like a filing cabinet. Chapter 3's question ("why do they turn away in the first place?") follows from Chapter 1, not from the list. Chapter 3 to Chapter 4 is broken by the Picture. Chapter 3 ends "So the question isn't whether you're in this. It's what to do from inside it." That's a perfect hand-off to Chapter 4. Then the Picture intervenes. Then Chapter 4 opens by reaching back two chapters: "Chapter 2 claimed there is one guide". Move the Picture after Chapter 4, as an afterword, and both seams close.

**Where it ended for me.** At "Somewhere, somebody you'll never meet is about to give up on the part of them that knows." That's where I put the book down and went out to the brook. The book's actual last sentence is good, perhaps the right one: "That's why the traditions could tell you the darkness is empty and tell you to give your life to the fight in the same breath." But it comes after a sentence that doesn't parse and five other endings. End nearer to it.

**Wrong, overclaimed, or misleading, from my seat.**

- "Nice is worse than useless against evil." Benedict tells us to receive every guest as Christ. Ordinary courtesy is how most of us keep the stranger in view on an ordinary day. You mean false niceness, so say false.
- "But contempt is the one we still praise as *goodness itself*". A Christian reader will at once hear "You brood of vipers," and the tables overturned. The book quotes Paul on Satan but never faces the Gospels' own harsh speech. You needn't rebut anything. But a Christian reader will wonder whether Jesus was the first stranger or the second, and the book would be stronger for having thought about it.
- "The plastic in the blood of every child now alive." A factual claim with no source, in a register the book hasn't used before. It reads like a headline.
- Appendix 3 is promised five times. I don't have it, and a reader of this version won't either.
- The Preface's "plant medicines" and the footnote's mushrooms are handled honestly. I note it only because a Christian reader will flinch, and the footnote is what lets them stay.

## What landed

The buttons. Without having to think, I know whose face I put there. It was the politician who dressed his campaign in the yellow star to frighten people into voting for him. I've taught that story perhaps two hundred times and never noticed I was Rabbi Meir in it.

The sigh. My son-in-law calls my work "fairy tales," and I have a sigh for him. Your paragraph described it to the second: "For one second the verdict flashes through my eyes, and I make sure they see it, and then I go back to loving them as if nothing had happened." My daughter has seen it. I know she has. That's the one that will cost me sleep, and it should.

Relief or robbed: if the politician simply quit tomorrow, nobody exposing him, I would be robbed. I'd want the reckoning. That is a finding, and it's an unpleasant one.

What changes something for me: the choice in Chapter 3. That the choosing happens while you can still feel both pulls, and after that there are only reasons that feel like judgment. It's the best account of the moment before a sin that I've read outside the Tanya, and plainer than the Tanya.

What doesn't: "The Picture I Use." I don't object to the Devil. I object to the parade of witnesses. A storyteller who brings five authorities is afraid the story won't hold. Yours holds.

## What happened in me (the newer book)

**Limbo.** The wrong I recalled is from my thirties. I published a translation of one of Rabbi Nachman's tales whose best reading wasn't mine. It came from an older colleague, dead by then, who had worked it out at a Shabbos table years before. I thanked him in the acknowledgments in a line that told nobody what he'd given me. Your question, "The knowledge that it was wrong: did it feel like new information?", answered itself. No. I knew while I was typing the acknowledgments, and I made the line smaller on purpose. And yes, when a student once asked me where that reading came from, I changed the subject, and part of me watched myself change it.

**The second stranger.** I had one. In yeshiva I was mocking a slow boy's reading of a Rashi, and my rebbe said in front of everyone, "Eliezer, that's beneath you." It was harsher than anything a scold could have said, and I didn't dig in. I went red and went to sit with the boy. Your chapter described that afternoon better than I've ever managed to, and I have told it from a pulpit.

**The buttons.** I told you about these already: the politician with the yellow star on his posters, and the first button. "Relief, or robbed": robbed.

**The face.** I tried it with a stranger on the bus and it came, slowly. Then I put the politician's face there, and you're right: "the story is there before the face is." I saw the poster before I saw a man.

**The tree and the arch.** There was a carob, very old, on a corner in Nachlaot. They cut it for a parking space. It landed in the same place a cruelty to a person lands. Not as strongly, but in the same place. That check worked on me, and I didn't expect it to.

**The choice.** I tried "hold someone's eyes and decide, while holding them, to stop seeing them" with my son-in-law, at the Shabbos table, when he said "fairy tales" again. I couldn't find the moment. The sigh was already on its way out when I noticed it. That is the most useful thing I take from the book.

**Contempt for the not-yet-free.** "The beginner's question, heard with a patient smile." That's me with every student who asks me to tell them the moral of a story. I've built forty years of teaching around that smile.

## Bought, tentatively bought, refused

**Bought outright:** limbo (that the knowledge wasn't new); the two strangers; "Love and contempt are addressed to the same place"; that harm needs a story and decency doesn't; the grudge as upkeep; cutting in line; the choice in Chapter 3; the hatred of harm versus the second heat; relief or robbed; the one-word examples. Each of these I checked in myself, and each held.

**Bought tentatively:** "The simplest explanation is that care belongs to awareness itself." The face exercise moved me toward it, but "simplest explanation" is an argument from tidiness, and it's carrying the whole metaphysics. Also the far end: "The most lost person is carrying *more* of that knowledge than you are, not less". In my own case the big wrong did take longer to admit than the small one, so I bought the first half. The step from there to the worst man alive rests, by your own account, on mushrooms. You're honest about that, and that's why I give it "tentatively" and not "no."

**First refusal:** "**There is one kind of evil, and this is it.**" (Chapter 2's list.) It's the first place I said no out loud. My tradition doesn't let me make evil that simple. The Midrash reads "very good" in Genesis as including the *yetzer hara*, because without it no one would build a house, marry or have children. The appetite that becomes evil is also the appetite for life. Your Picture calls it "It is anti-life." The rabbis would say it's life without a master. Those are different diagnoses, and they prescribe different cures. Yours is to look until it dissolves. Theirs is to harness it. I think you've seen something true about contempt, and then made it the shape of all evil.

**Refused:** "In an important sense, nobody's there." And then: "But the judgement that *someone in there is choosing this* is aimed at a person who, for that moment, isn't home. It's incoherent." In Chapter 1 you wrote that love and contempt "both say it to the somebody who is." The somebody is there or he isn't. Chapter 1 is right, and Chapter 2 contradicts it. Telling me nobody's home is another way of not seeing him, a gentler hood. The Torah holds the sinner present and answerable, and that's exactly why teshuvah is possible. Your Chapter 3, about where responsibility lives, says the same thing better. Cut "nobody's there."

**Refused:** "I can't find a hot atrocity that didn't begin there." Some began in greed, some in fear and some in plain obedience, with the contempt arriving afterward to make it bearable. That's an overclaim, and a reader who knows any history will stop trusting you on the next page.

**Refused, as stated:** "There is only one guide you can trust." Four pages later you concede "the check is unreliable on anyone you already hold in contempt". That's the very person for whom I need a guide. So it's one guide I can trust some of the time. My tradition gives law, a teacher and a community because the heart is deceitful above all things. You half-grant this ("None of this is against rules"). Make the claim the size of the concession.

**Flinched:** the Hitler paragraph. "He's an example of the opposite" is probably right. But he arrives as a quick illustration in a list, and I spend my life fighting a politician who used that history as a quick illustration. Either give him room or leave him out.
