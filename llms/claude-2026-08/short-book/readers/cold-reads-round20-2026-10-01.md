## Daniel (52, law professor, former federal prosecutor, Catholic; cold, the cold-call smile)

### Stage 1

Aditya,

You asked me to read this the way I read anything, so I did: with a pencil, looking for the joint where the argument would give. I'll tell you where I bought, where I held on provisionally, and where I refused. I'll also tell you what happened when I ran your checks, because I did run them, on my own record, and some of what they turned up I haven't said aloud to anyone.

**The opening.** A small thing first. I'm Catholic, and you open on the Gospel of Philip. I know enough to know it isn't in my Bible, and for half a second I braced for a Gnostic sermon. It passed. The sentence itself is good, and the rest of the chapter never leaned on its authority. Then you used "limbo," which for me is a word with a whole catechism attached: the place for the unbaptized, which the Church has quietly stopped insisting on. I smiled at it. Your limbo is a better use of the word than ours turned out to be.

What opened me early was this: "Hold them tentatively, until you see the picture they assemble." And then you stated the central claim before arguing it: "Contempt is the claim that you're a different kind of thing than me, but for it to land, you must be the same kind of thing." That's how I want an argument to begin. Here is what I must prove. Watch me. I wrote "burden accepted" in the margin.

**The memory.** I took the instruction seriously. The case I went to was my marriage. For about six years I worked until ten most nights, first at the U.S. Attorney's office and then building the center here. My wife, Adaeze, said it in every way a person can say it. I had answers. The mortgage. Tuition. The work matters. People's liberty is on the line. When our counselor finally got me to say it in her office, it was not news. You asked: "did it feel like new information? Or like something you had always known, but somehow hadn't *let* yourself know?" The second. Plainly the second. I bought the first claim, and I bought it more firmly than you asked me to, for a reason you may not know about.

The law already has your limbo. We call it willful blindness. A defendant who believes there's a high probability a fact is true, and takes deliberate steps not to confirm it, is treated as knowing it. The courier who never opens the suitcase. I've asked judges for that instruction, the ostrich instruction, more times than I can count. I never once thought to give it to myself. That was the first moment in the chapter where something moved in me. The doctrine I used against other men describes, exactly, how I spent six years with my wife. I didn't need you to persuade me the state exists. I'd been convicting people of it.

"If that doesn't match anything in your memory, the rest of this won't make sense, and I'd rather you know that now." Good. You named the condition your argument depends on. That's honest.

Your deflection questions: did I deflect, and was it passive? No, it was not passive. I'm a trained advocate. I argued. Which is why "Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" landed hard. In a courtroom, the side with the long brief is usually the side with the weak facts. I know that.

But here's the first weak joint I marked, and I'd be no use to you if I hid it. I teach evidence. The most confident witness in the room, the one whose knowledge "arrived before" any reasoning, is often the eyewitness who is wrong. Unreasoned certainty is also where prejudice lives. A juror who just *knows* the young man in the hoodie did it isn't arguing either. In my memory, the part that didn't argue was right. I granted the point for my case. I did not grant that the non-arguing side is always the conscience. You don't say it is, quite, but the question leans that way.

**The cashier.** I pictured the young woman at the Jewel-Osco on Lake Park. Kindness went without anything. Cruelty wouldn't go until my mind, unasked, supplied her a fault: she's slow, she rang up the wrong thing. I noticed that, and it was interesting. But the check is also built to come out the way it comes out: you specified someone "who's done nothing to you." Of course cruelty needs a reason there. "Did either one need a reason before it would go?" has its answer in the setup. What saved it is what happened next, which you didn't ask for: I thought of my students. When I cold-call an unprepared student with the smile they dread, do I need a reason? I have one ready, always. *The profession demands it. Their clients will pay for their sloppiness.* The reason was waiting before the smile was. That I hadn't noticed.

**Remorse.** I tried hard to break this one, because "never" is the word I look for. As a prosecutor I once declined to charge a nineteen-year-old courier. Two years later he was arrested in a shooting. I felt sick. But when I pressed on it, it was doubt about my judgment, not the feeling of having betrayed myself. So in my own case, it held.

Then "Can you imagine it even in the worst person?" I can, actually, and I want you to know that. I sat in proffer sessions with men who told me they were ashamed of having let someone go, of having been soft. They spoke of it like a sin. You could answer that this is shame before the crew, not remorse, and I'd probably accept that. But you defined remorse as "the feeling of having gone against myself," and if "myself" means the better nature, the definition does the work the check was supposed to do. I granted it tentatively and kept going.

**The encounter.** Believable. More than believable: it is, in part, a trial technique. Provoke the defendant on cross until he loses his temper in front of the jury, then point to the outburst in closing. "I'm provoking him in a way that I'll later hold against him" is something I did on purpose, with full awareness, as craft. So my first reaction was that you'd made a deliberate thing sound unconscious. Then I thought about the classroom again, and I didn't do that one as craft. I smile, they fumble, the fumble proves they weren't prepared. I never tell myself I'm provoking anything.

The line that went into me like a nail: "He can't prove I'm doing this, can he? No. And crucially, *neither can I*. So I know it's not on purpose." Absence of proof taken as proof of innocence, by the person who arranged the absence. I've spent my career on exactly that distinction. I laughed out loud, and then didn't.

I skimmed at "Ha! Classic projection." and through the "invitation" paragraph. The scene is a little long, and the asides are a little broad. But I believed it as what a mind does. I've had that mind.

**The corruption.** "Why the fuck shouldn't I?" I've heard almost that sentence, almost those words, from defendants. That rang true. Then: "Well, look what it got me: proof that he's evil." That's cui bono. In court I'd object that benefit isn't proof of intent. Then I remembered how many closings I gave that ran entirely on cui bono. I granted it the weight I used to ask juries to give it.

**The two checks.** Vengeance or the pill. My worst enemy is Patricia Hale, the dean who killed my center's funding. I want to tell you I'd choose the pill. I would. But the honest answer is that I want a pill that makes her change her mind *and remember who was right*. I want her to come down the hall to say it. That isn't your binary, but it's the punishment craving dressed up as reconciliation, and I didn't like seeing it. With certain defense lawyers, the ones who played games, I'd mash the vengeance button and you know it. Your "I'll be honest: there is in me" helped. You owned it first. That's what let me own mine.

One thing: you offered a pill against vengeance, then asked about "that first button." Small, but I'm the reader who notices that kind of thing, and I stopped on it.

"What might you be revealing to people when you feel that urge?" I didn't understand the question. I read it twice and moved on.

"I tell myself it's innocuous as long as I don't act on it. Confined to my skull. And that lie is exactly how it escapes my skull, causing harm." Asserted, not shown, at that point. It got shown later, by the smile.

**"I'll go ahead and name what's happening."** Mostly insight, some lecture. Insight: "I am in limbo about provoking him, and showing him that I am in limbo *is* the provocation." I read that twice, the second time slowly, and it held. "Nothing that could be audited, *even by myself*." That's the smile. A student cannot file a grievance about a smile. Neither can I examine one. And: "I'm counting on him to know exactly what that is: harm that's sure it's good." Yes.

"The signal is the pretense that there is no signal." I read that three times. I think it's right, and I think it's one turn too clever for where it sits.

Lecture: from "That's what teaches him to make the same mistake" to "infecting others." "It is like a corruption that's passing from my mind to his" is a causal claim. I've seen contempt harden men; I've also seen men harden in ways no one person caused. And "We never tell ourselves" is where "I" became "we" without anything earning it. That's where I started reading as counsel for the other side.

**First real refusal.** "It acts as if it has goals of its own, and it pursues them *intentionally.*" Then "The only thing that benefits is *it*." Here I refused. You've put intent into something I can't put on the stand. I'm Catholic; I know what you're walking toward, and my tradition has a name for it. Maybe Chapter 2 earns it. In this chapter it's attribution without foundation. To your credit you said "Here's how it feels to me," and you said "That doesn't let me off the hook. It's my mind, and I answer for what it does." Without that sentence I'd have stopped reading as a friend; I've heard too many defendants explain that something else was driving. With it, I could follow what came after. The common-knowledge argument didn't depend on the agent, so I lost nothing there.

"What this part of me is, and why it behaves the way it does, is the subject of Chapter 2." That's in the middle of the chapter, before half of what follows. It read like a closing statement given halfway through the evidence.

**Integrity.** "But someone always was: me." As a Catholic I'd have said God was watching, and I was raised on the examination of conscience, so this part was familiar. What was new was the next turn: the watcher isn't the part making the reasons. That's what was true in my marriage. The lawyer in me was busy; something else was watching him.

Then "Why should turning away from your better nature require turning away from "yourself?"" and the reverse question after it. I read that paragraph twice and still don't think I followed the reverse question. The next one I did follow: "Did it feel like losing something of yourself, or like getting something back?" Getting something back. In the counselor's office I didn't lose anything. I got something back, and Adaeze said later that I'd looked like myself for the first time in years.

**"What we most fundamentally are."** Granted, in my own vocabulary, not found by your argument. Catholicism says we're made in the image of God, and that the image is wounded but not destroyed. So it didn't sound grandiose to me; it sounded like the catechism with the theology filed off. But I noticed what you did: you stated it as "Here's what I think," which is fair, and then later, in the common-knowledge section, it came back as something I "already know": "Your better nature is the deepest part of you; at some level you already know this; and you also know it about others." That's a claim about me, and about everyone, and it had been upgraded without new evidence. That annoyed me. I believe something close to it. I don't like being told I know it.

"my better nature will simply never fall for it. That's not something I can choose to override." Never. Cannot. Twice in two sentences. I wanted the evidence for "never."

**The corrector.** I had one. My first supervisor at the U.S. Attorney's office, Ruth Abramson, after I'd gone too hard on a cooperating witness who was nineteen and terrified. She closed her office door and said, "That was beneath you." That was all. She was angrier than I'd ever seen her. I did not dig in. I wanted to be what she thought I was. Your check worked exactly as you described: "They might have been *more* fierce than I just was with my enemy. But did it make you dig in? Vow revenge?" No. And "Take it out, and the fierceness stays; only the poison goes" is the best practical sentence in the chapter, and a sentence I'd give to every new prosecutor. Hate the sin, love the sinner: I've heard it all my life. I hadn't heard it put as the sinner being more correctable when you do it that way. That's a different, harder claim, and I think it's true.

**Common knowledge.** I followed it, and this is where you made your case, or most of it. The inference is a good one: if I could predict his reaction, what did I have to know about him to predict it? My first objection was that you can predict a dog will bite if you kick it without crediting the dog with a conscience. Then you answered it without knowing I'd raised it: "And hypocrisy only means something between two people who answer to the same thing." That's impeachment. You can only impeach a witness with his own prior statement. You can't impeach a dog. His accusation wasn't a bite; it was a charge of hypocrisy, and that charge is only available between parties bound by the same rule. That surprised me, and it's the one sentence I'd quote to someone else.

But note the gap. What that proves is that we answer to the same standard. Your conclusion, a sentence earlier, is bigger: "This is only possible if, at some level, we know that we are the same kind of thing—beings that must turn away to cause harm—and each knows the other knows it, and so on." "Must turn away to cause harm" is more than "answer to the same thing." You've shown the shared standard; the "must" is being carried in from the cashier.

"My justifications for it, on the other hand, are believable only to me and my allies." True of every closing argument I gave that I'm not proud of.

"What I'm hiding isn't that I *could* do what they do, it's that I'm *already doing it*." That one cost me something. The defense lawyers I despised "played games." What was the smile at the podium if not a game?

"Believing I'm fundamentally different and special is inherently self-deceptive: it licenses the very behavior that shows I'm not, so I can only keep it by not looking." Bought.

Then two places I felt handled. "As an external check, you can now trace the logic and see that your contempt (if you have any) wouldn't be coherent unless this were true. And if you don't have any, I can only assume it's because you're already way ahead of me." The first sentence tells me what I'll see; the second winks. "If I'm wrong, I'm wrong. But this is how things appear to me." That earned some of it back.

"I think we've all had this experience, where we directly intuit why someone is misbehaving, and what they need in the moment." I tried, honestly. Can I remember correcting someone nastily and later doing it better? No. I haven't changed yet. I'm still smiling at the podium. So the question asked me and the next sentence answered for me, and the answer was wrong in my case.

**The gaze.** Yes. A store detective at Marshall Field's in 1994, following me through menswear, and the look when I turned around. Did he seem "turned away" from himself? I can't see into him, and I won't claim to. He seemed absent, a man doing a job he'd been told was reasonable. "Is it possible that you look a little bit like this when treating others with contempt?" This is the question I'll remember, because it left me room, and because in the room it left I found a student evaluation from three years ago: "teaches through fear." I'd filed it as a complaint from someone who didn't do the reading. "I'm not saying you're as bad as him." Good. You didn't need to say I was.

**Self-serving.** "When I'm sure someone knew exactly what they were doing, I've made them a villain. When I'm sure they had no idea, I've made them a machine." That's the Model Penal Code in two sentences: purpose and knowledge on one side, negligence on the other. Recklessness, conscious disregard of a known risk, sits in between, and recklessness is your limbo. The law has been there longer than you think. Your examples, though, private equity and the donor's bill, are the enemies a certain reader already has. They let me nod at other people for a paragraph. "I suspect each knows" I appreciated: marked as a suspicion.

**Other kinds of contempt.** This caught me. "The stupid, the slow and the weak get the same verdict, with better cover: *I'm only being accurate.*" That's my cold-call. That's how I've described it to colleagues.

Then the paragraph about people we love. My daughter, Chiamaka, is a 1L at another school. In October of her first semester she sent me her memo. I sent it back with a great deal of red and one word at the top, "Sloppy," which I meant as rigor. She hasn't sent me one since. "The look on their face that tells me it landed, which I pretend not to have seen, leaving them nothing to accuse me of." I didn't see her face; it was email. I heard her voice at Christmas when someone asked if I'd been helping with her writing, and she said, "Dad's busy." I pretended not to hear it.

"It has the same flavor as what we call evil." Too strong for a red pen, I'd have said a week ago. I'm less sure now. I'll grant "flavor."

**Did it end in the right place?** It ended in the right place for me, by accident, because the last paragraph happened to be my daughter. But the chapter ends three or four times: "If I'm wrong, I'm wrong," then the gaze, then "Self-serving," then "Other kinds." The last two sections read like additions after the verdict. They were the two that caught me hardest, so I wouldn't cut them. I'd ask whether they belong before the verdict.

**Length.** It drags from "We never tell ourselves" through the paragraph that begins "Here's how it feels to me." That's where it argues by restatement: conscious-but-unaware gets defined three times, "better kind of thing" appears so often it starts to sound like a slogan. If I had to cut a fifth, I'd cut there, the "gap in my awareness" paragraph, the "So why doesn't it *feel* like we know this" paragraph, and the "excruciating pain" sentence, which tells me about your pain instead of letting me see it. I'd keep every check.

**Words.** "Limbo" I liked, for my own reasons. "Mash that first button" made me wince, as did "Ha! Classic projection." and "way ahead of me." "Darkness" I accepted. "Better nature" is Lincoln's and it works.

**Last.** Was any of it obvious? The integrity saying, and hate-the-sin. Every Catholic has heard both since First Communion. What became newly consequential was the willful-blindness move: a doctrine I have applied to other men for twenty-five years turned around on me. And the smile. I teach Evidence on Tuesday. I don't know yet whether I'll cold-call differently. I do know I'll watch my own face when I do it, which I have never once done. And I'm going to ask Chiamaka for her next memo. Whether she sends it is up to her.

Would I pass it on? To Ruth Abramson, yes; she'd recognize everything and would mark up the "we"s the way I did. Not to my faculty colleagues; they'd go straight for "everyone" and "never" and miss the cross-examination scene, which is the best thing here. And I'd want to pass it, with some nervousness, to Tony Russo, my old trial partner, who still laughs at defendants in the hallway. He'd stop at the Gospel of Philip. I'd tell him to skip to the part about proof.
