## Peg (Karma Kagyu, three-year retreatant, retired special-education teacher, leads a Thursday Mahamudra group)

### Stage 1

Dear Aditya,

I'm Peg Haldane. I taught special education for thirty years, I've practiced in the Karma Kagyu lineage for about as long, and I lead a Mahamudra group on Thursday nights. You should know before you read this that I came in braced. Most books that try to say something about the mind in my direction either borrow our vocabulary and use it loosely, or hand out the nature of mind like a brochure. I was waiting for you to do one of those things. For most of the chapter you didn't, and the places you came closest are where I'll be most specific.

I did the exercises. I'll tell you what happened in each, in the order you gave them.

**The memory at the start.** It came immediately, which tells you something. In 1993 I had a fifth-grader, Dustin, who couldn't decode past a second-grade level. I hadn't adapted his materials the way I knew I should have; I was tired, and I had eleven other kids. At his triennial review I wrote that he "was not making adequate progress due to inconsistent effort." He was moved to a more restrictive placement that spring. I knew when I wrote that sentence it was about me, not him. I didn't let myself know it for about four years.

So when you asked whether owning up felt like new information or "like something you had always known, but somehow hadn't *let* yourself know," I didn't have to think. The second. Bought, fully. And "It was right there to see—fully available to consciousness—but you were *turned away* from it" is the most accurate thing anyone outside the dharma has written to me about this. It isn't repression. It was sitting in the room with me. I liked that you said "If that doesn't match anything in your memory, the rest of this won't make sense, and I'd rather you know that now." That's a teacher who's willing to lose a student honestly, and it made me trust you.

The deflection questions worked too. Did some part of me know I was deflecting, even as I did it? Yes. I changed the subject every time his name came up in the staff room, and I knew why. "Limbo comes in degrees" also held: Dustin is harder to remember this way than the small things, not easier.

"If seeing that makes you want to call yourself names, hold off. That's not owning up; it's another way to hide." I teach this almost word for word and I was glad to see it so early. A lot of Western students turn the first glimpse of their own mind into a self-flagellation project and call it practice.

"Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" This is where I first felt free rather than led. You asked and stopped. And the answer I found was my own: the part that knew didn't argue because it had nothing to gain. The arguing side was the one with a stake.

**The cashier.** I did it with the young man at King Soopers on Table Mesa, the one with the lip ring who's always a little slow. The smile went without anything. The sneer wouldn't go until I gave it a reason, and the reason I reached for was that he's slow. That surprised me, and not pleasantly. But here's what else I noticed, and you should know it: what I actually give him most weeks isn't a smile or a sneer. It's the politeness with nothing behind it, and that needs no reason at all. It goes on its own. I don't know whether that's contempt or just inattention. Your later paragraph on the cold version made me suspect it's closer to contempt than I'd like.

**Remorse.** I looked for remorse for having been decent and couldn't find any. Regret, yes: I once lent a parent money I never saw again, and I felt foolish. Not remorse. I bought the asymmetry. "This suggests our moral compass isn't just arbitrary; it only points in one direction" I bought tentatively. "Suggests" is the right verb and I was glad you used it.

"I'm usually not aware of this need. Watch how it affects my behavior anyway." Good. You were about to show me your mind rather than mine, and that's the order I trust.

**The encounter.** Believable as what a mind does, yes, and believable because you didn't make yourself look good in it. "There's no bottom to what can be done to such a person" stopped me. I skimmed the middle stretch, from "Plus, he can't prove I'm doing this" through "Classic," not because it was wrong but because I got it a beat before you finished it. "Surely that's not what I'm doing. I'm just being fierce with evil, right? Right." I laughed, unhappily.

The hot version isn't me. I don't call people out. So the short section on the cold version was the door I came in through. "A politeness with nothing behind it" is my "and how is your practice?" to the students who collect empowerments. It's my careful patience with our board treasurer, Gary. It's my warm silence at Thanksgiving with my son's in-laws. And "and I write it off as "just being honest."" Yes. I tell myself I'm being accurate about where people are on the path. I reread the whole encounter with the cold one in mind, as you asked, and it fit better than I wanted it to.

**"I'll just go ahead and name what's happening."** Insight, not lecture, though it's dense. I had to read "Neither half alone would be enough to get me what I need" twice, and then the two sentences after it, before I saw why the accidental version and the self-aware version would both fail. Once I saw it, it was very good. "In brief: I am in limbo about provoking him, and showing him that I'm in limbo is itself the provocation" is the sentence that finally made it click; I'd have been fine reading that first. "The signal is the pretense that there is no signal" I read twice and then a third time for pleasure.

"He sees that he cannot appeal to my better nature, because I've convinced myself this *is* my better nature." That's the one. That is precisely how spiritual pride works, and it's why a teacher's cold contempt is worse than a layman's: it comes dressed as the dharma.

"Deny it, and you're the kind of person who does. Get angry, and that's proof too. Stay calm, and you don't even care. Apologize, and it's too convenient." I've been on that end. My son's father-in-law, Rick, has a way of saying "We'll be praying for you, Margaret" that does this. Anything I say back confirms something. (I'll come back to whether I'm reading Rick right. I'm not sure I am.)

"My stories kept changing; my conclusion never did." Landed.

**The corruption.** "It won't feel like a loss to him. It will feel like he's gotten a bit wiser, a little more realistic about how the world works." This is the best thing in the chapter for me as a teacher. I have watched students learn cynicism from teachers and call it maturity.

"From the inside, being proven right and being right are the same experience. The only difference is who produced the evidence." And "If I'd looked carefully, I'd have seen my role in causing it. So I didn't—and that *was* my role in causing it." Both landed. Neither needed anything after it.

**The punishment checks.** Is there something viscerally satisfying about seeing evil violently punished? Yes. I'm a sixty-four-year-old Buddhist widow and I cheer at the end of crime shows. Bought.

**The worst enemy.** I took Rick. Two buttons. I reached for the pill without hesitation, and felt good about myself for about four seconds. Then I noticed what I was picturing: Rick's heart changed, and Rick *knowing* it had been changed, and knowing he'd been wrong about me for twelve years. The pill was my vengeance button with better manners. I hadn't wanted to mash the first one. I'd wanted the second one to do the first one's work. I don't think you designed the exercise to catch that, but it caught it, and I'm grateful. "Is some part of you itching to mash that first button? I'll be honest: there is in me." I believed you, and your saying it first made it easier to look.

"And that lie is exactly what lets me act on it, in ways I rarely recognize until later." Bought.

**"Whose intention."** The part that "acts as if it has goals of its own, and it pursues them intentionally": I heard Mara in it, honestly, or the way my teachers talk about ego-clinging as though it had a strategy. I bought it tentatively. What saved it was the next paragraph, and it came in exactly the right place: "Don't mistake the grammar for the claim: I'm not saying there's a separate thing living in me." And "None of this lets me off the hook. It's my mind, and I answer for what it does." If you'd waited one more paragraph to say that, I'd have closed the book for the evening thinking you were heading toward demons.

"When I'm sure someone was fully aware of what they were doing, I've made them a villain. When I'm sure they had no idea, I've made them a machine." I've done the second one for thirty years with the treasurer. Gary isn't malicious, I tell myself; he just doesn't understand. That's the machine. That paragraph changed something.

**The bad essence.** "But I think badness is the result of turning away, not its cause." This is our teaching, said without our words: the obscurations are adventitious. It caught me because you got there from your own encounter rather than from a text. The paragraph that starts "Here's what makes it especially difficult" read more like you heading off an objection, and I could have done without it. But "And I only do that because, at a level I cannot let myself see, I fear I might be one of them" caught me hard, and the deeper fear after it, "that there may be no such thing as real goodness, in me or in anyone, so I'd better protect myself at any cost," caught me harder. I have had pointing-out instructions. I have rested in what I was shown. And that fear is still in me, under the patience. I don't usually admit that.

"*Only a chump believes otherwise.*" Landed, and I connected it immediately to the line in the enemy's head earlier, "I'd be a chump." The fear I hold is the lesson I teach. I sat with that one for a while.

**"What does "yourself" mean here?"** This is where I first refused something, and I want to be careful about how.

I did it. I went back to Dustin and looked for the one I'd hidden it from. And there was awareness, plainly, the thing that had seen it all along. So far, yes. But then you ask: "Can you feel how it's just the thing you normally feel as "I"? Whatever it is that's *aware*?" And I can't say yes to that, because in my practice those are exactly the two things you learn not to confuse. The thing I normally feel as "I" is the one that was protecting itself in 1993. The awareness that knew isn't that; when you look for the "I" in it, you don't find one. So I found something close to what you point at, but I found it by refusing your description of it.

Then the next question did something better. "Why would you have to hide something from *yourself* in order to keep it from your better nature?" When I tried it, the answer was: because they aren't two. And "did it feel like *losing* something of yourself, or like getting something back?" Getting something back, without a doubt. So the question worked even though the sentence before it misdescribed what I found. I could still follow everything that came after.

I'll also say: a sentence like "Whatever it is that's *aware*?" in a book for anyone is a small pointing-out instruction. It's restrained, and you said "I'm not asking a philosophical question," which is right. But some of my students would take that line and think they'd been introduced to the nature of mind, and they haven't. I don't know what you should do about that. I'm just telling you where I felt the floor shift.

**"Isn't one part among others."** "Our better nature isn't one part among others. It's a description of what we *are* in the absence of all self-deception." I found it in myself, but I found it the way I was taught to find it, so I can't tell you whether you earned it or my tradition did. It didn't sound grandiose to me; it sounded like buddha nature in plain clothes. "In the absence of all self-deception" is the right qualifier, and it's better than the "I" sentence above it. "Not as some grandiose idea, but as a direct experience" made me wince a little. Someone who has had the direct experience rarely says so. I'd rather the page show it than claim it. The later line "**Love and contempt are addressed to the same place**" did more to make me believe it than anything in this section.

**The gaze.** I took the principal I worked under in the late nineties, who looked at me in IEP meetings as though I were furniture that occasionally spoke. Could I feel he was absent, turned from himself? Yes, actually. Then "Why would he have to turn away at all, if what he is, is essentially bad?" When I tried to answer it, I couldn't make "essentially bad" survive the question. Something bad all the way through has nothing to avert its eyes from. The turning implies a something. I knew this doctrinally. I hadn't felt it about him. And did I look a tiny bit like him when I treated him with contempt? A young woman in my group told me last spring that I teach "like a closed door." Yes.

"Now, when I feel a fundamental difference between me and my worst enemy, I ask which part of me needs it to be there." I wrote this on an index card.

**Integrity.** The person who corrected me with integrity was my retreat master, in the third year. In a group interview I was describing my practice in a way that, I see now, was meant to impress. He said in front of eight people, "Margaret, you are using the dharma to become a more impressive person." It was harsher than anything you did to your enemy, and it was the kindest thing anyone said to me in three years. I didn't dig in. I went back to my cabin and wept and then practiced better than I had in months. "Not more gently" is exactly right, and "*you're better than this*" is exactly what I heard under his words. "It's possible for the fierceness to stay while the poison goes. What's left is fierce kindness." Yes. We'd call it wrathful compassion, and I'm glad you didn't.

**The time I corrected someone badly.** 1986, a paraprofessional named Lorraine who had restrained one of my kids when she didn't need to. I dressed her down in front of the class, and my face said what I thought she was. She didn't argue. She got colder with the kids and quit by spring. Twenty years later, with a different para, same problem, I took her into the supply room, told her plainly it couldn't happen again, and told her I knew she'd had a hard morning. She stayed four years and was very good. "So the harm goes somewhere I can't see": it went to the kids Lorraine worked with afterward, I suppose. I've never thought about that until today.

**The saying about integrity.** "But someone always is: me." I spent three years alone in a cabin. I knew this. You put it well, but it's the one place in the chapter I'd mark "I already knew."

**Common knowledge.** I followed it, and it held, and it added something. "Does it surprise you that a stranger could count on that about you?" It didn't, and I want to tell you why: I tried imagining writing "inconsistent effort" while looking squarely at the fact that I hadn't adapted his materials, and my hand wouldn't do it. I could only write that sentence turned away. So no surprise; it just confirmed the first claim.

What surprised me was the hypocrisy step. "But hypocrisy only means something between people who share a standard—and since we're enemies, I tell myself we share none." Rick thinks I'm a hypocrite: a woman who talks about compassion and then sits in warm silence at his table. When I sit with that, I can't say he's wrong. And if the charge lands, we share the standard. Rick and I answer to the same thing. That's a strange sentence for me to write and I believe it. "Neither of us had to be told what my look meant, or what turning away is" held too.

**Where someone's contempt reached in me.** Back to Rick and "We'll be praying for you, Margaret." I felt for where it reached. "Not your pride: an insult to your pride is easier to shrug off." Right. It went under that, to the place that wants to be counted as a real person and not a project. And then I had to be honest: I'm not sure Rick means it as contempt. He might mean it. Your line "And sometimes I'm simply wrong: what they did wasn't harm at all" came back to me here, and I let it stand.

"**Love and contempt are addressed to the same place.** One says *I know you're in there.* The other says *there's nobody in there*, and they both say it to the somebody who is." This is the best sentence in the chapter. I'm going to bring it to my group. It's what pointing-out is, from the other side: a teacher saying "I know you're in there" to someone who doesn't yet. And it's what my closed door says in reverse.

**The last time I sent that look.** Last Thursday. A man in my group, Kyle, mentioned he'd be driving to Crestone for his third empowerment this year. I said, "And how is your practice?" Did it feel like justice, just a little sweeter than justice needs to be? Yes. Did I know he'd read it? Yes. Did I mean him to know I meant it, while giving myself an out? That question has the out built into it; that's why I use it. It's a legitimate teacher's question. No one could call it contempt without sounding unhinged. I've been doing your hall of mirrors with a sentence that sounds like care.

**"Evil."** I flinch at the word generally; we don't use it much. "That's the thing I normally call "evil"—cruelty for its own sake; the thing I hate most in the world—and I was revealing it to him on purpose." I accepted it, slowly. I asked myself whether "and how is your practice?" could be called cruelty for its own sake, and the honest answer is: a teaspoon of it, yes. The sweetness is the giveaway. "It's why I don't think "pure evil" is a different kind of thing, but the same thing at full strength" I accept; it's what my tradition holds, and you reached it from your own taste, which is the only way it's worth anything. "In fact, "bad people" is just the name I use to describe people I feel okay doing this to" landed hard.

**The advisor passage.** I took "the same thing" to be what I was shown in pointing-out, though I'd have accepted plain conscience, and I think you mean both at once. "I treat it like an advisor offering an opinion, which I can weigh against other practical concerns and set aside if the price is right." That's my careful patience with Gary exactly. I weigh. I decide the board meeting isn't the place. "That I have the right to look at what is right and say not today" caught me. "So part of me already knows the difference between the "good" I've invented to excuse myself and the kind we all recognize" I bought.

The next paragraph lost me for a moment: "Sometimes this reminds them to do the same—especially when the world has given me every reason to abandon it. Even if they do, I can't take credit for it." I read it three times. "Them" becomes "me" mid-sentence, and I couldn't tell whose abandoning was meant.

**"Try to imagine the same act without turning away."** Answered above. I couldn't.

**The spread, to the end.** "I'm making the world a little darker, and I can feel it even as I do it." I believe you. "And I send it *about* people, to my allies, with nobody's face in view." That's the sangha kitchen after the Thursday group. Caught.

The paragraph about quiet contempt says again what the cold-version paragraph said. "The stupid, the slow and the weak get the same verdict, with better cover: *I'm only being accurate.*" is the earlier "just being honest" over again. You only need one. Since the earlier one is where I came in, I'd keep that.

The paragraph about people we love is hard and true. My son says I "meditate at him." When I read "For one second the verdict flashes through my eyes, and I make sure they see it, and then I go back to loving them as if nothing had happened," I put the pages down.

And then: "The subtlest contempt I know is the one I feel toward people who haven't seen through this trap yet. It can look like patience, or even like care. Sometimes it looks like me writing this book *at* people, instead of *for* them." Aditya, that is me. Patience, care, *at* instead of *for*. A closed door. Meditating at my son. You couldn't have known, and you named it anyway, and you named it in yourself first, which is the only reason I could take it.

The last paragraph ended in the right place. "What I need in order to look is what my enemy needed from me, and what the corrector with integrity gave me: to be seen as more than what I do." It took me back to my retreat master without being told to. I wouldn't add a word.

**Repeats and length.** Things said twice: the cold version and the quiet-contempt paragraph, as above. "Conscious yet unaware: that's the limbo state" tells me what I'd already been shown. The "Whose intention" section circles; I felt the shape of the argument twice there before it moved on. "Here's what makes it especially difficult" could go. If I had to cut a fifth, I'd take it from "Whose intention," from the quiet-contempt paragraph, from the comic middle of the encounter, and from the paragraph about my enemy's "hardening" and the flicker of satisfaction, which the "proven right" lines say better. The opening sentence of the chapter is long enough that I had to read it twice, and it promises the whole chapter before I'd agreed to anything.

**Words.** "Evil," as above. "Better nature" I translated each time, but I'm glad you didn't use ours. "Not as some grandiose idea, but as a direct experience" made me wince. Nothing else.

**Last.** Was it obvious? Some of it was, to me, because I've been taught it. The asymmetry of remorse, badness as absence, someone is always watching. What was not obvious, and what has become consequential: that my patience is a delivery system. I knew contempt was a defilement. I did not know that "and how is your practice?" works *because* Kyle can read it, and that I count on that. That changes what I'll do this week. I'm going to call Kyle before Thursday, and I'm going to ask him about Crestone and actually listen. I'm going to stop asking that question until I can ask it without the teaspoon.

Would I pass this on? My first impulse was to send it to Rick. Then to the empowerment collectors in my group. I noticed both impulses and I think you'd recognize them. I'll pass it to Ruth, who co-leads with me and has the same cold streak I do, and who will tell me if I've misread it. And maybe, later, to my son, but not yet, because right now it would be me meditating at him with a book.

With real gratitude, and some discomfort,
Peg Haldane

### Stage 2

Dear Aditya,

I've now read the second file. I'll be plain: the chapter did more to me, and on the whole it treated me better too. But the second file has things in it that the chapter needs back, and one of them is something I'd have marked as the best line in either version if I'd met it first.

**What happened as I read the second file.**

The opening stopped me before anything else did. "Imagine looking into a stranger's eyes long enough that their face stops being something you're looking at and becomes a place somebody is looking out of." That's a practice, not a premise. I did it with the stranger I'd used for the cashier check, the young man at King Soopers, and there was a moment when the face turned around, so to speak, and someone was in there. "This one is like me. I want them to be all right." That's very close to how we teach loving-kindness to beginners, and you got there without a single borrowed word. "With it comes a sense of their innocence. Not that they're incapable of causing harm—but the sense of something innocent underneath it." That is the closest either version comes to describing what I was shown in retreat, and it's done by pointing at a face, not at the nature of mind. That's the right way to do it in a book.

Then you ran it again with an atrocity. Your "It's harder, isn't it?" irritated me, because it answered for me. But your next sentence, "If it isn't, make the case real: someone from your own life, or from the news, whose harm you can't wave off," gave me the room back, and I used it. I took a man from the news, a few years ago, an aide who abused nonverbal children in a classroom in Aurora. I spent my career with children like those. And yes: "only something rotten." I could feel the verdict in my own eyes and I could feel it wanting to land. It was harder than anything the chapter asked of me, because the chapter let me choose Rick, and Rick has never done anything worse than pray for me.

"Usually the latter." That one I didn't like. You asked a question and answered it in the next line.

The encounter in the second file is less alive than in the chapter. "Truth be told, something deep inside knows he's right" is a report; in the chapter, "*Shit.* He's onto me." is the thing happening. But the second file has "What I do to you no longer qualifies as harm." That's sharper than the chapter's "There's no bottom to what can be done to such a person," and I'd want both.

Then this, in the cold-contempt paragraph, which went straight through me: "And if neither does—maybe because your enemies are rarely in the room with you—check if the people who *are* in the room might sense something "dark" about your behavior." My enemies are never in the room. My son is. My Thursday students are. The young woman who said I teach like a closed door is. In the chapter, I came to that recognition alone, at the very end, through "the subtlest contempt." Here you sent me there at the start, and gently. And then: "If not, I have no argument to offer here. Read on, and see whether anything else fits." That's the most respectful sentence in either version.

The naming section in the second file is longer and does more explaining, and I followed it, but I read it the way I read the chapter's "Whose intention": waiting for it to move. Two things in it caught me. "It just convinces me it's the *good kind* of darkness: the kind all good people must harbor to keep *real* evil in check." That is the exact self-justification of a stern teacher. I've told myself my coldness keeps the dharma from being cheapened. And "I call it an invitation. He sees through that, so I call it being fierce. He still won't let it go, so I call it projection." The chapter's "My stories kept changing; my conclusion never did" is the better sentence, but this shows the stories one after another, and seeing them listed is how I recognized my own.

The limbo section is nearly the same as the chapter's. The chapter's version is firmer ("that knowledge was never unconscious"), and the firmness is what made me trust it. "Finally: when you were in limbo, did some part of you know you were, even as you hid it? If so, limbo contains knowledge of itself, in limbo." I read that three times. It's true, and in a practice context it's interesting. On the page it's a riddle.

Then the passage on self-contempt, which is the thing the chapter most needs back. "The most common contempt of all is the one we aim at ourselves: *you are what you did.*" And: "Nobody ever looked honestly at something in themselves while being told they were worthless for having it." Aditya, I have spent thirty years trying to say that to Western students and never said it that well. Half the people in my group came to the dharma carrying that verdict against themselves, and they bring it to the cushion and call it practice. The chapter has the seed of this ("If seeing that makes you want to call yourself names, hold off. That's not owning up; it's another way to hide") and comes back to it at the end, but the second file actually makes the case, and makes it right after the memory of wrongdoing, which is when a reader needs it. The second file also ends that passage with "What you need in order to look is what your enemy needed from you: to be seen as more than what you did." The chapter saves almost the same sentence for its last line. I think the chapter is right to end on it; I think it would land even harder at the end if the reader had been given this earlier passage near the start.

"turning away isn't one form of darkness among many. It is the very *root*." I accepted it; in my tradition ignorance is the root and this is a fair translation. "All harm begins by *turning away* from others. I can't prove this to you, but I invite you to check it yourself" I bought tentatively, and I liked that you said you can't prove it. "Can they do it with kindness in their eyes?" I tried it with the aide in Aurora and couldn't picture it.

The gaze question is weaker here than in the chapter. "Maybe there's no part of them that cares, but maybe they just no longer have access to it. Either way, the state looks essentially the same." That leaves the essence question open and walks away from it. The chapter's "Why would he have to turn away at all, if what he is, is essentially bad?" is the one that did something in me. But "What you're seeing in them is limbo, aimed at *you*" is good and the chapter doesn't have it.

The malice-and-contempt levels I had to read twice and still wasn't sure I had them.

"So what I was hiding from myself, underneath everything else, was nothing but *the fact that I was hiding*." This interested me as a practitioner. It's close to how we talk about the obscuration that obscures itself. I'm not sure a general reader would feel it, and I'm not sure I did, as opposed to recognizing it.

Then three lines I'd want back. "The darkness leaks out of my skull, empowering his, because its meaning is universal. My justifications, on the other hand, are believable only to me (and my allies)." (I'd lose "out of my skull.") The point is a real one and the chapter doesn't make it: my look needs no translation, and my reasons do. "As far as I've seen, everything I can accomplish *with* the darkness can be accomplished better without it." That's the practical promise the chapter's fierce-kindness passage implies but never quite states, and it's what I'd say to a student who thinks gentleness is weakness. And "In my experience, my darkness doesn't survive my looking directly at it; not its righteousness, anyway." That is a meditator's sentence. If I'd needed proof you've sat, that line would be it. The qualification after the semicolon is what makes it honest.

Then it lost me. "I want to be crystal clear here." And "in my absolutely deepest moments of introspection." Those two made me wince, the second especially; it's the one place in either version where you sound like you're claiming a depth rather than showing it. And it ends on "I might even have an inkling of what that way is," which stops where the chapter starts paying out. No corrector with integrity, no fierce kindness, no "Love and contempt are addressed to the same place," no subtlest contempt. The second file is a diagnosis. The chapter is a diagnosis and a door.

**Which version did more to me, and which treated me better.** The chapter did more. The integrity section, the love-and-contempt line, and the subtlest-contempt paragraph changed what I'm doing this week, and nothing in the second file reached that far. On treatment, it's closer than I expected. The second file has the two most generous moments in either version ("If not, I have no argument to offer here" and the self-contempt passage).
