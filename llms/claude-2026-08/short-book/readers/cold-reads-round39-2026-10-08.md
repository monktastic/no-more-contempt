I owned it to myself maybe eight months later, and I have never told her.

Your question: did it feel like new information, or something I hadn't *let* myself know? The second, without any doubt. The first place I refused anything: "that knowledge was never unconscious." For my memory, yes. As a claim with "never" in it, no. I've sat with people whose knowledge of what happened to them, or what they did, was not available in the way you describe. Some of that is dissociation and some of it I can't name. Then the second stranger, and it's Molloy again, but this time he's catching me on the same night, in the same wagon. Same offense, two men, two outcomes. The chapter puts Molloy across the room from the bad stranger, with a long scene between them. The second file puts them side by side, and the difference hits you all at once. "And if you had to guess: is *this* the kind of person who sneaks around trying to get away with stuff?" No. Molloy never was. That question I answered on my own and nobody pushed me.

So for the first two sections the second file did more to me than the chapter did. Then it lost me, and I'll get to that.

**Then the catch.** It came late, in the second file, and I didn't see it coming. "And what do we call people who obviously want to cause harm—especially when pretending it's good? How do we treat them? We call them *bad people,* and treat them with *contempt.*" In my first letter I told you about Halloran, the defense attorney who smirked at a nine-year-old on the stand. I've hated that man for eleven years. Reading that, I realized Halloran is the first stranger. My contempt for him is contempt for contempt. I'd been sitting there the whole second file as the guy who got wronged by the stranger, nodding along, and then the file turns around and I'm the one treating the stranger exactly the way he treated me. The chapter never got me with that. I'd keep it.

**What the second file has that the chapter should take back.** You asked me to be thorough, so here's the list, most important first.

1. "He was judging you *from* the very state he was judging you *for*." That's the whole thing in one line. The chapter says it in pieces. I'd have underlined this if it were paper.

2. "Certainty, urgency, the sense that he must not let this go—those are what facing real evil actually calls for. What's missing is his felt connection to what they were meant to serve. *Life*." The chapter has "my fierce desire to stop him comes from a good place. So do the certainty and urgency, the sense that I must not let this go." That's the same first half. But the chapter never says what's missing, and the second file does. For a man who did SVU, it matters a great deal that you let me keep the urgency. I needed to be told that the fire wasn't the problem. And "*Life*" is the right word for what it was supposed to serve. Those kids were why I did the job. Somewhere around year five I lost track of that and it was all about the men.

3. "*My darkness isn't real darkness—it's the kind all good people must harbor to keep bad people in check. They just take it the wrong way, because of course they would.*" That's the sheepdog speech. Every academy class in the country hears some version of it: the sheep, the wolves, and you're the sheepdog, and the sheepdog has to have some wolf in him. I believed that for twenty-six years. The chapter's "I'm just being fierce with evil, right? Right." is close, but it's one man talking himself into it. The second file names the actual creed, the one with a whole profession behind it. Every cop who reads it will recognize it.

4. "The record will show that the second stranger was *harsher*—and so he should have been the one that made it worse. Which means that any harm caused by the first one cannot be blamed on him." I spent my life writing reports. This is exactly how the paperwork lies without anyone lying. The chapter has "Later, everyone will argue about the things that can be measured—who said or did what—obscuring the actual cause." That's the same point, but the second file puts it in a way a cop or a lawyer will feel. Molloy screaming on Kensington Avenue would look worse on body cam than the guy who just looked at me. Body cam would have hung Molloy and cleared the other guy.

5. "It's the same faculty that let you see him. That sees anyone. Demote it, and you see a little less of everyone." That's what happened to me in SVU. Not only to the perps. To Denise, to the girls. Kathleen's "You look at everyone like a suspect" is this sentence. The chapter says the corruption passes to the enemy. The second file says it costs you everybody, and that's the part I paid.

6. "Limbo is for things we know are wrong. What's wrong with steering a bad person into revealing what they already are?" And then "He can't fix you, and he can't make you worse." That second one is what every cop believes about the men he arrests. I believed it. The chapter answers it, but it never lets the cop say it first, plainly, in his own words. Saying it plainly lets a reader like me nod before the floor goes out.

7. "*Our conscience is the only thing that warns us when we've strayed from it.*" And in general, the word "conscience." The second file uses it everywhere. The chapter uses "better nature." In my first letter I told you the word I found when I looked for "yourself" was conscience, because that's what the nuns called it. The second file gave me my own word back. "Better nature" sounds like Lincoln. I liked the chapter's "Not because it's nicer. Because it's the one that's home." better than anything in the second file on that point. But I'd want the word conscience somewhere in the chapter, early. A lot of people like me will find it faster under that name.

8. "And yet, despite "losing" that battle, notice that it never budged an inch. It kept on quietly knowing what it knew." In my first letter I wrote, about your question on why one side had to argue, that the side that knew never argued with me, it just sat there. The second file said that before I did. The chapter only asks the question. Here I'd have liked the answer too, because it's the thing I'd lived for twenty-three years after the wagon.

9. "If all you could hear was your own conscience—no justifications to hide behind—could you have even done what you did?" This is the chapter's "Try it now: imagine the same act without turning away," but it comes at the start, while the memory is still in your hand. In the chapter I had to go back and dig the wagon out again. Either place works. I'd say the early one is stronger because I was already holding it.

10. "*if this is the kind of world we live in—where people can be cruel and the world calls it "righteous"; where we all know what's happening but pretend not to—then fuck it. Game on.*" The chapter has the hypocrite saying "I'd be a chump." Both are true; Ray said both to me. But "Game on" is how the corner talks and how Ray talked in 2001. "Chump" is more polite, and I think you were being polite. I'd consider it.

11. "What you showed them, for a second, was that you'd stopped seeing them." That's the sigh at Denise in one sentence. The chapter's love-and-contempt line is better as a line. But this tells me what the sigh *was*.

12. "*I am going to provoke you until you become the very thing I need you to be. Which means that when I'm finally forced to do to you what I must, nobody will feel sorry for you*". On the street we had a name for this: contempt of cop. You mouth off, you get pushed, you push back, and now it's resisting and nobody feels sorry for you. I watched it happen a hundred times and did it a few. The chapter has the same thing in "*Yes, we ultimately cause harm through the same mechanism—but I'm going to get away with mine, by making you rot for yours.*" The chapter's is shorter and I'd keep it short. But "until you become the very thing I need you to be" is the line a street cop will recognize.

13. "Check whether, from here, looking at the flash without the reasons, you still endorse it. If you don't, that isn't a defense. But notice what's doing the refusing: the part you were hiding from." The chapter does this with the sigh too ("Now look back at it with their face in view, and without the reasons"). But the second file's last sentence there says what the refusing *is*. I'd keep that sentence.

**What the chapter was right to leave out or change.**

The biggest thing: the second file keeps it on "him," the stranger, for page after page, The Tell, The Why, The How, The Righteous Appearance, The Loop. Nobody in it is confessing anything. The chapter starts with *you*, Aditya, doing it, in your own scene, with your own teacher and your own button. A man who confesses first, I'll listen to. A man who spends five sections explaining what's wrong with the other guy sounds like the other guy. Somewhere in The Loop I was skimming. I couldn't tell you what "Which makes you increasingly sense the whole thing as a ruse, threatening to expose it" means without going back, and I didn't go back.

"I mean, *obviously*." That felt like being laughed at. "Harder, almost certainly." Leave that one out too; let me say it. "Because we **all** know it." And "We all know this is how it works. We have to, or else it wouldn't work." The second file keeps telling me what everybody knows. The chapter has "Maybe this isn't how it works in others, but it's what I found in myself." That's the line that made me trust you, and there's nothing like it in the second file.

"Try this: go out in public, find a stranger, and shoot them a dehumanizing gaze. It's hard to do, isn't it?" No, Aditya. For me it isn't. I did it for a living. You learn to look at people a certain way on the job and it doesn't cost you a thing at the time. It costs you later. The chapter's exercise, the face you stay with until somebody's looking out of it, asks me to *try*, and doesn't tell me how it'll go. That's why it worked on me.

"**The only reason we ever turn away is to enable harm, and we cannot intend harm without turning away**." The second half I bought in the chapter, with the wagon. The first half I won't. I turned away from the photographs of those kids to keep from losing my mind. I turned away at crime scenes so I could do my job. That wasn't to enable harm. Maybe you mean a different turning away, and the chapter seems to know that. In the second file it's in bold and says "only," and I balked.

"It's the thing we call **evil**. Not as a judgement, but as the very essence of what "evil" *means*: to turn away from the life in front of us in order to pursue our own ends." The chapter is more careful. It lets me call the bottomless look evil, "cruelty for its own sake," and I followed it there. The second file wants the sigh at Denise to be the same thing as what the men I arrested did, "the smallest morsel contains the structure of the whole." Maybe it does. But I'd have to get there myself. Said that flat, it reads like a man who's never been in an SVU interview room.

"And it's what this book exists to provide you." Sales pitch. Leave it out, like you did.

"I'll also call it his "dark" part, or just his "darkness"". I'm a Catholic, I don't mind a devil. But in the second file it's always *his* darkness, the stranger's. In the chapter it's "a part of me," yours, and then "It's my mind, and I answer for what it does." The chapter is right. And the second file's long taunt, in italics, ending "Good luck," and then "But good luck proving it" again two lines later, is a bit of a movie villain. The chapter's one line does more.

The chapter also has things the second file has nowhere: the cashier, the remorse question, the teacher who caught you stealing, the two buttons, the comment section, the sigh with an actual wife on the other end, the advisor, and "We were never disagreeing about what the ground was. We were fighting over which of us still stood on it." That last one is still the best sentence in either file. And "Love and contempt are addressed to the same place." The second file has nothing as good as either of those.

**Order and length.** Seeing them side by side, the second file's order is better in one place: bad stranger, then good stranger, back to back, on the reader's own memory, before anything else. The chapter's Encounter is a better scene, but the reader is watching you instead of being on the end of it. If the chapter could put the reader on the receiving end for half a page before your scene, it might hit harder. I don't know how you'd do it without losing your scene, and I wouldn't lose your scene.

Length: the second file's middle, The Tell through The Loop, is where it's longest and does the least. That told me something about the chapter too. The chapter's middle, "Whose intention," is where I got tired in the chapter, and it's covering the same ground: was it intentional, whose intention, the villain-or-machine bit. Both versions sag on the same question. That question might need less room than you're giving it.

The second file ends on "It's still there in you, quietly knowing what it knows, patiently waiting to be remembered. Just as it is in him." I liked that. It's quiet, and it ends once, which the chapter doesn't. The chapter ends three times and then gives me an exercise. But the chapter's exercise did more to me than the second file's last line, so I'd still take the chapter's ending, trimmed.

**If I could pass only one.** The chapter, to Kathleen, like I said. It treats her like someone who can find her own answer, and it's a man owning his own stuff, which is what she always wanted from me. The second file I'd give to nobody as it is. But I'd tear out the two strangers, the first three pages, and give them to Carmen Ruiz. She'd read them and say that's the whole job. Then she'd say she's been telling me that since 2014.
