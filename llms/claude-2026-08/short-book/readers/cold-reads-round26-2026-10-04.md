## Gwen (57, retired nurse manager, twenty years in AA, sponsor; cold, the experienced patience)

### Stage 1

Dear Aditya,

I read it at my kitchen table with a legal pad, the way I'd read a sponsee's Fourth Step. I'll tell you where I was as I went, in order. First, so you know where I'm coming from: twenty years sober, a dozen sponsees, a nurse for thirty years before that. I came to your first page ready to be told about the work by somebody who hadn't done it. That isn't what happened, mostly. I'll tell you where it did.

**The memory at the start.** You asked for something I knew was wrong at the time and couldn't admit until later. I didn't have to dig. March 2005, I called in sick to a Saturday shift on 4 North, hungover, and let Darlene Pruitt work a double she'd been dreading because her mother was in hospice. I told myself she'd volunteered. She hadn't. She'd said "if nobody else can."

Your question, "did it feel like new information?", was a fair one and I could answer it. No. When I finally said it out loud in my Fifth Step it was the least surprising thing I've ever said. My sponsor didn't even look up. Then you said "that knowledge was never unconscious." I bought that outright. In the rooms we call it denial, and nobody who has been through it thinks denial is ignorance. You know exactly where the bottle is. You just don't look at the shelf.

"If that doesn't match anything in your memory, the rest of this won't make sense, and I'd rather you know that now." That sentence did more to make me trust you than anything else on the first page. You gave me the door before you asked me to walk in.

"Did some part of you know you were doing that, even as you did it?" Yes. When Darlene called me the next week to say thanks for asking how her mother was, I changed the subject fast. That was not passive. I steered.

"If seeing that makes you want to call yourself names, hold off. That's not owning up; it's another way to hide." Good. I tell sponsees this in nearly the same words. Self-pity and self-loathing are the same coin in our program, and both keep you drinking.

"Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" This is the line where I felt freest. You asked and stopped. I sat with it a while. The answer I came to is the one I suspect you wanted, but I got there myself, and that matters.

**The cashier.** Here's what happened, honestly. I pictured the girl at Rosauers on Thursday, the one with the purple streak who scans everything twice. The smile went right out of me, no reason needed. The sneer went out easily too, at first, and I almost wrote you to say your check failed. Then I caught it. Before the sneer went I'd already thought *she's so slow*. It came so fast I didn't count it as a reason. So I bought your point tentatively. But you should know the reason can arrive so fast that the reader thinks the cruelty needed none. A nurse manager has a lot of "she's slow" ready to hand.

**Remorse.** This is the first place I refused, at least partway. "I've felt remorse for harm I've done, but never for having been decent." I have. I paid my sister Carol's rent three times in 2019 and felt sick about it, that sick-in-the-gut feeling, not just "I got taken." Anyone from Al-Anon would tell you the same. A lot of what looks like decency in a family with a drinker is enabling, and enabling carries remorse.

I kept going, though, and later I'm not sure the refusal holds. When I look at the rent, what I feel sick about isn't the kindness. It's the lie inside it: I was paying so I wouldn't have to say the word "drinking" to her face. That's not decency. That's turning away dressed as decency. So your asymmetry might survive, but you didn't help me get there. "That suggests our moral compass isn't just arbitrary; it only points in one direction" came too fast for anyone who has been around codependency. I could still follow what came after. I just carried a little stone in my shoe for a few pages.

"A bad person who does a secret act of kindness, and then lies awake at night wracked with genuine remorse" made me laugh. No. I can't imagine it either.

**The confrontation.** Believable, yes. More than believable. That's the inside of a head I've lived in. "And he can't prove I'm doing this, can he? No. And crucially, *neither can I*. So I know it's not on purpose." That is drunk logic exactly. *If I can't prove I'm an alcoholic, I'm not one.* I underlined it twice.

Where I skimmed: "Surely that's not what I'm doing. I'm just being fierce with evil, right? Right." and "*Ha!* Classic projection." Those read a little performed. The scene is strongest when it's quiet and ugly, like "Who cares if I give him a little nudge to help him reveal what he truly is?" That one I recognized in my own mouth.

The cold version paragraph saved me. "A quiet filing-away, a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them." I don't do hot. I do bright. My ex-husband Ron gets a bright, pleasant civility that he once called "your customer-service voice." I hadn't connected it to anything until I read that paragraph. Keep it where it is.

**"I'll just go ahead and name what's happening."** Mostly insight, but dense. I had to read "Neither half alone would be enough to get me both his reaction and my innocence" twice, and "The signal is the pretense that there is no signal" three times. The second one I got eventually, and it was worth it.

What caught me in that section was this: "Deny it, and you're the kind of person who does. Get angry, and that's proof too. Stay calm, and you don't even care. Apologize, and it's too convenient." I have been on the receiving end of that. Family meetings in 2004, everything I said got read as more evidence. And then, sitting there, I saw I've done it to Carol. In my house we have a phrase for it: "Denial is a symptom." Anything she says against my verdict proves the verdict. Your sentence about the stories changing and the conclusion staying put describes something my own program's language can be used for. I'm not comfortable with that, and I don't think I was supposed to be.

"He sees that he cannot appeal to my better nature, because I'm convinced this *is* my better nature." Yes.

**The two checks.** "Is there something primally, viscerally satisfying about seeing evil violently punished?" Yes. I watch a certain kind of television for exactly that.

Worst enemy, two buttons. I tried it twice, with two people, and got two different answers. Ron gets the pill. I don't hate Ron anymore; I just don't let him in. Dale, the man my sister lives with, who keeps the vodka stocked and calls her "my little party girl," gets the first button, and my hand moved before I'd finished reading the paragraph. You said "I'll be honest: there is in me." That's the first place I felt you'd put yourself on the table, and I liked you better for it.

"Right?" on its own line felt like a push. Then "That lie is exactly what lets it act, in ways I almost never recognize." I let it go, because by then I believed you.

**The part with "goals of its own."** I bought it immediately, and I'll tell you why, because you may not know. We talk this way all the time. "My disease is doing push-ups in the parking lot." "It's cunning, baffling, powerful." "It wants me dead, and it's patient." Nobody in the rooms thinks there's a little man in there. We think that when you stop watching, your own mind behaves as though there were one.

I braced, though, because this is exactly where people use the disease as an alibi. "I didn't design the setup" made me tense up; it sounded like the start of an excuse. Then: "None of this lets me off the hook. It's my mind, and I answer for what it does." That came in time, just. One sentence later and I'd have put the pages down. It's very close to how we say it: you're not responsible for having the disease, you're responsible for your recovery.

"When I'm sure someone was fully aware of what they were doing, I've made them a villain. When I'm sure they had no idea, I've made them a machine." That's the best thing in the chapter for me. I do both. Carol is a villain on Thanksgiving and a machine ("it's the disease talking") by Christmas, and either way I'm not sitting across from my sister.

"I do it because I believe there *are* 'bad people'... I fear I might be one of them." For a drunk this isn't speculation. Every one of us was afraid of exactly that, and most of us were sure of it for a while.

**"What we most fundamentally are."** I granted it as your claim; I didn't find it in myself, not at first. My tradition goes the other way. The Big Book says self is the problem, and the help comes from a Power greater than ourselves. So when you say the better nature is what we "most fundamentally are," part of me heard self-help, the "you are enough" posters in the treatment-center hallway. It did sound a little grand.

What changed it some was your question just before: "And when you finally owned up, did it feel like losing something of yourself, or like getting something back?" Getting something back. My amends to Darlene in 2007, in the parking garage at Sacred Heart, felt like getting myself back. I'd say God gave it back. You'd say it was there all along. I'm not sure those are as far apart as they sound, and I wasn't sure of that before I read this.

"They say integrity is what you do when no one is watching... But someone always is: me." We say "we're only as sick as our secrets." Yours is better, because it says who the secret is kept from.

"As best I can tell, the only solution is to stop turning away." This is where I felt pushed. "Stop turning away" is "stop drinking." True, and nobody ever got sober on it. You do come back to that later ("It takes work"), and I was glad, but here it sounded like the finish line given as directions.

**The person who corrected me.** My first sponsor, Bev Kowalski, in 2005. I'd slipped and told her I hadn't. She said, "You're lying to me, Gwen, and I'm not going anywhere." Not gentle. She was angry. But I didn't dig in. I cried in her Buick. Your sentence, "It feels like a reminder—*you're better than this*," is exactly it. She took my indignation away from me because she gave me nothing to be indignant about.

**The time I corrected someone badly.** 2011. I dressed down a new grad, Kayla, at the nurses' station over a heparin drip, in front of two techs. The error was real; the audience was for me. By 2016 I'd do the same correction in my office with the door shut, and fiercer about the drip. The fierceness stayed and the audience went. "Remove it, and the fierceness stays; only the poison goes." I believe that from my own record.

**Common knowledge.** I followed it, mostly. "Hypocrisy only makes sense between two people who answer to the same thing" is the line the whole section hangs on, and it held. The "each knows that the other knows that the other knows, etc." I didn't follow and didn't need to.

What surprised me was your question: "In the opening, were you surprised that I knew you needed limbo to cause harm? If not, why not?" I wasn't surprised. I'd never noticed I wasn't. That stopped me for a good minute.

"I treat my conscience like an advisor offering an opinion." Yes. And "But it's not an advisor." In the rooms we say "my best thinking got me here." Your line about being "just another person who knew what was right, decided it was negotiable, and then looked for someone worse than me" is a fair description of every resentment on my Fourth Step.

**Where contempt reached me.** 2006, detox at the old Deaconess, a resident looked at my chart, looked at me, and said "Again?" That's all. It didn't reach my pride. My pride had been gone for a year. It reached right where you say, the part of me that still knew I counted. I've hated that young man for twenty years. Possibly more than he deserves.

**The last time I sent the look.** Thanksgiving 2025. Carol poured a second glass of wine and I put my fork down and didn't say anything. Everybody at the table read it. My niece looked at her plate. Carol said, "Say it, Gwen." I said, "I didn't say anything."

Your questions: "Did you know they'd read it? Did you mean them to know you meant it, while giving yourself an 'out'? Did you count on their seeing that too, so they couldn't call it a slip, or name it without sounding crazy?" Yes. Yes. And yes. "I didn't say anything" was the out, and she knew it was the out, and she couldn't do anything with it. She hasn't invited me to a holiday since. I had filed that under "detaching with love." It's not on my amends list. I have been very proud of my amends list.

I had to get up and walk around the kitchen after that.

"Love and contempt are addressed to the same place." I wrote that on the pad.

**The second half of Common knowledge** is where you lost me a little. "That's why, although it's normally presumptuous to assume things about your mind, this one feels like safe ground." You'd already earned the ground. Saying it's safe made me wonder. "Here's something you can check: in moments where you're feeling fully whole" is one check too many; I didn't try it. The gaze check I'd already done with the detox resident. It felt like you asking twice. "It's always the one that wants to excuse my contempt." "Always" pushed. And "What I was displaying through my eyes was the very essence of cruelty—the thing I hate most in this world" was too big. I'd rather you'd told me whose face you were looking at. That's my one real complaint about the whole chapter: you take your inventory, but there are no names in it. "My enemy" is everyone and nobody. A Fourth Step without names isn't a Fourth Step. The button was the one place you were concrete about yourself, and it was the place I trusted you most.

**The spread.** It caught me. "The kid who watches him do it." I learned the silence at my mother's table. She did it to my father over his beer every night for twenty years, and I watched, and now my niece watches me do it to her mother. I'd never put those two dinners side by side.

"I send it to people I love... The sigh that tells them what I really think of them, dressed up to make me seem like the reasonable one." That's Carol again.

Then this: "The subtlest version I know is the one I feel toward people who haven't seen through this trap yet. It can look like patience, or even like care." Aditya, a woman I sponsored, Jen, fired me last year. She said I made her feel "like a case." I told myself she wasn't ready. I have a warm, slow, experienced voice I use on newcomers who I've privately decided aren't serious, and I've always known part of it was a performance. I did not know until I read that line that it was the same thing as the fork going down at Thanksgiving. It is. It's a verdict with good manners.

The ending is right. "What I need in order to look is what my enemy needed from me, and what the corrector with integrity gave me: to be seen as more than what I do." That's where it should stop, and it does. It brings me back to Bev in the Buick, which is where I'd want to be left.

**Length.** It drags in the back half of Common knowledge, from "How is it possible that we all know this about one another?" to the gaze check. That's where it gets said twice: that the better nature is the deepest thing, that you see it rather than guess it. "Notice how precise the trap is" also goes back over ground the Corruption section had already covered. If I had to cut a fifth: the "fully whole" check, the gaze check, the "safe ground" paragraph, and some of "Notice how precise the trap is." I'd keep every check that asked for a person and a day.

**Words.** "Evil" wore on me. You use it lightly sometimes and heavily other times, and I couldn't always tell which. "Rot" in the italic speech was fine. "The very essence of cruelty," as I said. "Limbo" I liked; it's the right word and it isn't ours, which helped me hear it fresh. "Better nature" I have; it's Lincoln, and my father liked Lincoln.

**Last.** Was it obvious? A lot of it. Denial, resentment, secrets, "my best thinking," the disease with goals of its own. I've heard those in church basements for twenty years. What wasn't obvious was where you pointed them. The program taught me to see all this in myself when I'm the one drinking. It didn't teach me to see it in myself when I'm the one sober and sure, looking across the table at the one who isn't. That's newly consequential. Carol's silence isn't detachment. It goes on the list, and I'll call her this week. I don't know what I'll say yet, but I know I won't put my fork down while I say it.

Would I pass it on? Yes, to Linda Haugen, who's in my Al-Anon home group and has the same silence for her son that I have for Carol. I'd tell her to skip the second half of Common knowledge if it bogs her down and go straight to "The spread." I'd hold off with my newest sponsee; she needs the first three Steps, not this, and some of the confrontation would scare her. And I'd want Jen to read it, which I won't send her, because sending it would be the look.

Thank you. This one cost me something, which is how I know it was worth reading.

Gwen Albright
Spokane

"I can't sincerely hold against him evidence that I know I planted. Conscious yet unaware: that's the limbo state." (second file). Clearer than the chapter's "I can't have masterminded a plan in which two aspects of my mind coordinate so precisely to get me what I need," which, as I said, made me tense up because it sounded like the start of an alibi. "It's doing the same thing in him, and each of us is sure we're free of it." (second file). Carol thinks I'm the sick one. She told my niece I'm "drunk on the program." I hadn't thought about that as the same thing running in both of us till this line.

## Ibrahim (44, family therapist with a Sufi practice; cold, the gentle knowing smile)

### Stage 1

Dear Aditya,

I read the chapter at my kitchen table after the kids went to bed, with a pen, the way I read anything that claims to know something about the self. You should know my bias going in. I've done muhasaba, the examination of the self, for about twenty years under a teacher in my order, and I've been a family therapist for almost as long. "It was right there to see—fully available to consciousness—but you were *turned away* from it." Yes. My tradition would say the nafs had its hand over my eyes, and that my hand was on top of its hand.

"Any time someone came close to exposing it... did you find a way to deflect? If you did, did that happen passively?" Not passively. When Maryam said "Your father asked for you," I changed the subject to our daughter's school. I remember doing it. So: bought.

Then this line, which I underlined twice: "If seeing that makes you want to call yourself names, hold off. That's not owning up; it's another way to hide."
