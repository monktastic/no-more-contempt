## Tomás (55, hospice chaplain in San Antonio; Ignatian Examen; Catholic)

Aditya,

I read it twice: once straight through, and once with a pen, stopping at each question to do what you asked. You should know who's reading. I'm a hospice chaplain. I've prayed the Examen most nights for thirty years and I give the Exercises to people in daily life. So a lot of this was familiar ground to me. What I want to tell you is where it was more than familiar.

**What happened, in order**

The opening didn't move me much. It's a thesis, and I've heard people promise to show me evil is an absence since my novitiate. I held it the way you asked. I didn't buy it and I didn't refuse it.

The Limbo section took me straight to a memory. I'll give you the real one, since you asked for real ones.

Six years ago a man named Arturo Saldívar was dying on our unit. I'd sat with him most of the afternoon. My son Mateo called about his car, and I stepped out to the stairwell for maybe twenty minutes. Arturo died in those twenty minutes. That evening his daughter Marisol took my hands and asked, "Was someone with him?" I said, "Yes. I was with him."

I knew it was wrong as the words left my mouth. That knowledge came before any reasoning; my stomach knew first. The reasons came after, and they came quickly. *She needs this. He was unresponsive anyway. The dying often wait until the room is empty; we tell families that all the time.* That last one is true, which is what made it so useful. Your line "Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" stopped me cold, because the part that knew never offered me a single argument. It just sat there.

Then "Any time someone came close to exposing it..." Here is what embarrasses me as a man of the Examen. I prayed every night for months after that. And every night I lingered in the first movement, the gratitude. I found a great deal to be grateful for. I would get drowsy before I reached the review of the day. Did some part of me know I was doing that? Yes. It's the oldest trick there is, and I teach people to watch for it. Ignatius says the enemy behaves like a false lover who wants the affair kept secret. I've explained that rule to a hundred retreatants and I couldn't see myself doing it. I only told it in confession the next spring. I have never told Marisol. Your chapter has me asking again whether I should.

Could I have said it without the justifications? No. "They wait until you leave" was the thing that made the lie sayable.

Remorse in reverse: I hesitated at "Can you imagine even the worst person feeling it over that?" I have sat with old men who were ashamed of a decency. One was a rancher, Mr. Ochoa, ashamed that he'd held his grown son and cried at the boy's wedding. But when I listened longer, that was shame before his dead father's eyes, not remorse. It wasn't the sickening sense of having gone against himself. So the check held, but a chaplain will reach for that counterexample, and it took me a minute to sort it out. That was my first hesitation. It wasn't a refusal.

**The two strangers**

The first stranger: twelve years ago I reached a death forty minutes late because I'd been paged to the wrong floor. The attending, in front of the family, said, "This is why nobody takes chaplains seriously." He wasn't talking about my lateness. He was talking about what I am. I dug in immediately. I blamed the paging system out loud, and I spent a week building the case. And yes, "I'd take that bet at any odds." I was sure he was a man who cut corners and called it efficiency. I had no evidence at all. I'd met him twice.

The second stranger: my novice master, Fr. Walsh. In a manifestation of conscience I was telling him a story about my own humility, and he cut me off. "You're telling me a story where you come off well. You're better than that, and you know it." That was far harsher than the doctor. I went red. And I didn't dig in at all. I laughed, a bad, surprised laugh, and told him the true version. "Not more gently" is exactly right. People confuse that kind of harshness with cruelty, and it isn't. He was harsh, and it was the kindest thing anyone did for me that year.

The question that ends the section ("What is that thing whose side you are both on") is good. You didn't answer it, and I'm glad.

**The Encounter, and my own look**

This is where the chapter stopped being familiar.

My brother-in-law Ray is third-generation, same as me. His grandfather crossed at Piedras Negras. He forwards memes to the family chat about "illegals." At Easter he said, "I'm not against Mexicans, I'm against illegals," across the table from Rosa's mother, who crossed in 1971. I gave him the look. Then I said, "Your abuelo would be proud of you." He got louder. He doubled down, and I felt exactly what you describe: "a brief flicker of satisfaction, vindication, even relief." *There it is.* I left early and told Rosa I couldn't be around that. In the car she said, "You enjoyed that." I said she was wrong. She wasn't.

Your monologue is the best thing in the chapter. "Sure, it may be a bit dark, but it's the *good* kind of darkness—the kind that fights the real kind" is precisely the sentence I would never say aloud and say inwardly all the time. "I wasn't *exposing* his darkness. I was creating more of it." That one went in. And this did more than either:

"If I'd looked carefully, I'd have seen my role in causing it. So I didn't—and that *was* my role in causing it."

That's the Examen in reverse. It's what I was doing with Marisol, aimed outward.

**The buttons**

Mr. Kessler is our administrator. He cut the chaplaincy budget by a third and called it "right-sizing." My hand went to the vengeance button, and I'll be specific about what I imagined. Him in a bed on our unit, with no chaplain, because of course there wouldn't be one. I'm not proud of how quickly that picture came. And when I heard in the cafeteria last spring that his regional promotion had gone to someone else, I enjoyed it. It did nothing for a single patient. The pleasure was in the punishment.

With Ray I went to the pill fast. Then I distrusted that. I wanted the pill because I want a different brother-in-law. I don't want to deal with this one. A heart changed without consent isn't a conversion; it's a replacement. So the button test is a little rigged for someone like me. The "good" button can be contempt too: *I'd rather you were someone else.* You may want that, or you may not. It caught me either way.

**The Corruption**

"Whatever your wrong was, it needed an exception" is sharp and true. My lie to Marisol needed *this is pastoral, so the rule doesn't bind me here.*

"Evil behavior is unconscious in me, and fully aware in my enemies. That's why I deserve understanding, and they deserve contempt." That is my exact double standard. I give Kessler no "unconscious." I give myself all of it.

"This was intentional, and done in limbo." I bought this because of the Examen business. Something in me steered the prayer away from the stairwell for months. It was skilled. It knew where the stairwell was.

Then there's the long paragraph that starts "It depends on a kind of implicit common knowledge," and here is my first refusal. "\"Good\" has no fixed content." No. I'm a Catholic who reads Aquinas; I can't follow you there, and I don't think you need me to. Your real point, and it's a beautiful one, is that I already trust Ray's better nature without knowing what it would do. When Ray's conscience seems to point at something terrible, I conclude he's been fooled, not that his conscience is bad. That's true of me, and it's a deep observation. It doesn't require that good has no content. It only requires that I don't know the content in advance for him, or for me tomorrow. Say that, and you lose no Catholic, no Muslim, no Kantian. Say what you said, and half your contemplative readers will stop and argue, and they'll miss the trust point, which is the best thing in the paragraph. I could follow after the refusal, because the trust point doesn't stand on it. But I was arguing with you for the next page.

"The signal is the pretense that there is no signal." That is the angel of light in Ignatius's second-week rules, said in nine words. Keep it.

**Where contempt reached me**

In 1987, in an H-E-B in Laredo, a man told me to go back to Mexico. I was born in Laredo. I was twenty, in a Jesuit novitiate, quoting Augustine to myself. It didn't touch my pride much. Pride I could handle; I had a vocation and an education. It went in further, to the place that knows I count. I've felt it for almost forty years.

Then I read "**Love and contempt are addressed to the same place.**" and I thought about Ray. My "your abuelo would be proud" went to the same place in Ray that the man at the H-E-B aimed at in me. That's the paragraph I'd fight for. For a chaplain it's also simply true at bedsides. The dying hear contempt and love in the same place, and that place is still there at the end when almost nothing else is.

**The Way Out, and whether the picture came together**

It came together, and I can tell you where. "Badness is the result of turning away, not its cause." I've held that since I was nineteen, as doctrine. "It's an absence that behaves like a presence" is Augustine with Ignatius's enemy-commander welded on, and the welding is the useful part. Augustine alone lets you stop looking, because it's "only" a privation. Ignatius alone gets you a real enemy out there to fight. You have it right that the second one, believed wrongly, makes more of it.

What was new to me was the second claim, that believing in evil essences reliably produces more evil. I believed that one on the strength of Easter, not of the argument.

The hostage line is the strongest image in the chapter: "a hostage held up to the window, not a monster on display. My contempt is one of the hands holding him there." I'll tell you why. Three years ago I sat with a man who had abused his daughters. Everyone on the unit knew. In his last week, with almost no voice left, he asked me twice whether they would come. They didn't. I've never had words for what I saw in him, and that's it: something under crushing load, still showing through. I had been one of the hands. I gave him the minimum.

"Neither of us ever gets both views of the same moment, so each of us is accurate about the other and blind about ourselves." True, and well said.

The fear paragraph ("I fear I might be one of them"): you asked for one memory where that's plainly true. Here's mine. My father used to say *mojados* about the men who came after us. I polished my English and my Latin and went to the Jesuits, and I've never once said that word. But my contempt for Ray is partly that he's the cruder version of my father's climb, and I'm the refined version. I need him to be a different kind of thing. That's why I hit him so hard at Easter. I wouldn't have seen that without your sentence. I'm still not comfortable with it.

The "grammar" paragraph is excellent for an Examen person: "Look at the grammar you used: you hid it from *yourself*." Who was the self I hid the stairwell from? Not a part. The one who prays.

The next paragraph, the one that starts "Here's what I make of that," hedges where it should land. "I'm not claiming there's a thing in there you could point to" is answering a philosopher who isn't in the room. You already have "it's the one that's home." Stop there.
