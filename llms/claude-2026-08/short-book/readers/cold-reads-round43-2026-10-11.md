Replace the chapter's "The harm I cause isn't physical—at least, not yet." I said cut that paragraph. This is what it should have been. It goes right before "It's as if I'm saying to him:".

The chapter did more because it begins where I can check: my own wrong, my own memory. Darnell's grade came up in the first minute. The second file begins with mechanism and a long monologue. Then, a hundred lines in, it says "There's something strange going on here—and the easiest way to show it is by example." It brings in Limbo as the example, and then says "Okay, back to the scenario." By then I had been told a great deal and shown much less. In a classroom that order loses the back two rows.

The opening exercise of the second file is beautiful, though, and for a sitter it nearly works on its own: "Imagine looking into a stranger's eyes long enough that their face stops being something you're looking at and becomes a place somebody is looking out of." I know that shift. On a course we keep our eyes down for ten days. I did the second half with Moss, and I could not find the recognition. Then the file said "It's harder, isn't it?" Yes, it was. But I resented being told, and the chapter never did that to me.

The second file also treats me worse in its voice. "here's the thing," "I want to be crystal clear here," "Classic." And "I don't think anybody, underneath, believes their contempt is righteous" is a claim about everybody. I can check it in myself, and I half agree, but I cannot check it in anybody else.

It also makes a promise in the middle of the argument, "We'll see later where it actually comes from." It sent me looking forward instead of inward. And "All harm begins by *turning away* from others. I can't prove this to you, but I invite you to check it yourself." At least it says so. But it's the root claim of the whole book, and it arrives bare. The chapter gets to the same place through the remorse asymmetry, which I could check. That's better.

**What the chapter should take**

First, and most important, this:

"For now, notice this: flashing someone your inner darkness can be frightening or off-putting, but it usually isn't *maddening* in a way that produces defiance. For that, we must also telegraph the fact that we think it's *righteous.*"

and later, "If the hiding stops there, we call it *malice.*"

This fixes the error I marked in the chapter. There you claimed the contempt "stung" and called that proof, after saying the second stranger could sting more. The second file names the real difference. It isn't the pain. It's the claim of righteousness carried along with the darkness. That's why Emeka's jokes make me dig in, and why a man who simply hated me would not. One sentence of this in "The Encounter," or in place of "That mine stung is proof," and the argument closes. Second:

This is the demonstration the chapter only asserts. In the chapter you wrote "My stories kept changing; my conclusion never did," and then "A merely unconscious process doesn't do that." That I recognize. It is how I argue with Grace about Moss. Put these three sentences into "Look back at the encounter and watch it work," and cut the general claim.

Third, the self-contempt paragraph, and especially this line: "Nobody ever looked honestly at something in themselves while being told they were worthless for having it." The second file puts it right after the memory. I'd put that one sentence wherever the chapter first turns the reader on himself hard. That's the exception paragraph, or right after "What I've been hiding." Leave the closing version where it is, shorter.

Fourth, smaller: "If not, I have no argument to offer here. Read on, and see whether anything else fits." The chapter has nothing that lets a reader off the hook like that. This is the honest line of the second file, and a reader who doesn't recognize the hot form will trust you more for it.

Fifth, maybe: "As far as I've seen, everything I can accomplish *with* the darkness can be accomplished better without it." The chapter says something similar ("it would have worked better"), but the second file says it as your finding, "as far as I've seen." That marking matters to me. It's the difference between a theorem and a conjecture with good evidence.

And this, which I liked as a concession: "Maybe there's no part of them that cares, but maybe they just no longer have access to it. Either way, the state looks essentially the same." The chapter's version of the dehumanizing-gaze question ends "Why would they have to turn away from anything, if what they are is essentially bad?" That's sharper, but it presumes its answer. What I would not take: "limbo contains knowledge of itself, in limbo." It's clever, and I'd enjoy it in the appendix. The two strangers. The second file has nothing like them, and they are the best teaching device in either version. Mr. Lucas did more to make the argument real for me than any paragraph of analysis.

The monologue. The chapter's is half the length and just as true. The second file's version has more jokes. "*Ha!* Like I said: projecting. Classic." That is accurate to the mind, but it plays to the audience.

The ending. The second file ends on "I might even have an inkling of what that way is," which is a tease. The chapter answers its own question. Better.

**Did it change my cuts?**

Yes, three ways.

I had said to move the cold-contempt paragraph out of the Encounter. The second file shows me why it's there: for the reader who never does the hot form. Dear Aditya,

Third, the honest limit. "It does not get you that care is what awareness *is*." In my first letter I refused the chapter's "It's what I am in the absence of all self-deception" and "the one that's home."
