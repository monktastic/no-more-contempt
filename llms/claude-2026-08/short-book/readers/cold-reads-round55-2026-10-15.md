# Round 55 (October 15): Aditya's rewritten Chapter 1, three readers

*Three cold readers read only Chapter 1 as committed at 62aa7b3 (the layered confession, The How, The Mechanism, the condensed Way Out). Brief: section by section; whether the turn lands and the Way Out follows from it; every sentence they had to reread; contradictions; questions they didn't have and ones left unanswered; repetition; would they give it to someone they care about. Letters verbatim.*

## Lena (school librarian; nine years of Mahamudra)

Dear Aditya,

Steady, and devoured from The Encounter through the end of The Mechanism. My attention went slack twice. The first time was the opening paragraph of The How, where "better nature" arrives as a defined term and I felt myself being handed a concept instead of finding one. The second was the first paragraph of The Orientation, which is dense enough that I read it three times. If I were going to put it down, it would have been there, near the end. I didn't.

I did the exercises. Here's what I used, briefly, so you know where my verdict comes from.

The wrong I knew at the time: during my divorce I told our friends a version in which my ex had "checked out." I left out that I had stopped wanting him to come back a year before he left. I knew it was a lie of omission while I was telling it. My reasons were ready: everyone tells their side, he'd tell worse, it's nobody's business. When I finally admitted it to myself, years later on a retreat, it was not new information. It felt exactly like your word, "limbo." So the opening worked on me.

The remorse-in-reverse question: I once paid his half of our son's orthodontist bill without telling anyone, and later lay awake over it. I looked at what that was made of, as you asked. It was resentment and feeling like a fool, which is your list. That was a little uncanny. It also made me feel slightly steered, because your list had already answered for me. I'll come back to that.

The buttons: with the loud parent from our school board meetings, the one who reads passages from library books into the microphone in a mocking voice, I'd press the pill and mean it. With my ex, there was an itch for the first button. It was small, but it was there, and I was glad you said "there is in me" before I had to admit it.

**SECTION BY SECTION**

**Limbo.** This did the most work on me. The questions left room. "Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" stopped me for a long time. One sentence I couldn't parse is listed under Confusions.

**The Two Strangers.** It's clear, and I recognized both strangers. The second one is the best thing in the chapter for me, because "he could have been *harsher*" refuses the soft reading I half expected. My teacher has been the second stranger to me more than once.

**The Encounter.** I laughed at "it's the *good* kind of darkness—the kind that fights the real kind!" and then stopped laughing, because that's exactly what I tell myself at board meetings. The button exercise and the question about watching someone get what was coming to him both hit. I watched that parent get voted down in May and the pleasure was not only in the books staying on the shelves. The parenthetical about abusers is right and well placed.

**The How.** The paragraph beginning "If you've been on his end, you know how it closes around you" is the truest description of my marriage's last year I've read. It also made me uneasy about the book's own shape, which I'll say more about below. The final irony, "Evil behavior is unconscious in me, and fully aware in my enemies," landed.

**The Mechanism.** It's the intellectual centre, and also the place my attention flagged and then came back hardest. "Love and contempt are addressed to the same place" is the sentence I'll keep. The layered confession is discussed under The Turn.

**The Way Out.** It's partly a return to my own memory, which works, and partly metaphysics. More under The Turn.

**The Spread.** This hurt the most, and I needed it. "The sigh that tells them what I really think of them" is something I do to my son over homework. I hadn't counted it as contempt. Now I do. The staff-room debrief after a board meeting is your "I send it *about* people, to my allies." The last two paragraphs are generous to the reader and to yourself at once.

**The Orientation.** "Underneath the argument, my enemy and I were never fighting over whether a shared ground exists. We were fighting over which of us still stood on it." That's a good ending. The first paragraph is too compressed to earn it.

**MAJOR OBJECTIONS**

1. **The innocence sentence, for a badly hurt reader (The Mechanism).** You write of your own state: "And yet, in another sense, it was utterly innocent." Two paragraphs later: "I now think that "pure evil" is this error at maximum intensity". A reader who was abused will do that arithmetic in a second. If pure evil is this error, and this error was "utterly innocent" in a sense, then the man who hurt her was utterly innocent in a sense. You don't say that, and "It must be fought, with everything I have" pushes against it. But the word "innocent" sits close enough to "pure evil" that I would not hand this page to a friend of mine who survived her father without warning her first. I'm not saying you're wrong. I'm saying a hurt reader may stop there and not read the next sentence.

2. **"Both would have recognized the badness" (The Orientation).** "The first stranger's allies would have called what he did good, and his enemies would have called it evil, and both would have recognized the badness underneath it." I've sat in a room of people cheering contempt for my side, and I'm not sure they recognized anything. Maybe you mean they knew it in limbo. If so, say so, because as written it's asserted about people I've watched. Everywhere else you ask me to check my own case. Here you tell me about other people's insides. It's the one place I stopped trusting you for a moment.

3. **The funnel (The How, and the book around it).** You describe the trap perfectly: "Deny it, and you're the kind of person who does. Get angry, and that's proof too. Stay calm, and you don't even care." Then the chapter does something similar to me, gently. If I deny the turning away, that's turning away. If I want to call it unconscious, you've told me I "desperately want to." If I'd keep the justifying part, well, who would? The question "which would you keep?" in The Way Out has one answer. I don't think this is fatal, because you include yourself every time, and the remorse list says "Mine turned out to be." But a reader who distrusts teachers will feel every exit closed, and in my sangha that reader exists.

**THE TURN**

Yes, it landed, and I believed you. The layers are the right form. They're how looking actually goes on the cushion: the first thing you see is the story, then something less flattering under it, then something worse, and the last layer doesn't feel like a discovery so much as a recognition.

The third layer is the one I found in myself: "I wanted him to *suffer,* but in a way that nobody could accuse me of." That's my ex, exactly. The fourth layer, "I needed others to carry the darkness I was hiding from myself," I believe as your report. I can't yet say I find it in me, and the chapter leaves me room not to, which I appreciated.

It changed how I read The Encounter. On first reading The Encounter is almost comic. After the turn, "the *good* kind of darkness" isn't funny. I went back and read the whole section again in a different key.

One sentence in the turn I couldn't follow: "And how better to do that to him than to show him that I was doing it, while hiding it from myself?" See Confusions.

The Way Out: half of it follows and half is a change of subject. The opening paragraph follows beautifully. You send me back to my own memory and ask whether owning up felt like losing something or getting something back. It felt like getting something back. "If it's something I *do*, then I can stop" and the closing question, "which part of me needs there to be one," follow directly from your confession. They're a practice, and I'll use it.

What doesn't follow is the paragraph that starts "But that doesn't make evil just nothing." I had just been invited to stop. Suddenly I was reading about whether evil is an absence or a presence and how it "tracks, it aims, it fights to stay hidden." It felt like a different book arriving. Speaking as a Buddhist, it's a real question and I care about it. But I hadn't asked it yet, and here it interrupts the way out.

**CONFUSIONS**

- Limbo: "picture someone in front of you, fully in view, and see whether using them will go." I think "will go" means "whether you can go through with it." It read like a typo the first time.
- The How: "Watch how it accomplishes its goal." The nearest "it" is "that need," so the need has a goal. The next paragraph's "It shows him, through my tone and my gaze" took me two reads to attach to the need, not to "my enemy" or "the darkness."
- The How: "Part of what it sees in others is that they, too, can only cause harm by *turning away*. When I turn away from mine, other people look defective." "It" is the better nature and "mine" is my better nature. I got there, but slowly.
- The Mechanism: "I am in limbo about provoking him, and revealing that I am in limbo is itself the provocation. The signal is the pretense that there is no signal." I reread this three times. I think it means that what provokes him isn't the look itself but his seeing that I won't admit the look.
- The Mechanism: "What I've been hiding isn't that I *could* do what the "bad people" do. It's that I'm *already doing it*, by using them as a place to hide the fact that I am." The end loops back on itself. I think it means that I'm doing harm, and the harm I'm doing is using them to hide that I do harm. It's circular on purpose, maybe, but it read as a knot.
- The Mechanism: "And how better to do that to him than to show him that I was doing it, while hiding it from myself?" Do what? Make him carry my darkness? I think it means that showing him my contempt while denying it is how the darkness transfers to him. But "do that to him" has no clear antecedent.
- The Mechanism: "The problem is, it's never truly undetected. I now recognize this as the great lie we tell ourselves." "This" grammatically points at "it's never truly undetected," which is the truth, not the lie. I think the lie is that it goes undetected.
- The Way Out: "So whether or not I can ever know, that belief isn't helping me." Know what? I think it means "whether bad people have a bad essence."
- The Orientation: "Even when it's pointed away from goodness, cut loose from the part that knows, it can prove that it isn't." Isn't what? Pointed away? I think so.

**CONTRADICTIONS**

- **Parts.** The How: "I call that deepest part of myself my *better nature*." The Way Out: "My better nature isn't one part among others; it's what's left when the hiding stops." Also The Way Out: "you hid it from *yourself*, not from some part of you." But Limbo was built on parts: "The part that knew might not have been able to say how it knew. Meanwhile, another part was busy providing reasons." And The Way Out itself asks me to keep "only one of the two parts." I can see you're moving from parts to something that isn't a part. That's the Mahamudra move, and I welcome it. But the chapter calls it a part and then says it isn't one, without marking the shift.
- **"Better nature," "innocent part," "the *you* that knows you count," and what The Way Out says you call "I".** Are these one thing? "I was knowingly smothering his innocent part" (The Mechanism) introduces "innocent part" once and never again. I assume it's the better nature, but a new name in the middle of the confession made me wonder whether it's something else.
- **Evil as error and evil as agent.** The Mechanism: "pure evil" is "this error at maximum intensity." The Way Out: "Badness is the result of turning away, not its cause," yet evil "tracks, it aims, it fights to stay hidden." An error that aims? I can hold both: an absence that behaves like a presence. My sangha will too, since it's close to how we talk about ignorance. But a reader without that background will see a contradiction.
- **"Darkness."** In The Encounter, it's something I have: "I don't even want to believe I have that kind of darkness in me." In The Way Out, it's something I do: "But if it's something I *do*, then I can stop." I take the second as the correction of the first, but nothing tells me it is.

**ANSWERS TO QUESTIONS I DIDN'T HAVE**

- The Mechanism: "That in no way excuses me. I know I'm still fully responsible for all the harm I've caused from this confusion." I hadn't thought you were excusing yourself. The disclaimer made me wonder who you were defending against.
- The Way Out: "But that doesn't make evil just nothing. Call it nothing, and I'll underestimate it." As above, I hadn't asked.
- The Spread: "*That wasn't the real me* can be used as an excuse, but I also think it's true." I didn't raise that objection either, though I liked the line.
- The Spread: "Sometimes it looks like me writing this book *at* people, instead of *for* them." This is honest, but it pre-empts a complaint I hadn't made. Actually I had half made it, in objection 3 above, so maybe it's earned.

**QUESTIONS I HAD THAT WENT UNANSWERED**

- The Mechanism: "It must be fought, with everything I have." How do I fight the parent at the microphone without contempt? The second stranger is the picture, but in The Two Strangers he catches me doing wrong. I'm not sure what he looks like in a public meeting, facing someone I think is harming children. I know it's chapter one.
- The Encounter: "The other gives them a pill that instantly changes their heart." Changes it to what? Toward me? Toward the good? I pressed the pill for the parent partly because I imagined her agreeing with me. That isn't the same as wanting her heart changed, and I'd like the exercise to catch that.
- Limbo: "The part that knew may not have won the argument, but it never budged, either." But The Encounter says my justifications come to feel "as though they *are* my conscience. The two get just a little harder to tell apart." If the knowing part never budges, how does it get harder to tell apart? I suspect the answer is that it doesn't budge but gets harder to hear. I'd like to be told.

**REPETITION**

- A wordless signal, said twice in one paragraph (The Mechanism): "And because what it means is already known to both of us, it needs no words." Then: "A wordless signal only works if both parties already know what it means."
- Right there to see. Limbo: "It was right there to see—fully available to consciousness—you were just *turned away* from it." The Mechanism: "It had always been right there for me to see—I had just been turned away from it." The second is a deliberate callback and it works, but then The Mechanism does it a third time: "The horror is right there for them to see, the way mine was for me and yours was for you."
- Intentional in limbo. Limbo: "notice how intentional behavior can take place in limbo, too." The How: "It was intentional, but done in limbo." The Mechanism: "It looks intentional, just as mine did, because in an important sense, it is."
- Turning away to cause harm. The How: "that's why I must *turn away* from it to cause harm," then "they, too, can only cause harm by *turning away*." The Mechanism: "beings who must *turn away* to cause harm." Three statements; the third felt like a restatement.
- Deniability. The Encounter: "he can't prove I'm doing this, can he? No. And *neither can I*." The Mechanism: "it's all plausibly deniable, *even to myself.*" These are close, but the second adds "That's not an accident," so I'd keep both.
- "Unconscious." Limbo: "We like to call such knowledge "unconscious," but what you just recalled wasn't." The How: "I desperately want to call this behavior _unconscious_." The second applies the first to the author, which is fine.

**WOULD I GIVE IT TO SOMEONE I CARE ABOUT?**

Yes, to most of my sangha, as it stands. They'll do the exercises and they'll recognize turning away as what we already call ignoring. The Spread alone is worth it.

I would not give it, as it stands, to my friend who survived her father. Before I would, two things have to change. First, the "utterly innocent" sentence needs to be held further from "pure evil," or made explicitly about you alone. Second, "both would have recognized the badness" needs to become something you can show rather than tell. I'd also move the paragraph that starts "But that doesn't make evil just nothing" out of The Way Out. It belongs later, once someone has asked.

Thank you for this. I went to a board meeting in my head while reading it, and for the first time I noticed that I wanted that woman to see me despising her. I'm still sitting with it.

Lena

## Tony (HVAC business owner; no practice)

Aditya,

Steady, and devoured in two places. It went slack in the first half of "The Mechanism," where you get into who knows that who knows. **SECTION BY SECTION**

**THE TURN**

It mostly landed, and I believed you, mostly because of the word "malevolent." No preacher says that about himself. The first three layers I could follow in my own case with Dennis. Righteous first. Then knowing I was being a bit of a bastard about it, but he had it coming. Then "I wanted him to *suffer,* but in a way that nobody could accuse me of." I can't find it in me yet, and the scare quotes around "victim" made me squint. Then "I was knowingly smothering his innocent part—a part that, for all I knew, could still be rescued." This guy had just been cruel to somebody, and the word "innocent" is a lot to ask. "Rescued" is a preacher's word.

Did it change how I read what came before? Yes. Once you'd confessed it, the Encounter read less like a clever bit and more like a guy telling on himself. I trusted the earlier sections more looking back.

Did The Way Out follow? The second half did. The first paragraph felt like a change of subject. You'd just been telling me about Dennis-type stuff, and suddenly it's grammar and what I call "I." "What you hid it from is what you call "I" when nothing is being hidden." That's where it started sounding like your meditation group and not like you and me. **2. Section by section**

**Limbo.** My case: in grad school I dropped two participants after seeing they killed the effect, and wrote the exclusion rule afterward. "Everyone does it" was close to literally true in 2009. When I finally disclosed it, years later, the knowledge wasn't new information. It was old, and I'd been keeping it at arm's length. "Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" is a very good question, and you let it stand without answering it. The reverse-remorse paragraph is the weakest part. The case you offer, "a bad person who does secret acts of kindness, and then lies awake at night", is a straw case. The real cases of reverse remorse are conflicts between two goods. I once gave one student an extension and felt bad about it afterward, and what I felt was unfairness to the others. You file that under "guilt at breaking a rule I was raised on." But fairness isn't just a rule I was raised on, and filing it there makes the asymmetry harder to test. Not a major objection, because you frame it as yours ("Mine turned out to be something else").

"Nobody ever looked honestly at something in themselves while being told they were worthless for having it." I'd vouch for that sentence. It matches the shame/guilt literature closely.

**The Two Strangers.** My case was a reviewer whose report began with the authors' "apparent unfamiliarity" with the basics. I dug in for weeks. Objection, minor: "Would you have dug in?" is a real question. But the next lines answer it for me: "it still wouldn't have triggered the digging-in response." Some people dig in no matter how they're addressed. I'd rather you had asked and stopped.

With my former coauthor, part of me did itch for the first button: a public correction he'd have to sign. Then I noticed something worse. I wanted the pill only if he'd *know* his heart had been changed, and be ashamed of the old one. That's the first button wearing a disguise. I'm grateful for the parenthesis about abusers; it's placed exactly where it's needed. **The How.** "I had to be present enough to plant it, and absent enough not to recognize it as planted." This is the paradox of self-deception, put more cleanly than in most of the philosophy. And "Evil behavior is unconscious in me, and fully aware in my enemies" is the actor-observer asymmetry with its moral payoff showing. "Deny it, and you're the kind of person who does. Get angry, and that's proof too." I've lived that with the reviewer: any rebuttal reads as defensiveness.

Major objection (trust): the section opens with "The reason I don't have to argue myself into doing the right thing is that in the absence of justifications, it's what I naturally desire." I often argue myself into the right thing: telling a coauthor the effect didn't replicate, going to a hospital I'm afraid of. Maybe what I'm arguing against there is fear, not conscience. **The Mechanism.** The common-knowledge material is the part I'll think about for weeks. "The look is the deed" is right, and so is "gaze and tone, which have the added benefit of being unquotable." Major objection (harm, and trust): "My claim that I'm a *better kind of thing* is maddening precisely because we both know it's false." They collapse, and they believe the verdict. Shame-prone people withdraw; they don't defy. Your model has only one response to contempt, defiance. A reader who grew up under a contemptuous parent will read "we both know it's false" and think: I didn't know. I believed it. Then either the chapter isn't about them, or they failed at something everyone else does. The "addressed to the same place" passage could be the most healing thing in the book for that reader. A smaller trust point: "He knows it's true, and I *know* he knows it, and he knows that too." Your evidence for what he knows is your reading of his eyes, from inside a monologue you've told me is self-deceived. I believe you about the speaker's side. The target's side is inference.
