# Appendix 1: What Contempt Presupposes

*For the reader who wants the argument stated explicitly. Nothing in the book rests on this; the book rests on what you found when you looked. Here is the whole of it on one screen, then the support, then what I believe, marked.*

*If you want rigor, read it all in order. If you're a general reader or a practitioner, read the skeleton, then sections 3 to 9; sections 1 and 2 are the argument in full, and you can come back for them.*

**The claim.**

Moral contempt says *this is all you are*. It can only say that by addressing the capacity in them that the verdict denies exists. Its explicit message denies equality. Its successful operation presupposes it. (Moral, to set it apart from disdain for someone's competence or taste, which carries no verdict on what they are. Everywhere in this book, contempt means the moral kind.)

**Checked, in your own case (Tier I).**
- O1. Intending harm to someone requires holding them out of view.
- O2. Holding someone out of view takes work, and the work rises under exposure.
- O3. Contempt lands as a knowingly disavowed double standard, and the sender counts on its being read that way.

- O4. Contact carries care: seeing another person carries the wanting that they be all right, before any thought.
- O5. Turning away obscures the care; it doesn't remove it. It's still there when you look back.

**What each does.** O1 to O3 are the mechanism of one encounter. O4 and O5 say what's being turned from, and that it survives the turning. Nothing is assumed. What the act needs in order to work, its coherence conditions, is derived from these five; and since the act worked, the conditions held.

**What the act presupposes.** That the other person's faculty is the same kind as yours, since they read the move. That there's a standard shared by both of you which turning away departs from, since what they read was hypocrisy; and by O4, that the standard is the care. That the care in them is present, obscured, and movable. Each of those is the opposite of the verdict.

**What follows.**

Turning away from them is turning away from your own caring, one act. Limbo is that same turning away seen from inside: the knowledge is right there and not let in, because the care it would show doesn't go anywhere. One act, not two conditions. The state is hidden from the one in it, because the faculty that would show it to him is the one he's turned, and visible to everyone else, because theirs isn't. Contempt is that state made legible to its target on purpose, with the verdict attached. And the target can be moved, which is what the sender is counting on.

**Conclusion.**

The act presupposes what it denies, as a condition of its possibility. Contempt works only on someone who can harm only by turning away and who can turn, and on nobody else. And you already knew this, because every act of it that worked was relying on it (in the sense section 8 makes exact).

**The seams.**

That you relied on the person's recognition and responsiveness is Tier I. That what they recognized it with is care, present and obscured, is O4 and O5. That care is their nature in the working sense, the baseline that turning away departs from, is what hypocrisy presupposes. That it's the nature of awareness as such is a further reading, marked at the end.

**The punchline, marked as belief.**

If the care is what awareness is, then evil is awareness turned away from its own nature, and you can only do it to another by first forgetting it in yourself. And the knowledge is common because nothing tells the two awarenesses apart: to know it in yourself is to know it in him. Contempt is coherent only if, somewhere, you already know that.

## 1. The deduction

### Three things you checked

**O1. Intending harm to someone requires holding them out of view.** 

Chapter 1, The How. You tried to give a stranger the gaze and couldn't. Not a rule, not a decision; you could see them, and the intention lost its footing. To keep it, you'd have had to stop seeing them first. That's the direction that holds without qualification. The converse holds too, with a purpose attached: we hold people out of view in order to harm or use them.[^converse]

**O2. Holding someone out of view takes work, and the work rises under exposure.** 

The Tell and The Loop. The stranger tracked your every exit. Pointed at, the behavior didn't get embarrassed; it intensified in the direction needed to stay hidden. And your own case, from When You Send It: the flash had to be aimed, had to land, and had to go unrecorded, and none of that was calculated. It was there in one motion.

**O3. Contempt lands as a knowingly disavowed double standard.** 

The Righteous Appearance and the Taunt. When it landed on you, you didn't only feel threatened or disapproved of. You recognized that he was doing the thing he condemned you for, that he meant you to feel it, and that he'd deny it if you named it. That's what made it maddening rather than merely hostile. And notice what he was appealing to. The sting needed you to recognize the violation, which is a capacity; and the verdict said you were the kind of thing that has no such capacity. He was addressing the very thing he was denying was there. And from the sending end, in When You Send It, you counted on exactly that recognition: for it to sting the way you needed, they had to read it as the real thing. A threat, a status blow, a loss of face would each explain a bad reaction. None of them explains hypocrisy, and hypocrisy is what you felt.

Those three are Tier I, in the sense the book uses: each is a claim about experience that you checked in your own case, and each is open to a reader who looked and found otherwise. Everything below is what they imply, and it is only as strong as they are.

### Two more things you checked, which explain the first three

**O4. Contact carries care.** 

When you see another person, you recognize them as like you and want them to be all right, before any thought. You checked this at the start of Chapter 2: hold a stranger's eyes and the wanting arrives with the looking. O1 is the same fact from the other side: if seeing someone were neutral, harming them wouldn't require breaking contact first. The fact that it does means the seeing has a direction. Care isn't something added to perception. It's what perception of another is like when nothing is in the way.

**O5. The care isn't removed by turning away. It's obscured.** 

You checked this too, twice. In the limbo recall, the knowledge that it was wrong didn't feel like new information; it had been there the whole time. And when the second stranger made it safe to look, the care was there to be found. O2 is the same fact seen in the effort: Nobody builds machinery that elaborate to keep something from a part of themselves that doesn't care. If the care were gone, there would be nothing to hide from, and the hiding would stop. It doesn't stop; it escalates. So the thing turned away from is still there, doing what it does, and the size of the hiding measures it. A naturalist can accept both, with the care read as an evolved disposition; what's disputed is what the care is, not whether it's there, and section 9 is where that reading and mine part.

Nothing else is assumed. That his faculty is the same kind as yours isn't a premise here; it's one of the things the act turns out to need, and the turn derives it from O3.


### What follows

**(a) Turning away from them is turning away from yourself.** 

By O4, the care is in the seeing. So to hold them out of view is to hold your own caring out of view, which is what "turning away from your conscience" has meant since The How. One act, not two. This is why a dehumanizing gaze reads as someone absent from themselves: the same absence, seen from outside.

And with O1 it gives the book its definition of evil. Every intended harm is done from that state, and there is no other state it can be done from. So at the level of the person, "evil" in this book names no substance and no essence. Each premise supplies a word of that: O1 gives the *is*, since the harm and the turning are one event; O4 gives *from what*, your own caring; and O5 gives *no substance*, since what turns away obscures and adds nothing. It names the turning: your own caring, held out of your own view, so that theirs can be held out too. You can only do this to them by first losing it in yourself. There is no other way in. (Section 9 says what I think the caring is, and if that's right, "your own caring" here reads "your own nature.")

**(b) Limbo is turning away, seen from inside.** 

Turning away is holding a person out of view. By O4, what's in view when you look at them is the care and the sameness. By O5, holding them out of view doesn't remove either; it leaves them there, unlooked at. Knowledge right there to see, and not let in: that is Chapter 1's definition of limbo, word for word. So limbo isn't a second condition that harm requires on top of turning away. It's the same act described from the side of what you know, as turning away is the same act described from the side of the person you're not looking at. In the notation, Limbo(P) = OpKnow(P) ∧ ¬Own(P): the knowing is intact because the care doesn't go anywhere, and the owning is absent because you're turned. That holds for any intended harm. Contempt needs a more specific version of it: enough operative knowledge to aim the verdict, predict the reaction, and use it as evidence, without enough ownership to recognize that the evidence was planted. Both halves are forced. Full ownership would make the planted evidence worthless to him; no operative knowledge would leave him unable to aim, and embarrassed when caught rather than escalating. And the state is hidden from him for the same reason it's visible to you: the faculty that would show it to him is the one he's turned, and yours is intact. What he hides was never the deed. It's what the deed reveals: his sameness to the person he condemns.

**(c) The state shows.** 

By (a), what's absent in him is the seeing. And you can tell whether somebody is seeing you only because you're seeing them; that's the faculty that reads it. So his absence is what you perceive, with the very faculty he's turned away from. No message is needed for this. It's what a turned-away face is.

**(d) Contempt.** 

Turning away, made legible to its target on purpose, carrying the verdict *this is all you are*, and denied as righteous. The cold form, the executive with a reason on file, shows too, but nobody aims it. Contempt aims, because the sender needs the verdict to land. The disavowal is forced by (b): he can't own it, so he denies it, and the denial isn't a tactic but the shape of the state.

**(e) Protest becomes proof.** 

Also from (b). A sender in that state reads reactions as confirmation, because the evidence he's collecting is evidence he's producing, and the producing is the half he can't see. So any answer the target gives is used, and both of them can count on that in advance.

**(f) The target can be moved.** 

By O5, the faculty is present in the target, obscured, and turnable. That's what the sender is counting on: the message is meant to push them further away. But the faculty that can be pushed further is the faculty that could have turned back. A rock doesn't need provoking. To be pushed from the light, you have to be somebody the light was available to.

Call a person **fundamentally innocent** if they can harm only by turning away, and can turn. Not harmless, and not incapable of harm. By O4 and O5, everyone the book calls redeemable is this, and only this.

### The turn

Start with what the act presupposes directly, before any interpretation. By O3, for contempt to land the target has to recognize the same turning away in the sender that the sender condemns in them, and feel it as a double standard. That requires two things of the target: that they can still tell a turned-away face from a looking one, and that the standard being violated is one they hold. And the sender counts on both. So the act, on its own, presupposes that the two of you stand on the same ground: each can read the same move in the other, which is to say his faculty is the same kind as yours. It presupposes that the target is not exhausted by the verdict, because its effect depends on a capacity the verdict says they don't have. And it presupposes something about which way is up. Hypocrisy needs a shared standard and a departure from it. Hypocrisy alone doesn't say whether a harmful disposition might also be intrinsic; it says only that both people recognize a standard and recognize the sender as departing from it. So the act needs a baseline in both of you that turning away departs from, and O3 gives that much: a shared standard, and a capacity in the target that survives. What the standard is comes from O4: the standard is the care, since that's what the seeing carries. Nature, in the sense the book has used the word since Chapter 2, is that baseline, what you're like when nothing is in the way, and Chapter 2's language check is the same fact: one direction is coming back and the other is losing yourself, and nobody taught you which was which. Its explicit message denies equality. Its successful operation presupposes it. The shared standing and the surviving capacity don't need O4 or O5; naming the standard does.

Now add them, and the shared recognition takes a shape. By O4, what still tells turning away from looking in the target is the care itself, since the care is in the seeing. By O5, it's present and obscured, not gone. By (f), it can be moved, and the sender is counting on that too. So the message *this is all you are* can function only by addressing the part of them that knows it isn't. If nothing in them still made the distinction, there would be no one for the message to reach and no conscience for it to corrupt. Contempt works only on the fundamentally innocent, in the sense defined above, and on nobody else.

The sender relies on all of this holding in the target. Not as a theory he holds; as what the act runs on, known the way (b) says he knows things, enough to use and not enough to own. And the target relies on the same in the sender, since reading the state is reading a faculty of the same kind, turned. That's why the knowledge is common and not merely shared. This isn't a ladder of propositions that either of them climbs; section 2 says why. And none of it needs to be provable to a third party. Contempt can sometimes be shown plainly; what its operation requires is deniability between the two of them, and the deniability in (d) is what it runs on.

The act presupposes what it denies. Not as a by-product, and not as evidence a careful observer could collect afterward. As a condition of its possibility. Contempt can portray darkness as someone's nature only by exploiting the shared knowledge that it isn't.

## 2. Why the reflection has no floor

*This section is for the reader who likes the analytical version. Section 3 says the same in prose, and nothing after this depends on it.*

Section 1 said what has to already be true between two people for contempt to work. This section says what the message is made of, why it looks bottomless when you try to locate it, and where the bottom is.

Start with the sentence that seems to have no floor. The particular limbo called contempt is hiding something from yourself while revealing it to me. Call that something X. And what is X? The fact that you're hiding X from yourself while revealing it to me. Ask what X is and you get X back. There are four structures inside that sentence, and they aren't the same kind of thing.

**First, the payload.** Two things are already known between the two of you before anything happens, and each of you knows the other knows them.[^ck]

- **S**: we are the same kind of thing. Nothing in either of us is morally above the other.
- **T**: a person turned away from you and needing you to see it is doing it for harm. A turn can have many causes, grief, fear, a claim you can't meet; a turn aimed at you and held so that you'll register it has one function in that encounter.

Ordinary harm uses the turn once: you turn away, that lets you do the deed, and the deed does the damage. Contempt needs no separate deed. The look is the deed. Here is what it delivers, from the target's side, in the order the pieces land:

- **The turn.** He's turned away from you, and you read the look.
- **The intent.** By T, you know what an aimed turn is for. He means harm. Nothing has to be said.
- **The wanting.** He wants you to recognize the intent. If he didn't, this would be a leak, not a message.
- **The injury.** Your recognizing it is already the harm; no separate deed has to follow. But the recognition is also meant to produce a reaction, and the reaction is going to become his proof.
- **The disavowal.** He will deny the intention if you name it, and he'll believe the denial.
- **The proof.** So your protest becomes evidence of the verdict that provoked it, and you know that in advance.

Those six are the ingredients of the payload. Call it **H**: being made to know, without recourse, that he has turned away from you and intends this very knowledge to hurt you. Written once, exactly: let G(P) mean *make the target know, without recourse, that you intend P.* Then H = G(H). He inflicts H by making you recognize that he intends to inflict H. The act's purpose is its own reception. That's the fixed point, and it has real content at every ingredient. What it lacks is anything outside itself to point to, and that lack is what "without recourse" means: there's nothing auditable to produce against him, so anything you produce is your reaction, and your reaction is his evidence. The disavowal and the proof aren't added to H. They follow from its having nothing outside itself, and you know that in advance, so they're part of what it delivers. That's where it closes.

**Second, the reflection.** Around H there's a tower of mutual recognition: you recognize that he meant you to recognize H, he knows you recognize it, you recognize that he knows. Those are depths, not ingredients. Each reflects the same H and adds nothing to it, which is why the mirrors feel bottomless and why the bottom is never a new thing. What the trap needs is only the first few depths, operating, and you checked those at O3. Whether the whole tower is present at once is section 9's claim, under its own premises; neither is the game theorist's common knowledge, and the footnote says why.

**Third, the loop in time.** If you protest, he takes the protest as evidence of the verdict that provoked it. The renewed verdict sends H again, which produces another reaction, which is used again. That isn't a depth of recognition. It's a feedback loop, and it's the direction in which the hall of mirrors goes on for years.

**Fourth, the hiding that is the showing.** What makes all three possible without any auditable act is that his intention is unowned by him, and the non-ownership is part of what you're meant to see. He doesn't hide the intention and then let it slip. What he reveals is that he's hiding it. Section 3 has this in prose.

So the trap is finite in content, repeatable without limit in time, and open to reflection as far as either of you goes. On section 9's reading the reflection is present whole. Either way the reflections have no floor, and they're all reflections of one thing.

**Where the recursion comes from.** All malice hides one thing from the one doing it: S, the sameness, held down even in the sadist. But malice as such needs neither of two things contempt needs. It need not be received; the target can be asleep while the deed is done. Some malice does demand reception, a threat, a sadist's display, but it needn't disavow itself as righteous; the honest villain says *yes, I mean you harm, and no, you're not my kind.* So in plain malice the payload stops at the intent, and in the threat at the wanting, with nothing to deny: name it and he agrees with you. Contempt needs both. The target must receive the harmful intention, which makes the recognition the payload, and the sender must sincerely deny that it's harmful, which puts the intent in limbo along with S and turns every protest into proof. The recursion is generated by that conjunction and by nothing less. It's why malice is frightening and contempt is maddening.

**The fixed point, for those who want it written down.** H is a fixed point in the ordinary sense, the value G returns when applied to its own output, and no iteration from a seed is needed to define it. What's unusual is its content. An ordinary fixed point of a message-operation would have content of its own, a threat or a claim, with the self-reference wrapped around it. H has nothing inside the wrapping but the ground it stands on: S, held out of view, and the fact of holding it. Take S away and there's nothing for H to be about.

You can write the reflection down too, as a seed and an operator: M for the turn, F(P) for *hide P from yourself while showing it to me*, and the tower M, F(M), F(F(M)). Keep one thing straight if you do. The tower doesn't collapse as propositions; "I'm hiding that I'm hiding it" is a different sentence from "I'm hiding it." What collapses is the acting. There is one act in the encounter, turning away from S, and every level of the tower is that act under a further description. No depth calls for a new act, which is why the sender never has to climb it and why it fits in one look. Limbo is that act from inside; F is the act as a witness describes it; H is the act named by what it does. And the tower doesn't start with the witness. Limbo contains itself in one person, before anyone is watching. The witness adds no depth. He's the one person standing where the act can be seen, because he's turned away from nothing.

Now look at what's in the room. Other things are present, but every capacity doing anything here belongs to the faculty S names: the recognition of a perceiver of the same kind, present in two bodies and turned against itself in one. The darkness contributes no equipment of its own. Everything needed to send the message, conceal it, receive it, and answer it is borrowed from the faculty whose sameness the message denies.

H has no external payload beyond its own delivery. If it had one, a slur, a shove, a lie you could catch, you'd have something to point to. Names for it exist and each catches a piece: hostile metacommunication, a double bind, deniable provocation, gaslighting. What doesn't exist is a name that survives being said to his face, because every name is auditable and the act isn't. Say *you're being contemptuous* and you've made a claim about tone, which he can deny, and now you're the one making things unpleasant.

### A note on Gödel

The comparison will occur to some readers. It's an analogy, not an argument, and the analogy is to the second theorem. A formal system strong enough to matter can't certify its own consistency using only its own resources; a stronger system can assess it from outside, and then the question moves to the stronger one. The lesson contempt teaches, demote the part that knows and be only the part that proves, asks the reasoning part to vouch for itself in the same way, and the reasoning that justifies an act can't show, by producing more justification, that it hasn't been recruited. That's Chapter 6's claim in Gödel's shape. What the theorem doesn't supply is that the outside faculty is sound: it gives truth conditional on consistency, established from outside, and the analogue, that the part that knows is right if it isn't itself turned away, is a problem the book carries rather than a result it has.

## 3. The hall of mirrors

*Section 2 in prose, for the reader who skipped it.*

Go looking for where the harm is. In his accusation? Defensible. In the lie behind it? He doesn't experience himself as lying. In his hiding of the lie? In your seeing him hide it? In his seeing you see it? In his hiding *that*? That reads like a chain of separate events. But check what each link contains. His hiding of the lie is: he's turned away, and you can see it. Your seeing him hide it is: he's turned away, and you can see it. His hiding *that* is the same. Every depth describes the same act. The sentences differ, he hides it, he hides that he hides it, you see that, but none of them requires another thing to happen. And none of the depths can be verified from outside; that isn't a fact at some particular depth, it's the condition every depth is under. Each place you look hands you the next place to look, and none of them is the one. That's the hall of mirrors.

Why is it one act? Because the faculty he's hiding from is the faculty that reads him. There is one faculty at work in everything Chapter 1 described, and it does one thing: it recognizes life. To see that something is alive isn't to observe a property; it's to recognize a perceiver. So when two people see life in each other, each is seeing that the other sees, and seeing that they are seen. The organ you're looking with is the organ you're looking at. He turns away from it. What he can still see is what he hasn't managed to stop seeing, and that residue is exactly what he's turned from, so it never reaches him. But turning away has a look, and your copy of the same faculty reads it instantly, because it's reading its own kind in another body. So the act that keeps it from him is the act that displays it to you. He doesn't hide it and then let it slip. The hiding *is* the slip. **The signal is the pretense that there is no signal.** And look at what's in the encounter. One faculty, in two bodies, and one of them turned. The message says *you are not what I am*, and it's made of nothing but what you both are, and only what you both are can read it.

Here is the taunt from Chapter 1 in one sentence, from his side:

> *I'm teaching you to overlook what you know in favor of what you can prove, by overlooking what I know in favor of what I can prove, which is something you know but cannot prove. Which is what teaches you to overlook it.*

Four clauses, and none of them is spare. The first is the lesson: demote your knowing. The second is the method: he demotes his, leaning on the fact that only provable things count, so the demonstration isn't an illustration of the lesson, it *is* the lesson, performed. The third is the reception: it reaches you through the very faculty it devalues, so to receive it at all you have to trust something unprovable. And the fourth is the effect, which isn't the first clause restated. The first names an intention; the fourth names the mechanism by which the intention succeeds, and it succeeds *because* of the third. Being held in the position of knowing-without-proof is what does the teaching. The sentence closes on itself, but causally rather than verbally. Section 2 compresses it to H.

One more thing worth having in hand before the argument resumes: what the reflection contains. The whole list fits in a look. Equality: there is nothing morally superior about any bearer of the faculty, which falls out of what it sees, a perceiver of the same kind looking back. Pretense: when a bearer acts superior, the faculty knows it's pretending, since you can only turn away from something you have. Projection: seeing the other as bad is the pretense pointed outward. Harm: turning away from someone's humanity harms them, and the faculty knows exactly how it lands. Inexpressibility: none of this leaves a record, because the faculty doesn't testify and no other faculty saw it. The list doesn't close. Anyone who has made the move knows what follows from it, because they learned it the first time they did it.

## 4. The title

This book was originally titled *Contempt Is Evil*, and here is the sentence in the form the deduction gives it.

By (a), evil at the level of the person names the turning: your own caring held out of view so that theirs can be. By (d), contempt is that turning made legible to its target, on purpose, with the verdict attached. So contempt isn't a response to evil, or a symptom of it, or a lesser cousin. It's the thing itself, made into a message. That's the identification, and it isn't a judgment of anyone.

And O3 says you already knew it. For the message to sting the way you needed, they had to read it as the real thing: not harshness, which the second stranger had more of and which landed as a reminder, but a person putting them out of view and letting them watch. The cover, *mine is the good kind*, is refuted not by the fact that it stung, since a clean confrontation can sting too, but by what they recognized in the sting: the turning away, the intention that it land, and the disavowal. When you checked from the sending end, you found the same three. If it had been the good kind, there would have been nothing of that shape to recognize, and it would have landed as his did. So every time your contempt worked, both of you were relying on a shared recognition of what it was, and what it was is the thing the word has always pointed at. The title isn't a claim the book asks you to accept. It's a claim your own contempt has been counting on.

## 5. A prediction, and what supports it

If contempt were an increasingly accurate perception of badness, then the clearer the observer, the more of it they'd hold toward worse people, or at least the more they'd converge on it. If the verdict is the sender's own turning away showing itself, clarity should do the opposite: preserve or sharpen the perception of the harm while dissolving the verdict. The wise person sees the wrongdoing at least as clearly as you do and sees less essence, not less harm.

The second is what we see. Two strangers looked at one act, and one delivered the verdict and one didn't; the act didn't decide it. The people we call evil hold the most contempt, not the least; contempt for their victims is how the harm was done. And the people we'd call wise are the ones who can look at the worst person, seeing everything you see, without the verdict forming. Not because they're soft, and not because they're suppressing it. They haven't lost contact with the thing in them that recognizes the person, so there's nothing for a verdict to be made of, and the harm is as visible to them as it is to you. Which says what *now I finally see what they really are* is. The darkness you see is real, and that's why the sentence has force. The *really are* isn't a perception of them. It's your own turning away, reading itself as insight, and it's available to you only because you've lost contact with the thing that would have recognized them. That last one is intuitive rather than measured, and I'm not going to dress it up. But it's a differential prediction, and the perception account gets it backwards.

The same gradient is the fear account in Chapter 16 from the outside. The more a person has turned away, the more feared essence there is to put somewhere, and the more faces it needs.

## 6. Two things the word carries

Two things the word usually carries, and both are wrong, and you already know they're wrong. The first is size. We reserve "evil" for the extreme, but watch what cutting in line does to you. The cost is thirty seconds. The heat is out of all proportion to thirty seconds, because what you're reacting to isn't the cost. It's the exception: somebody put you out of view to get ahead, and let you see it. Chapter 1 found the same thing from inside, in the flash that was shameful to admit out of proportion to its size; the smallest morsel carries the whole. The second is outcomes. We think "bad" is about damage done, but the friend who forgot your birthday did more damage by any accounting than the friend who made one small cutting remark, and the remark is the one you're still thinking about a week later. The corpse case has no damage at all and everyone knows it's wrong. Two acts identical from outside can have opposite effects. What you're tracking, in every one of those, is the turning, and you were tracking it before this book named it.

## 7. What that settles

Contempt sets out to prove one thing: *this is what you are.* Given what you checked in O1 to O5, its machinery implies the opposite, and has to. If darkness exhausted anyone's nature, neither the target's original act nor the sender's reply would have needed a turning away; both would have been done while looking. Both required one. What this rules out is the verdict's exact claim, that this is *all* they are. That there might be care and a harmful disposition side by side, both intrinsic, isn't ruled out here. Chapter 4's argument, that the darkness has no equipment of its own, is what goes further, and this page reaches it from the other side: not by inspecting the stranger and finding only borrowed faculties, but by noticing that his act could only work if there was nothing else there to find.

And it settles one more thing, which the book has said in several places without deriving it. Chapter 1 ends with the part that knows "patiently waiting to be remembered." The map says you don't build it and you don't earn it; you remember it. The trip in Chapter 2 says the revelation didn't feel like new knowledge. Here is why that has to be so. Whatever recognition recognizes, when the turning back happens, must already have been there in limbo, because contempt was already using it. What turning back adds is nothing. It makes explicit, at the level of the person, what was already being counted on between the two of them. The second stranger did that once, between two people, without an argument.

Three more things follow, and each is checkable against the book's own cases.

The sender aims with the knowledge of sameness. To make the verdict land he has to know what it is to be the target, and only S supplies that. It's operative knowledge, not care; a person can take another's perspective while nothing in them minds. But it's knowledge of the one thing the verdict denies, and the more skilled the contempt, the more of it is in use. That's Chapter 12's far end from this side: the worst know the most and the least at once.

The second stranger is F's inverse. F shows S while refusing to own it. He showed S while owning it: he treated you as a person who had done something, which is the sameness made explicit between two people. That's why nothing about his act was auditable and why it couldn't be argued with. There was no hiding in it for a reply to catch on.

And the thing can't be fought as a thing. Contempt for the contemptuous is F(S) again, applied by the fighter. The noble one fighting the conspiracy is running the operation, and here that's derived rather than asserted.

## 8. The finale, with its seams

Here is the sentence the whole page is for, and then the seams in it.

Every time your contempt landed, you were relying on the person in front of you not being exhausted by their darkness: on their recognition, and their responsiveness, still being there to reach. O4 and O5 say what remained is care, present and obscured. The act itself needs the care to be what they are when nothing is in the way. The further reading says it's what awareness is, in anyone. If that reading is right, then the book's central premise, that nothing has to be built and the only work is turning back, isn't a hope laid over the evidence. It's the deepest account of what your own contempt was already counting on. Every act of it that worked was coherent only because you already knew this, and kept it where you couldn't own it.

The seams, one per sentence above. That you were relying on their recognition and responsiveness is Tier I: if the flash landed, they read the double standard, and you counted on their reading it, and you checked the flash. That what they recognized it with is care is O4 and O5, checked in your own case. That the care is the baseline and turning away the departure is what hypocrisy presupposes, so it's derived. That the care is the nature of awareness as such, rather than of human beings, is my reading further still, offered in the last section, with its evidence in Appendix 3. Why the reading isn't arbitrary: it makes a prediction the other reading can't, that contempt tracks the sender; it matches the shape four traditions reached by other roads; and it survives the hardest case so far. And two things the sentence does not say. It doesn't say the turning is easy, or that not-seeing can be reversed by deciding to see; what's trained is how fast you catch it. And it doesn't say the turning is enough. Being free of self-deception makes you honest, not wise, and what to do from there is a second skill. What it says is only this: everything you'd need was already in the room, because you were using it.

And what is not claimed, so that nobody reads more into the sentence than it says.

Not that turning away is sufficient for harm. It also produces numbness, avoidance, and paralysis, with nobody harmed. The direction that holds without qualification is that harm or use requires it. The converse holds with the purpose clause attached, which is how O1 states it.

Not the cold form. The argument is about contempt, the aimed form. The general rule for harm and use rests on O1, checked in both directions in The How, not on this page.

Not universality, from this page. O1 to O3 are claims about experience, stated as holding for everyone and open to anyone who looked and found otherwise; the interlude says what that finding would count for. The deduction itself needs one working instance, and your own, from When You Send It, is enough for it: if the flash landed, the structure held in that room, for both of you. That it holds in every room is what the rest of the book argues, case by case, and this page doesn't.

Not that every target can be reached in practice. In principle, (f) entails it: the faculty is there to be turned. What the page says nothing about is how much has to be true around a person before turning back is survivable, and Chapter 12 holds that for some it may not happen in a lifetime. Movable in principle is not the same as near.

## 9. What I believe, marked

*From here I'm saying what I believe about what the care is. The deduction reaches this door and stops. Nothing practical depends on going through it.*

Anyone who practices will have seen the shape several paragraphs ago, and Appendix 3 names the correspondences one by one. Stated against the argument: O4 is the claim that compassion is the nature of mind, not a quality some minds have. O5 is the teaching that the obscurations are adventitious; they never touch the nature they obscure. Turning away has the shape of *marigpa*, non-recognition: not a substance, and not a faculty of its own. And the turning back is recognition: revealed, not created, because there was never anything to create.

The core of the deduction is older than the book, and I should say where. That the affliction, looked at directly, is the nature it obscures: Mahamudra's "the nature of thought is dharmakaya," and Dzogchen's delusion as the energy of awareness, unrecognized. That the nature is present and untouched in the most obscured being: the Uttaratantra's adventitious stains. That evil has no substance of its own: Augustine. And the third consequence in section 7 is Shantideva's: anger at the person is anger at the stick instead of the hand that holds it, and the affliction is the enemy, not the being it's using. What none of them states is the interpersonal proof, that contempt has to be made of the recognition it denies at both ends and that the target's reading of it is the evidence. That part is this book's. Appendix 3 states each correspondence in full, with what it does and doesn't grant.

What the deduction adds to those, and it's the reason this page exists: the traditions arrived at them by practice or revelation, and this book arrives at their shape from the anatomy of a bad act. Contempt is coherent only if the sender already holds, in limbo, that the target's nature is care, that it's obscured rather than absent, and that it can be turned back. That is the soteriology, held implicitly, by the one person who'd deny it most. Said without the labels: awareness is caring by nature. Evil is awareness turned away from its own nature, and there is no other way to do it to someone than to forget it in yourself. Contempt is that forgetting, made into a message, and it can only be sent to a nature the same as your own, since nothing else could receive it. And limbo, turning away, and the parasite are not three things. Each is awareness turned away from itself, named from a different side: from the knowledge that's held down, from the person who's held out, and from the behavior that results. And the further claim, that awakening is awareness turning back toward itself, is on this account the same event as the implicit becoming explicit: what was common knowledge between two people at the level of what's looking becomes sayable at the level of the person. The rest of this section says it in the vocabulary of common knowledge, and the anti-ritual is there: contempt is the act that keeps the sameness from ever being said while both go on knowing it.

Now the limit, stated plainly, because this is where I'd overclaim. The rival explanation should be named. Evolved moral emotions plus the drive to reduce dissonance would also predict O1 through O5: we flinch at harming what we see, and we rationalize what we've done. The deduction didn't need to beat that rival. It needs only that the care is present and obscured, which you checked and the rival grants under other names. Where the rival and the traditions part is over what the care is, and that question is this section's. The deduction gives *present, obscured, movable*, and that care is the baseline turning away departs from. It does not by itself give "care is intrinsic to awareness," as against a baseline human beings evolved into, which could have been otherwise and could be missing in some. The turn survives that reading, because it needs only the capacity present and readable, and the naturalist grants both. So I offer the first reading lightly. What supports it, and the test that would count against it, are in Appendix 3, under "What's mine," where the traditions' claim and the book's road to it sit side by side.

On the reading I'm offering, contempt is the anti-ritual. A ritual makes a thing public so that nobody can go on acting as if they didn't know. Contempt keeps the sameness from ever becoming avowable, while both people go on knowing it. Every move in Chapter 1 is a move to keep one division intact, between what is registered and what can be said, and the deniability is what protects the division. "You're being mean" fails because it tries to make the thing avowable with one voice, and the deniability was built to absorb one voice.

The second stranger's act reversed that. He announced nothing; his words could have been nearly the same as the first man's, and the difference wasn't in them, which is why nothing about it was auditable. What he did was enact the sameness between the two of you: he treated you as a person who had done something, and for that moment you no longer needed to hide from what you already knew. That doesn't create the recognition. It makes it available, between two people, where before it was held down on both sides. That's the reason you couldn't win against him and didn't want to. What this book is trying to do, with a shared name, is the public version of that, which he didn't need and couldn't have supplied.

For the reader who wants the belief in the vocabulary of common knowledge, here it is, because that's the vocabulary in which it can be checked against section 2.[^caution] Now three claims about awareness, stated as what I believe.[^whatnot] The care isn't content that awareness happens to contain; it's part of what awareness is, and awareness knows its own nature, which is what the traditions mean when they call it self-knowing. Turning away from that is the one mistake open to a sentient being, and it's common to all of them; limbo, the state Chapter 1 started from, is that mistake seen from inside. And the knowledge of both can never be absent, only turned away from, because it belongs to the nature and the nature doesn't go anywhere. None of this is about particular informational content. It's about what any awareness is, and each piece is known from the inside. And one more premise, which the rest turns on and which side one already used: each awareness knows its nature not as a private property but as the nature of awareness as such, and it recognizes another sentient being as another place where that same self-knowing nature is. That's what "in view" was. You don't need the awarenesses to be numerically one. You need them to be indistinguishable in nature and to recognize each other as such.

Under those premises the recursion doesn't have to be built by two people reading each other's minds to ever greater depth. It collapses, and the reason is in the content. What's known isn't a fact about me or about you. It's a fact about awareness as such, and it includes that every awareness knows it, at worst in limbo. So to know it is already to know that you know it, and that you know that I do, and so on; each further depth repeats the same recognition and adds nothing. That is the fixed-point shape of common knowledge.[^formal] The sameness of the two people on the street has the recursive structure of common knowledge at the level of awareness, and has had it all along. What isn't there is the person-level version: the thing either man could avow, reason from, and say out loud. That's what I mean by implicit common knowledge: common knowledge at the level of awareness that is unavailable as explicit common knowledge at the level of persons. Knowledge in limbo is its person-level shadow: present, turned away from, and still steering.

One more thing I don't claim, because I don't believe it in the strong form. Not that the awarenesses are one. Only that nothing distinguishes them in nature: whatever is looking out of you and whatever is looking out of him have nothing in them to differ by, which is all the mutual recognition in (c) requires and all this part uses.

What decides it, in the end, isn't the argument. It's what you find under your own hiding when the hiding stops, and the book can only take you to where you can look.

## Where else to look

**On common knowledge.** Lewis, _Convention_ (1969); Aumann (1976), for the definition. Chwe, _Rational Ritual_ (2001), for the public event that usually produces it. The modal-logic literature on unawareness, for knowledge present in a state without being available for use. Section 2 is the contrast; section 9 develops the analogue under its own premises.

**On what it is.** Bateson et al. (1956) on the double bind: a demand, a contradicting demand at another level, and no way to comment on the contradiction. Pinker, Nowak & Lee (2008), "The logic of indirect speech," on content conveyed _so as not to_ become common knowledge; the trap is an innuendo whose subject is itself. Goffman on face-work, for the auditable surface the act hides behind.

**On limbo.** Fingarette, _Self-Deception_ (1969): self-deception as declining to _spell out_ what you're doing. Sartre's bad faith; Mele as the deflationary rival. Kunda (1990) on motivated reasoning: the search stops at the wanted conclusion.

**On the leak being functional.** Trivers; von Hippel & Trivers (2011); Kurzban (2010). Adjacent, with one inversion: here the leak is the payload.

**On the two strangers, measured.** Tangney & Dearing, _Shame and Guilt_ (2002): "I did a bad thing" repairs, "I am bad" hides and attacks. Braithwaite, _Crime, Shame and Reintegration_ (1989): stigmatizing versus reintegrative shaming, with outcome data. Gilligan, _Violence_ (1996): shame as the universal precursor to violence.

**On turning away as a perceptual event.** Harris & Fiske (2006): social-perception regions go quiet for dehumanized groups. Gray & Wegner on moral typecasting: seen as agent, unseen as sufferer. Bandura on moral disengagement, which catalogs limbo's toolkit.

**On the arrangement.** Girard on the scapegoat. Baumeister, _Evil_ (1997), for the exterior view of the license, the terminus, and the myth of pure evil. Browning, _Ordinary Men_ (1992).

**On the shadow.** Jung.

**On privation.** Augustine, _Enchiridion_.

[^formal]: I'm not claiming more than the shape. Making it formal would mean defining "knows in limbo" as an operator and showing the proposition entails that everyone so knows it, and the premise supplies that by assumption, not by proof. Take this as a proposed analogue of the formal state, not an instance of it.

[^whatnot]: What this is not: it isn't a claim that two persons have access to each other's information; they don't, and the book assumes they don't every time it distinguishes your memories from mine. It isn't derived from a belief that there is one awareness, which I don't hold; nothing here needs more than that nothing tells them apart. And it isn't the ordinary formal state, which supports inference and coordination. Knowledge in limbo is specifically kept from being taken up into reasoning. The formal literature does distinguish implicit from explicit knowledge, which makes room for this, and that's all it does. Limbo is more specific than anything there: available to consciousness, strong enough to steer behavior, actively kept from avowal, and more defensive the closer you get.

[^converse]: The other direction says that when you hold a person out of view, it's in order to do something their being in view would stop, so the holding-out and the permission are one event seen from two sides. That needs one distinction. Walking through an airport, you don't take in the hundred people around you as people. They're in the background, and that isn't turning away: if one of them dropped a bag and started crying, they'd come forward into view at once, and you'd feel the pull. Turning away is keeping someone out of view when they'd otherwise come forward, because their reality would place a claim on you. People also turn from each other to escape grief, or fear, or a claim they can't meet, and that isn't this page's subject.

[^ck]: Game theorists call something common knowledge when everyone knows it, everyone knows that everyone knows it, and so on without end. It normally takes a public event to build: a trusted party says the thing aloud where everyone can see everyone hear it. The child in *The Emperor's New Clothes* adds no information; everyone already knew. He makes it common knowledge. The term has two uses on this page. The rule, that holding someone out of view is for harm, is common knowledge in Lewis's sense: a convention nobody was told, grounded in everyone having been on both ends. The encounter itself is not common knowledge in the game theorist's sense. Nobody says anything, nobody is trusted, and nothing becomes established; for him to know that you know, he'd have to know your faculty was working on this the way his was, and neither of you can verify that. What's there instead is shared experience, and shared experience is enough.

[^caution]: One caution about the traditions, since Appendix 3 leans on them. They supply the premises: a nature that is primordial rather than acquired, ignorance as non-recognition rather than absence, recognition disclosing what was already so, compassion belonging to awareness itself. The Dzogchen prayer of Samantabhadra has the whole shape: one ground, and from it awareness and non-recognition. Under those premises you'd expect cruelty to involve obscuration and kindness to involve recognition. What they don't contain is the machinery of Chapter 1: deniability as the shell, concealment as the message, provocation as recruitment, the target's reaction used to ratify the verdict. That part is the book's. And "one ground" is not the same claim as one numerically identical awareness; most Tibetan accounts keep individual continua, and the literal one-awareness premise is closer to Advaita. This part needs three things, and no one tradition grants all three in so many words: that care is what awareness is, that awareness knows this, and that it recognizes the same nature in another. Between them they supply the pieces; the package is this book's. So the convergence is real and it isn't confirmation: grant the premises and much of this follows. What would count as confirmation is whether the mechanism predicts real encounters better than the accounts in Appendix 2 do.
