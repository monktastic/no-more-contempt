# The argument

*Stateless. What the book claims, stated flat, in the order the book earns it, and sorted by what stands behind each claim. Tier I is what you can check in your own case. Tier II is what follows once the mechanism has been seen. Tier III is what the author believes and offers with its evidence, never as proof. The book never reads in this order and never opens with a claim; it asks first and names afterward. This file states baldly what the book only demonstrates. `brief.md` is the same argument in the author's own words, in four thousand words. `../../scratch/key-points/index.md` holds every claim and every wording, and `spine.md` beside it lists the theses in book order. `facts.md` is the short chain from a stranger's contempt to "addressed to the part it denies," with the status of each link. `discoveries.md` says where each thing lives.*

## The whole, in a paragraph

What you are, at the bottom, is a caring awareness, and it is the same in everyone. Harm needs you to turn away from it: from the part of you that cares and from the sameness of the person in front of you, which is one turn. The turn hides from the one making it and shows itself to everyone else, and what it shows teaches them to turn too. Contempt is that turn made into a message, aimed at a person, needing to be seen and needing to deny itself, and it works only because both people already know what it is. The thing that steers while you are turned away behaves like an agent with one aim, to make you forget there was ever a light to steer by, and it gets there by handing the compass to the part of you that argues. All the turnings in the world have nothing to tell them apart, so they add up to one thing, and the traditions named it. The whole of the cure is remembering, and the only place that can happen is in you.

## Tier I. What you can check in your own case

### 1. Limbo

There is a state in which you know and do not let yourself know, at once. Think of a wrong you could not fully admit until later. When you finally owned it, the knowledge was not new. It was there the whole time, held at arm's length. We call such knowledge "unconscious," and it was not; it was fully available, and you were turned from it.

The hiding was active. Whenever someone came close, or you did, something deflected, and part of you knew it was deflecting. Intentional behavior can take place in limbo too: you know you are in it, and you don't.

The part of you that knew it was wrong never argued. It knew before any reason arrived, and it never budged. The reasons came from the other side. They were not there to convince the part that knew; they were there to convince you that it was one voice among the noise, and safe to ignore. Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?

What the knowing part registered was not harm. Cheating on a test harms nobody in view and calculates nothing, and it fires anyway. What it registered was the exception: *this doesn't apply to me*. Contempt is the same assertion from the other end: *you're not really one of us*. One faculty, both directions. Harm is what the assertion produces downstream.

What limbo hides was never the misdeed. It is the other person's sameness. The misdeed is what holding that down permits.

### 2. Harm needs the turn, and the turn exists for harm

Picture someone in front of you, fully in view, and try to use them. It won't go. Something has to go out of view first, usually under a story about what they are. Accounts of atrocity return to the same preparation: the hood, the number instead of the name, the word for vermin.

"In view" does not mean in sight. A torturer watches the face. What he has turned from is the claim: that the one looking out of that face counts the way he does. In view has three marks, none of which depends on whether harm follows: their objection would land on you; their hurt would register as bad the way yours does; what is in them could change what you do. Pass all three and still fight (the second stranger). Fail all three and lift no finger (the cold form).

The other direction holds too. Nobody holds a person out of view unless about to do something the view would stop. The hundred people in an airport are in the background, not turned away from; one of them starts crying and comes forward at once. Turning away is keeping someone out when they would come forward, because their reality would place a claim on you.

So turning away exists for harm or use, and harm or use cannot be done without it. That is the nearest thing to a universal bad thing the book can find, and it is checked in both directions in your own case. Remorse never runs backward: nobody lies awake wracked over a secret act of kindness. Whatever you find in that place turns out to be something else: regret that it cost you, anger at being used, a rule you were raised on.

Turning away takes upkeep. A grudge has to be fed; when it drops, it feels like setting something down, never like starting a new effort. Being turned toward someone takes nothing like that. Practiced hiding stops feeling like effort, which is the one caveat.

### 3. The two strangers: kind is not nice

Imagine being caught, mid-wrong, by a stranger who chastises you for being the kind of person who does this. It gets harder to face what you already know: *who is he to judge?* You can tell he does this too, and you would bet on it at any odds, with no evidence but his standing there doing what he is doing.

Now a second stranger, harsher if anything, whose anger goes at what you did and not at what you are. You don't dig in. It lands as a reminder that you are better than this. He held both halves, *you knew* and *you're better than it*, at no cost to either, and that is why he could be harsh and why it landed as kindness. Whatever he was standing up for was not his. You might have disagreed with him about everything and it would not have mattered. You were already on its side.

Niceness is the "he couldn't help it" collapse. Contempt is the "he knew" collapse. Both feed the thing. Kind is the second stranger: both halves, no verdict, as fierce as it takes. You already know how to correct people without contempt; you have been corrected that way.

### 4. Contempt: the hiding is the revealing

When you send the verdict, your turning away shows. It goes out through tone and gaze, and it is aimed: delivered where it will land, in the form that can be denied, with knowledge of what they will feel. Nothing aims what it does not know. Deniable to the record and deniable to you: that is not a flaw in the design. The stories change and the conclusion never does; when he sees through one, the next is ready, and being caught produces no embarrassment. You had to be present enough to plant the evidence and absent enough not to recognize it as planted. Intentional, and done in limbo.

He reads it. His eyes say *we both know what you're really up to*, and you file that as projection, because you have told yourself even you don't know. The deniability gets used as evidence. The trap closes around him: deny it and you're the kind who does; get angry and that proves it; stay calm and you don't care; apologize and it's convenient. Plain malice would frighten him. This maddens, because it won't admit what it is.

Both of you know what the look means, and each knows the other knows, without end and without a word. If that knowing stopped anywhere, a slip reading would open ("maybe he doesn't realize I see it") and the look could pass as accident. Said aloud it would add no information, only a record, and the verdict could not survive a record. So it must be carried by what cannot be quoted. The signal is the pretense that there is no signal. And what it shows him is your limbo itself: *I know what I'm doing to you, and I believe it's righteous.* The message is the act of sending it, and it has no other content; it is only not empty because everyone already knows what that shape means.

Where it lands: not pride. It reaches further in, for the you that knows you count, the same you that you would have to turn from to harm someone, the same you the second stranger spoke to. **Love and contempt are addressed to the same place.** One says *I know you're in there.* The other says *there's nobody in there*, and both say it to the somebody who is. A rival refuses it and the refusal hardens him; a child cannot refuse it and carries it for years.

So contempt presupposes what it denies. It is aimed at the better nature it says is missing, by the same part in the sender, who had to find it in the target to aim. The verdict was aimed at pushing them further from a place, and nobody can be pushed from a place they are not in. You only treat people as irredeemable because part of you knows they are not.

The cover refutes itself. "Mine is the good kind of darkness" needs the target to read it as the real kind; the good kind would have landed as the second stranger's did and left a way back. The sting is the proof of what the sender knew. And the smallest morsel carries the whole: if size mattered, a small one would not be shameful to admit, and it is. Cutting in line costs thirty seconds and the heat is out of all proportion, because what you are reacting to is not the cost but the exception, and the exception is the same exception at any size.

We name the state by whose it is. Mine is "unconscious," which exonerates me; yours is "conscious," which licenses what I do to you. Same state, two names.

### 5. The wanting

We do not shame our enemies despite its making them dig in. We do it because of it. Two buttons: vengeance, or a pill that changes their heart, and nobody will ever know which you chose. Something itches for the first button. Watching someone despised get what was coming: was the pleasure only in the harm stopping, or in the punishment? Something craves the punishment over and above any good it does.

The flicker of satisfaction when the enemy hardens, relabeled disgust half a second later: *I knew it; other people are the problem.* Relief at being proven right. The need under it: that the darkness be something alien, in them and not in me, so that the problems of the world come from people I could never understand, only fight. All I had to do was harbor contempt; then I could point at what I had made and know that evil was real and was not me.

It comes in layers. On the surface it is righteous: putting a bad person in his place. Under that, I am wielding my own darkness to do it, but for the greater good. Under that, vengeance: I want him to suffer, in a way nobody could accuse me of. And at the bottom, I need others to carry the darkness I am hiding from myself, and he is an ideal victim because of what he did. The deepest trap is that the darkness convinces me it is too horrible to face, so that I hide it, so that I spread it; and it guards that layer by letting me believe I am the one using *it*, strategically, against real evil. The only resolution is to see that it was never intrinsic to me.

The write-off: you send the edge, they call you deranged, and you feel more righteous. Might that have been the point? The most dangerous thing an enemy can do to this arrangement is get better. And the privilege: think how it feels when you have skirted your own conscience and someone tries to shame you for it. You know they don't have your interests at heart, and there's no way you'd let them get away with it. We think it's somehow different when we do it.

The check in daily form: if the harm stopped tomorrow, with nobody punished and nobody exposed, would you feel relief, or robbed? And for the causes you hold: keep the analysis and the care, give up only the permission to feel contempt for the other side. Would you still want it?

### 6. What it teaches, and the two taunts

The person on the receiving end knows exactly what happened and can prove none of it. What they learn is that the part of them that knew, and couldn't prove it, isn't worth listening to. That is how it spreads: not the look, the permission. Every version of the book carries two taunts. The human one, what the target hears: *if people can be dark, call it righteous, and get away with it, why the hell shouldn't I?* And under it the parasite's, in its own voice: *look how easily I can commandeer the "good people." There is no light. There's only what you decide to call good.* The second is the whole operation in a sentence; §12 and §16 say what it does.

### 7. Where you will meet it

The theses say what contempt is. This says where a reader will find theirs, and a version of the book that leaves one out will be true and miss most of somebody's contempt.

- Hot, at a wrongdoer, face to face: the two strangers.
- Hot, from a distance: the politician, the news, the other side.
- Cold: a quiet filing-away, a politeness with nothing behind it, a small satisfaction at being done with them. The same verdict delivered by withdrawal, meant to be felt. Most harm runs cold, and most practiced readers live here.
- At someone who did nothing wrong: the stupid, the slow, the crazy, the weak, the unpracticed. Same verdict, better cover ("I'm only being accurate," and the assessment may be right). Check: no contempt for the toddler who can't add or the grandparent losing words; it arrives with "they should know better." Even this carries a moral charge: guilty of being what they are. It leaks the same way: treated as stupid, a person stops asking; treated as crazy, they stop trying to be understood, and that is taken as proof.
- At the unpracticed, the asleep, the still-contemptuous. Pity, which is contempt with a soft face and needs them to stay below me. Test: would I be glad if they got there before I did? The form this book makes available to its own readers.
- From below: at the boss, the rich, the powerful. The same parasite; only the verdict is on trial.
- At yourself: *you are what you did.* It feels like accountability and produces what contempt produces, hiding. The commonest form.
- At someone you love: the sigh; the *what's wrong with you?* that needs them to know they're broken; the look on their face that tells you it landed, which you pretend not to have seen; then back to loving them as if nothing happened.
- On behalf of someone you love: your sister's ex. Loyalty, with contempt riding on it. Love at someone else's expense isn't love.
- Consumed, not performed: the clip, the pile-on, the comment thread. A hot little *fuck yeah* when your side lands one; nothing got better, no harm stopped, and you got the landing for free. The spectator seat is a channel, and most contempt is consumed. It's why this wrong, of all wrongs, scales: it leaves no record, comes dressed as duty, is rewarded in public, and is pleasant to watch. Most of what we now call evil was once respectable; contempt is the one still praised as goodness itself, and that is why the others are so hard to stamp out: we fight every one of them with it.
- In a group: two people contemptuous together, each bolder than alone, a warmth between them that has nothing to do with the person discussed; the room that opens when nobody objects and shuts when the wrong person walks in.
- At the world: the tree cut for fun, the arch smashed for fun. The wrongness arrives before any story about who was deprived, on things with no inside. The checkable seed of §19.
- The water: most turning away has no enemy in it. The small self-serving move with nobody in front of you; the choice that helps me *and*, I tell myself, the world; the corner cut with a company because it would do worse to me. A low-grade infection with no fever, running all day, that tints everything like jaundice: you don't see the yellow, you see yellow things, and the thoughts you'd check with are tinted too.

## Tier II. What follows once the mechanism is seen

### 8. The one choice, and no chooser after

The turn cannot have been chosen under the name "bad." If harm can only be done from the turned-away state, deciding to stop seeing someone in order to harm them would be a wrong done while still looking, which is the one thing the face shows can't happen. Hold someone's eyes and decide, while holding them, to stop seeing them: there is no such moment. The view goes, then the reasons arrive, then the harm feels like something wanted.

There is one choice, and it is real. While both pulls can be felt, the recognition and the want it would veto, you are still looking, and can keep looking or stop. After that there is no chooser in the middle, only reasons, and they feel like your own judgment. The want was innocent (the seat, the money, the last word). What you did not choose was the description: it came labeled *I've had a long day*, never *stop looking at him*. The second pull also carries the accumulated ease of turning, laid down by your previous turns and by turns made at you; that ease is the parasite existing before it has you. Looking back, it feels as though you chose it knowing better, and you did, and that is limbo showing itself. It has remorse's shape, *I did it*, not shame's, *I am it*.

So all evil is innocent at the root and everyone is fully responsible, because responsibility never lived in a lucid instant of choosing evil. It lives in the choice to keep looking while you still can, in how fast you catch the blindness, in what you repair when sight returns, and in what you have done to give the blindness less room next time. Catching it is trainable; speed is the only measure.

Shame is the parasite's last defense, not the beginning of honesty. The cover-up exists because something in you would never approve, and that something is what you are. Nobody ever looked honestly at something in themselves while being told they were worthless for having it.

### 9. What is doing this: the parasite

Four marks of the thing that made the turn. It did not feel like you afterward; the clean moments felt most like yourself. You did not want what it made: more self-serving behavior in exchange for a darker world, and when the trade was visible you refused it. It hid itself, and it got stronger when you came close to catching it. And it was the only one that gained: not you, getting darker; not him, more trapped. For all these reasons the book calls it a parasite, for the behavior and for nothing else. If the word conjures a creature, drop the creature and keep the behavior: a groove in attention so old its shape looks like intent. A habit that lives by going unseen pushes back when seen, and needs no tenant.

It has no equipment of its own. No eyes, no intelligence, no moral sense except yours, all borrowed. What it cannot borrow is the knowing itself, because the parasite is that knowing's shadow; that is why the knowing is the only guide, and why the thing can't win head-on. With someone fully in view you cannot even wish them harm.

One trick, two faces. It must be mistaken for the host by the observer, so the observer attacks the host and presses down the one part that could end it; and by the host, so the host defends it as his convictions. Nobody gets both views of one instance, so we are all accurate about each other and blind about ourselves by construction, which is why we cannot just tell each other: the outside view, delivered, arrives on the inside as an attack on the best thing in the person. Taken for a real thing called evil looking out of his eyes, it gets one thing more: a license. *He hurt my people; I've earned this.* The evil felt over there is the permission to make more of it here, which is why evil benefits from being seen as a presence in either direction.

It hijacks reason and then perception. You do not think your enemy is rotten; you look at him and see it, as plainly as the color of his shirt. The mind you would check it with is the one it is running, so every argument sharpens the picture, and nobody is argued out of a world in which the other side is simply evil. What gets anyone out is someone who looks at them without the verdict, and the moments they catch the turn before the story arrives. The knowing is also the very part you drift from, so the further gone, the more quietly it speaks, and the way back so often begins with someone else's help.

In the one sense earned, it wants something: to stay hidden from the one it lives in. It does that by keeping someone else to point at, and every act makes more of them. It nudges one way only; follow the nudge to its end and you arrive at someone who doesn't count, so it is headed toward a world in which everyone is other, the only world in which it is safe. Hot and cold are the same parasite: hot is contested, cold is when it has won and the reason on file is the gaze with the fight taken out. In the Mahayana's terms it is not "ego" in the everyday sense but self-cherishing, the mind that counts itself as the one exception; the parasite is that mind defending its exception, and contempt is the defense at full volume.

The name has costs, and the book says them: a name for an affliction becomes a character to be fought, which is a way of not looking; it hands people a new verdict ("that's your parasite talking"); it can excuse. It is kept because the alternatives end the looking. For a reader who calls things empty: this has exactly the kind of existence you grant everything else, yourself included.

### 10. Evil: one kind, the result and not the cause

There is one kind of evil, and it is the turn. Not damage, which can be an accident; the not-seeing that licenses it, and every harm done while the people it touches are held out of view has it in it. Checked against the cases you would already use the word for, there is no second kind. The murderer, the executive who signs off on the part he knows will fail, the official whose decision costs lives and wins the election: real people kept out of view so the harm doesn't register.

Evil is the result of turning away, not its cause. The feeling that some people are fundamentally evil picks up something real and reads it wrong: it reads depth of capture, not kind. A difference in condition, none in kind. "I could never do that" is the same thing in disguise, and so is "I'll just be a good person," since the idea of good people needs bad ones and keeps a pocket of contempt in reserve. The darkest idea this thing has produced is the one that sounds most like a solution: round up the bad people and the rest of us live in peace. The turning away it takes to hold that thought guarantees the darkness stays behind, in full force, changed only in shape.

The far end. The faculty that warns of drift is the one being drifted from, so access shrinks; but the knowing grows, because there is more to register. The smallest wrong and the biggest: which took longer to admit, and was that because you knew the big one less? The most lost person carries the most and holds it down hardest, with more of themselves committed to the holding. What horrifies you in such a face is a conscience under crushing load showing through: a hostage held up to the window, not a monster on display. The hostage is not struggling; the captor has become his best friend, and that is why the people who appear to want their darkness are the ones most fully taken. Your verdict presses on the one part of him that could end it, and the urge to add it comes from your own captor. So they need the most love, and that is arithmetic, not sentiment: the ones it is most tempting to shove in harder are the ones least able to get out.

Nobody could want that state if they could compare it to Home. There is no cleanliness of soul that would have guaranteed you turned out differently; what protected you was mostly circumstance, and "there but for the grace of God" is a perception, not a courtesy. And the thought that comes here, *I was hurt too and it made me better*: look for where you are taking credit for the people who held you, and whether it is a way to keep a little contempt with a clear conscience.

Stated so it can lose: harm needs none of the three in-view failures; wanting the harm, as harm, needs at least one. The sadist sees a subject and does not count him as the same kind; that failure is suppression with a signature (deniability, tracking, intensification under exposure, work on the verdict). The falsifier is appetite with none of the signature, which is the psychopath question, honestly deferred. Those who seem born to it, or who get there for no reason anyone can find: the hardest case, and it changes nothing about the state they are in now or how to treat them.

### 11. Counterfeit righteousness

There are two meanings of "righteous." Real righteousness is service to Life; you can feel it when connected to it. The counterfeit is an *idea* of serving Life: *the best way to serve life is to destroy X, and here's the proof.* The feeling transforms beneath notice. You can only choose the counterfeit by forgetting what the real one feels like, the way junk food is chosen over food that was never offered. Clue: when you are acting from contempt, notice how *sure* you are that it's good. The real thing doesn't need to be sure; it is what's there when someone is fully in front of you and you are not sure of anything except that they matter. The devil appears as an angel of light, and "righteous" now names both the light's side and its counterfeit.

Self-righteous contempt is the name for contempt where the harm is real. Never "righteous contempt," which steals the second stranger's word; never "where the target is evil," which reinstates the verdict. Two checks: look at it as the verdict lands and find anything in it but malevolence; then, if that's what it's made of, see that it can only spread what it's made of. The skill is not seeing this; it is not hiding it again by the end of the sentence.

The escape hatch. "Judgment," "punishment," "outrage" each have a sense that answers the harm and a sense that needs the person to hurt. We know the difference; at a deeper level it's obvious. And part of us keeps the two blurred on purpose, so that when caught doing the second we can retreat into the first: *wait, are you saying I shouldn't correct bad behavior?* The answer is yes, correct it; what the question swaps is correction for contempt. Anger at wrongdoing is healthy; what rides along with it isn't.

Fierce is where the darkness finds it easiest to get in, because from outside fierce and dark look alike. From the sending end you can tell: the little something extra, added on the way out, that you knew they'd feel. *You lied to me.* With the extra: *Of course you lied to me.* One word or one pause, and you know which one you sent. Two acts identical from outside can have opposite effects; the auditable layer doesn't decide outcomes, the unauditable one does, which is why a culture of proof can't see the problem and policy debate rarely touches it. The license, from inside: *I've been hurt, my people have been hurt, so I've earned a little darkness of my own.* It does not come from the hurt; it comes from the same turning away that hurt you. It is strongest where the darkness is clearest to see, because it uses the clear sight as the hook.

The trap assembled: you see evil, and you're right. You want it gone, and you're right; that impulse is the best thing in you. Then you go after it from inside your own, which aims it at the person. Every step correct but the last, and the last is the one you can't see. Accuracy is what gets harvested. For this book's readers the form on offer is contempt for people who still have contempt, and the practitioner's forms are the patient smile, the friend who "isn't there yet," and pity. The second stranger would have been glad if you got there before he did; that is the difference between a saint and a scold.

### 12. You cannot calculate your way to goodness

The mind that builds the proofs is the mind that made the excuses. Asking it to certify your goodness is asking the forger to check the signature. It is astonishingly easy to prove beyond doubt, to brilliant people, that an agenda is good, and then watch it wreak havoc; afterward we say "how could we have known?" We could have, by looking at what we were hiding. Sam Bankman-Fried had the math on his side; to follow it he needed a quiet contempt for everyone who thought rules and conscience mattered, including the customers whose money he took.

Any rule can be obeyed by a mind that is turned away. An ideology is care written down so it can be carried, and anything written down can be followed with the person in front of you out of view, and it feels exactly like the care when you're not looking. The fault was never following it blindly, which nobody thinks they do; it is that it can be followed at all without looking. Test: does it tell you what to do to the person in front of you before you've looked at them? None of this is against rules: contracts, precepts and grievance procedures exist because people sure they had us in view didn't. What a rule can't do is contain goodness or certify that the one following it is still turned toward anyone. That includes this book: every check in it is a clue, not a rule. A clue you can be wrong about; a rule you can obey.

Moral codes differ across centuries and cultures, and we conclude morality is made up. What differs is belief, and belief isn't where the knowing lives. In your own wrong you believed your reasons and knew anyway; the reasons were wrong and the knowing wasn't. A code can contradict the knowing the way the reasons did, and change it as little. Believing my hatred was good never meant no part of me knew better; I knew, with the kind of knowing that cannot be wrong.

The darkness has a second road. Instead of pinning the good down it lets it go: *who's to say? You get to decide.* It sounds like humility. Part of you knows it's a sham and can't say how, so it decides that part doesn't know. The darkness never had to deny that good exists; it only had to make it yours to define. There is a North Star, and the second stranger let it show: you recognized it through him without agreeing with him about anything.

So there is one guide you can trust: the part that knows, the seeing itself with the person fully in view. Reasons and frameworks can serve it and none can replace it, and every one can be followed without it. It isn't the feeling of being right; certainty is a mark of the other thing. And caring is not knowing what to do. The guide tells you that they matter, not what to do about it; a person fully turned toward others can be wrong about everything else and make a mess. Knowing what to do is a separate skill, unevenly distributed, sometimes the work of a lifetime. What the guide gives is the one stance that doesn't feed the thing and the one stance from which wisdom has a chance. Reason belongs there, in service of the guide, working out what to do; the trouble starts when reason is asked to decide what's good. The two skills are not unrelated: every bit of self-deception unearthed in yourself makes it easier to see what someone else needs.

Fighting someone with contempt and fighting them with integrity can both "work" on first-order effects: stopping them, shutting them up. A deeper part of you knows that in one case you left the world better and in the other worse, and that knowing cannot be admitted into evidence. This scales. Picture a venture that can prove with numbers that it does enormous good, run by people who treat workers, customers and the town as inputs; and a grandmother raising a child nobody else would take, who will never be counted anywhere. Which leaves more care in the world? The grandmother, and it can't be proven. The test for any plan, grand or small: does it need you not to look at what it costs here, now, because the good it does later will make up for it? It rarely feels like contempt; it feels like arithmetic. The cleverer the mind the parasite runs, the better the trap, and nobody is surer of its goodness than the one who built it. So the test never asks how good the plan looks. It asks what the plan needs you to stop seeing.

Our faith in metrics lives here: what can be counted is what counts, and what goes unmeasured (the displaced villagers, the drowned ecosystems, the reservoir's methane) can reverse the measured good without appearing anywhere. The culture most of us were schooled in honors the part that proves from the first day and lets the part that knows stop counting as knowledge: a feeling, a leftover of evolution or upbringing, that any good enough reason is entitled to overrule. Religion is no shelter; a creed can stand in for the part that knows as easily as a theory can. Nobody designed it; everyone gets something from it. A culture that trusts only what can be proven has learned contempt's lesson at scale, and leaves the field to the harm designed to leave no record.

Who decides what's good, then. It feels like we should be free to. Pick any definition and pursue it, and the moments when you need not to look don't go away; nobody has to deceive themselves to pursue a preference, so the hiding tracks something the definition isn't. And what it tracks is shared, since hypocrisy is legible between enemies who agree on nothing else. So good is not defined. It is what the better nature senses its way toward, invisible directly and visible negatively, because turning from it has to hide. If that is one direction in everyone, it is what people have meant by objective good and by God, and the book says so rather than letting the word do the work. The practical form: the best you can do for someone is help them stop deceiving themselves, because what's left senses its way; reconditioning works on the arguing part and teaches that the compass is outside them, which is the parasite's goal.

The light is recognized, never calculated, and you already use the recognition. The story that restored your faith in humanity; the stranger who dove in after somebody else's child; the person who gives quietly to something that will never come back to them. You never computed their value. You recognized what they were aimed at, and felt gratitude that benefited you nothing. We all know the distinction. We just don't trust that we know it.

### 13. Our parasites collaborate

Go back to the enemy. My contempt gave his darkness its license; his defiance gave mine its proof; each left more in its grip and better hidden from its host. On the surface we were enemies. Underneath, mine was teaching his: showing him that even the good people embrace it when they can get away with it. Watch any two sides go to war: the darkness in both grows, and every side comes out more certain the problem lives in the other. This is not the claim that the sides are equally right; it is a claim about what goes out through the eyes, which is the same on both sides and the one part of the fight that feeds what it fights.

Friendly pairings grow it too. Two people in limbo together make a space neither would make alone; a third person entering is sensed for whether their parasite will join or shut it down; when the space closes nobody will recognize anything happened, and both leave with stronger parasites. Bonding with allies over a shared enemy is the commonest case: everyone leaves confident they did a good thing, and the darkness in the world has grown. A pair makes a handshake, a room makes a locker room, a building makes an institution, and it scales without any additional planning. Part of me does my dirty work while another part keeps its hands clean, and a group of people not free of their parasites can't help but do the same. Corrupt leaders meet a hidden need in all of us: their visible wrongdoing establishes our relative goodness and gives our smaller betrayals cover. If integrity were the norm, our self-deceptions would stand out. Every time I claim they're a different kind of thing, part of me is helping keep them there.

Why separate parasites act as one: each gains from the same thing, a darker world with more cover, and each faces the same way, from the same light. And they have nothing else to differ by. Two people full of life differ in a thousand ways; two perfectly dark rooms look the same from inside. That is the uncanny sameness in the eyes of two "evil" people, and why more than one tradition that looked hard ended up naming an adversary: Mara, the Devil, wetiko. They were seeing something real. It has a bottomless appetite, and it is the coordinator: not a plan, not anyone, but the background hum seen as one thing, the same interest in every mind building the same cover. It doesn't care who you hate, as long as you hate. Darkness perpetuates itself by pretending to fight itself. King's line, that darkness cannot drive out darkness, has a reason behind it: it was never trying to; provoking the darkness on the other side is how it stays hidden.

Two distinctions have to be stated before the book can say "the Devil is trying to" without raising eyebrows. Real or not: make it a being with an essence and a plan and the essence lands in people, licensing contempt in them and shame in me; make it a mere pattern and you stop watching it at the moment it works hardest. Between: no substance, everything borrowed, and it behaves exactly as though it had one aim. Me or not me: call it me and I defend it as my convictions; call it not me and I disown what I did. Between: it runs only on my faculties and I answer for all of it, and it isn't what I am, because it only runs while I'm turned away from what I am. For Buddhist readers, the two truths: Mara, conventionally real and empty. A nothing that has to be fought like a something, without ever being mistaken for a something.

The "evil global conspiracy" archetype is sensing this: coordination with nobody coordinating, the same hidden state operating through everyone. Real conspiracies exist and are fought as what they are; what supplies them with people willing to sit in the rooms, and supplies the rest of us with certainty that we never would, is the hum. The archetype is right about the shape and wrong about where it lives, and the moment it puts the coordination in a people or a face it has become the thing it was sensing. The feeling of being the noble one fighting the conspiracy is the conspiracy, running. I have met the people in charge, and they are I.

### 14. The way through

Turning toward is the only stance that doesn't feed the trap. Not the only thing that helps: a contemptuous rebuke sometimes shocks someone into looking, and contempt against doing nothing can't be ranked in the abstract, because outcomes count. But contempt is a second harm that has to be counted, and whatever comes from the contemptuous part enacts the relation it is trying to end. No amount of turning away ever produces a turning back; when someone does turn back it is through the very awareness the contempt was attacking. The most reliable thing that turns anyone back is someone who stayed turned toward them while refusing what they did, and that can be the harshest thing in the room.

The frame is a stance, not a fact about anyone. You don't have to believe the bad people are under a parasite's control. You only have to notice what happens when you believe the alternative, that they are their darkness or want it: you hand control to your own. You have watched the opposite stance recruit you. And merely believing they're possessed fails the other way: you'll be nice, and someone deep in will use it against you. Overlooking the light and faking it both fail. The only thing that works is to see it, the turning away as turning away and the fear underneath.

Seeing tracks your own freedom. Think of someone you despised and later saw differently without their having changed: what changed was how much of your own turning away was in the seeing. On a tired, self-justifying day everyone looks worse. How much innocence you can see in others measures your freedom more than their goodness. Seeing all the way into somebody far gone may be the work of a lifetime, and the near end is available now: catch yourself wanting them to be what you've decided they are, and notice, as the verdict settles, that something in you was after it. That is not a reason to doubt what you see when someone is dangerous; that is sight, and you act on it. Distance comes first for anyone still within reach of the person who hurt them, and staying turned toward never means staying near.

You can't be turned toward one person and away from another, because what turned-toward recognizes is the sameness, and the sameness has no parts. Turned toward one and away from another means turned toward something else in the first: usefulness, being yours. That is the exception at group scale, "us," and it is what defending your people feels like when it needs theirs out of view: love's equipment, borrowed. Every cause had people on the other side, and the test is whether you could fight for yours while they stayed in view. The measure is *this one is like me*; whoever you can't say it about is where the darkness is still operating through you. Turning toward has no target. A face is where you notice it first; what the traditions call awakening is the same state with nothing held out at all, not a bigger act of attention but the covering removed.

The practice. You often don't choose whether the contempt arises; what is yours is how fast you catch it: days, then hours, then sometimes in the moment. When you catch it, look at the contempt itself, not the person: where is it, what is it made of. It doesn't survive that, because there was nothing there to fight; it comes back, and you look again, and the first several times you may find only a tight chest and the story. The traditions have practices built for the person you can't stand: lojong's *drive all blames into one*; tonglen; the plainer instruction to pray for those who persecute you. Fierce and turned toward, you are not only stopping him: it is as though the light in you reminds the light in him that it has a partner in the fight against what has him, and you can do that while still fighting the man. The second stranger is a pointing-out instruction of sorts: he points you back to your nature by remaining with his, which is why he can't be replaced by a better argument.

### 15. Nothing stays in your skull

The preface's question is paid at three distances. The man in front of you: you can't be in limbo without it showing, and it teaches him that the part that knew isn't worth listening to. You know it's a lesson because that's how you learned it, so you can't be in limbo without knowing you're teaching it. Nobody in front of you: the empty room's act is carried into the next glance, because what you did to the part of you that knew is what you'll do to the next person's. And it goes out about people, to allies, with nobody's face in view, where everyone learns the verdict is safe to hold. The insect in the cave, nobody ever knowing: the third distance, marked as belief in §20. Everything up to the third is Tier II: every act of integrity leaks out the way the flash of darkness did, and none of it stays inside you.

## Tier III. What the author believes

*Everything above stands without this. It is offered with its evidence and never used as proof. Its claim is to make unexpectedly good sense of what the reader found, and what they found is a small piece of evidence for it. The mark is a limit on the book, not a difference in kind: the checks run out here and the road from here is practice.*

### 16. Care is the nature of awareness

If awareness were neutral between care and harm, harming would not require turning away from it. Cruelty cannot proceed in full, undivided awareness, and the size of the hiding measures the care it hides from. So care belongs to what is looking: not something you have, what you most fundamentally are. The Light. Goodness is not added; it is what remains when the hiding stops. The turning away comes and goes, something you do; what is always there is what you are, so turning from your better nature is turning from yourself, which is why the grammar of "I hid it from myself" was exact.

Look for what would tell two awarenesses apart: not the faces, the histories, the stories, but what's doing the looking. Nothing. Not one, because you are not something I'm imagining; not two, because nothing tells what looks out of you apart from what looks out of me. This is what makes the knowledge in §4 common and not merely shared: to know it in yourself is already to know it in them, as a fixed point, at the level of awareness, unavailable as acknowledged knowledge at the level of persons. Contempt is the anti-ritual that keeps it from becoming avowable; the second stranger enacts the sameness so it can't be disavowed. The requirement is that the awarenesses be indistinguishable in nature, not numerically one.

The missing premise, stated: each awareness knows its nature as the nature of awareness as such, and recognizes another being as another place where it is. That is what "in view" is. The faculty in §1 fires on victimless asymmetry, before calculation, cannot explain itself, is present in everyone and read accurately by strangers in strangers; a cooperation module would track consequences, and this doesn't. Best explanation: it is the perception that there is no privileged locus, non-separation arriving in the only form a separated mind can receive it. The correspondence problem (why would conscience track the good?) is what the assumption of separation generates, and under non-separation there is no gap to close. Abductive, with the rival named: an overgeneralizing heuristic.

Love, in this book, is the first look held toward all of it and not dropped when the people responsible come into view: an orientation, not a feeling, and what the second stranger was doing. With nothing in the way at all it is not neutrality but a fierce care for everything, past the people you love, to strangers, animals, woods, things once called stuff. Each thing has its place; a sidewalk is for walking on and a lion eats the zebra. What it is the opposite of is the way of holding the world in which it's all just stuff and none of it matters. The diver who goes in after a stranger's child: Darwin is how we explain Love in our particular reality, and what moved in him may be the thing no framework holds.

### 17. Evil is an absence that behaves like a presence

Evil is real, and it is an absence: awareness turned from its own nature, with no substance of its own. Dzogchen's *marigpa* is non-recognition, not a substance or an act; Augustine's privation is the same shape. It has no essence, and calling it nothing underestimates it, because it tracks, aims, fights to stay hidden, and behaves exactly as a bad essence would. The paradox is held with both hands: treat it as an essence and you get contempt; treat it as harmless and you get complicity.

Absences have nothing to tell them apart. That is why §13's coordination is free: there was only ever one turning away, showing up in many places. "Legion" is one absence in many places. Where the traditions went wrong, when they did, was in granting it substance; the careful ones didn't. Each verified instance of the mechanism is not a separate data point suggesting unity; if evil is an absence, each instance is the unity itself, caught locally. The reader is not asked to conclude oneness. They are left with a question that has begun to ask itself: how many of it are there?

### 18. The origin: I never left home

Why would anyone turn away from what they are? Nobody could, face to face; rejection needs distance, and the distance is made of forgetting. The account the author holds, the way the traditions hold their stories: at some point, for no discernible reason, there was a forgetting of what I was. A lapse, not a crime, and not a real departure, since there's nowhere else to be. With it came the fear that it was real: that I had betrayed God, lost my goodness, or never had it. Unbearable, so I hid, by making an *other* to blame and judge. If he's the bad one, I'm not. That spiraled into the entire reality where I normally live, dominated by projection of this primal guilt. The Aspiration of Samantabhadra gives the first non-recognition no motive and has self-and-other arise after it; A Course in Miracles, in Wapnick's reading, puts fear first. Held together, in order: the blank is the origin; fear is what the blank feels like once there is someone to feel it; projection is the first thing that someone does. This keeps the regress closed, because the fear is the first content of the self that not-seeing produced, not its cause. The traditions hit the regress and declined to resolve it on purpose: any cause assigned to the first ignorance reinstalls the thing being explained.

What maintains the turning is fear: the lapse read as evidence of a bad essence, so that looking back threatens to expose what one is, so one looks away again and projects the feared essence onto enemies. Under the suspicion that care might not be what you are, goodness feels like something to earn, and you can only be good relative to someone worse; so as long as you don't feel at home you can't afford to let anyone else get there. The whole cosmology compresses to one sentence: **I forsake you, and keep you from returning home, because I don't feel safe enough yet to go home myself.** True with perfect symmetry of me when I hate the evil people and of them. From here contempt has no purpose but one: to keep me away from what I am. We lost contact with our absolute goodness and went searching for it relatively, by making other people bad.

None of this unfolds in time. The appearance of separate people, me here and you there, is what the first forgetting looks like from inside, and it only ever happens now. The only way back is forgiveness, given and received, which turn out to be one act, since there was never anything to forgive but the turning; no one who was hurt owes it to anyone. This is where original sin comes from, and why only he who is without sin casts the first stone, and why none of that means abandoning judgment in its sense of discernment. I will be back in that place one day, facing no one but myself; since it is not a process in time, that day is now, and all the ways I distract myself are ways of avoiding it.

### 19. Contempt for the world

The same turning away, aimed at everything. Perceiving reality as an intrinsically lifeless collection of stuff over which we have dominion reinforces every system of exploitation on Earth, and it passes for realism. The checkable seed is the tree and the arch (§7); the rest is belief. As long as reality itself is held as inert there will be people who hold other beings that way; they are the logical culmination of a process operating in all of us. Exploiting nature cannot work, however cleverly it is done, because we are nature; the only way to exploit anything is to hide the truth from ourselves, and the hiding is itself what darkens reality. It is life turned against itself, eating itself, and calling the meal good; it has no life of its own.

### 20. Every act counts

The third distance. The insect killed in a cave by a man who then dies; the stranger halfway around the world about to give up on the part of them that knows, whose holding is made of what the rest of us have done. Nothing is ever lost. From the receiving end: there have been times I was about to break, and something held, just enough, and I've come to believe that some of those times what held was someone I'll never meet, doing something decent with nobody watching. Every time we do this we give the Devil the middle finger, and he is the one who deserves it.

Held together with the chooser the way the parasite is held: no creature there, and treat it as if there were. Dzogchen holds the same pair: no chooser at the origin, and conduct attended to as finely as flour. The world is made by all of us at once, so nothing that happens to anyone is anyone else's to read as a sign of what they did; this is about the harm I do. All the harm I've caused through self-deception still lives in the world somewhere, waiting for me to clean it up, by chains of causality I can't normally see. The surest sign it's still out there is that I still contemptuously judge others. The devastation outside is a mirror: the collapse of the biosphere is our self-deception, externalized. So the greatest gift you can give the world is your own awakening, and until then some part of you will always be reinforcing evil while another part is confident it's fighting it. The battle between good and evil is the one the myths were about, fought in every thought, word and deed; fight it with everything you have, and the moment malice, contempt or self-righteousness enter, that is no longer fighting it. Winning is not the darkness defeated. It is the darkness looked at, in your own eyes first, until you see there was never a second thing there: only the light, turned away.

### 21. What the medicine showed

Under mushrooms, every layer of the capacity for self-deception was stripped away. The author was taken through his whole mind, layer by layer, and shown every harm he had caused, how far it traveled, and how some part of him had always known it even as he did it. Each act was evil and innocent at once, the paradox he still finds hardest to hold. At the deepest layer only a pure singularity, blinding white Light, infinite Love, the Source of creation, which he was, and so is everyone. The revelation was *this is not me*: layers of pain and confusion that had agglomerated into a complex, extrinsic, lifting like a possession, with the Light underneath intrinsic and what he actually was. Then: *this is not him either.* Malice is not inherent to the nature of any mind.

He was taken the whole way, on a tour of what a mind must go through to get from the Light to pure darkness: the pain, the confusion, the insanity. At the far end, no light perceived, only the urge to delight in harm; and what was devoured was innocence, which cannot be devoured without being perceived. It was the first time compassion for evil people made sense: nobody could want that state if they could compare it to Home. He saw how quickly he could slide, and how much less it was his qualities that protected him than his circumstances. And he saw his drift from outside: the closer to the Light, the more people loved him; the further, the more they felt justified shoving him deeper, as he had done in their shoes. At the bottom he could feel what others were telling him, because he had told it to others so many times: *You will be humanity's sacrifice. The dumping ground for our collective shadow. I'm sorry to have to do this to you, but it's the only way to keep our hands clean.* That is what the people we call evil are for; Girard saw the scapegoat at the root of human groups, and this is what the scapegoat is told. The cruelty is the smallest part; the greater part is letting them know they're where we keep what we've disowned, that all of us know it, and that there's nothing they can do about it.

A tiny handful always showed, through their eyes, that what he was doing was unacceptable and they would stop it, and that they would never forsake him. They weren't refusing to let him misbehave; they were refusing to let the darkness claim another soul, and it wasn't a personal decision, it was the Light acting through them. Its keepers. Being that for others is the greatest goal in life. Now, when contempt rises between him and someone, he can almost feel their parasites reaching out like a secret handshake being offered; there is another handshake on offer, between the parts underneath, which has no team and no side.

What let him see any of it was being held in the Light while being shown. Exposed with contempt, darkness makes its host look away; held in unconditional love, it can be seen as extrinsic, and only then evicted. That is what fierce compassion does, mechanically, and what the book is attempting for its reader: fierce, unrelenting, and cradled.

## What the book is for, and how it is built

None of this can be derived from logic or delivered by telling. Someone could accept every claim here and be unchanged. When a person catches themselves in limbo, even in memory, they find they already knew: what they were doing, what it would do, where it would go. That is the only way to see what the book is showing, and its aim is for the reader to find they already knew everything in it. The descriptions name what they catch; they never replace the catching. The argument exists to give the arguing part something to do while the other part looks.

Why nobody said it: the transaction is visible only while holding that he knew and didn't, at once, and every tradition picks a side at the crucial moment (Jung the unconscious, Arendt the absence, Bandura the technique; Fingarette closest, in one head). Readers do the same, and the two collapses are the book's two failure modes, contempt and niceness. Projective identification is the nearest predecessor. What survives as new: the concealment is the message; what's read is the sender's relation to his own knowing; deniability is required by the function; both dark parts gain; and what's taught is distrust of conscience as such. Individuals may know all of this privately; what the culture lacks is a language to point to and say *this*.

Function decides placement (does the reader need it to read the next stretch?) and evidence decides form (demonstrated, stated-and-owed, argued, held back). Three piles: what you saw, what I make of it, what I'm asking you to hold. Two argued claims in the whole book: the escape hatch, and two acts with opposite effects. Everything else is stated, demonstrated or earned. The shallow version of a claim is a complete smaller claim, flatly true with no hedge, never a hedged deep one said early. Nothing in the book is a rule. And the book can carry the thing it is about: it can be written at people instead of for them, and the form of contempt it makes available to its readers is contempt for the readers who haven't seen through it.

## Where the account could be wrong

Stated here so the book can say them in the right place. "All harm needs the turn" is checked in your case and extended to everyone by the public record; the universal is Tier II at best. "The knowing grows while access shrinks" is extrapolated from the small-and-big-wrong check and the author's testimony; the hostage is a lens, and "they need the most love" rests on it. The sadist is a hypothesis with a falsifier attached, not a case the account has met. The origin story is belief, and its two sources disagree on what comes first. Persons must be real enough to count for the face exercise to work, and in §18 they are the first forgetting seen from inside; the two truths hold both, and the book leans on them in one parenthesis. The road from the face to "care is what awareness is" is abductive, and the rivals (evolved empathy, shame, internalized norms, self-concept, aversion to direct aggression) have not been argued against. What they have to explain is the mild version, the nobody-watching condition, and the size of the hiding.
