# Appendix 1: What Contempt Presupposes

*For the reader who wants the argument stated explicitly, with the seams marked. Nothing in the book rests on this; the book rests on what you found when you looked. The short version's Chapter 2 says the whole of this in a few pages, in one voice. This appendix says which sentences of it you checked, which are inferred, and which are belief, and it takes the trap apart to show how it's built.*

*Section 1 is the trap, built up from one sentence. If you want the argument checked step by step, section 2 lays it out with the seams marked.*

## 1. The trap

The particular limbo called contempt is hiding something (X) from yourself while revealing it to me. And what is X? The fact that you're hiding X from yourself while revealing it to me. It is self-referential, with no base case. So how does it deliver anything meaningful?

Because hiding-while-revealing is the signature of being up to no good while pretending (in fact, believing) it's righteous. And you depend on this fact being common knowledge,[^ck] because look at what happens when it is.

A look is enough. Nothing has to be said, because both of you already know what a turned-away face aimed at you is for. Here is what that one look delivers, from the target's side, in the order the pieces land:

- **The turn.** He's turned away from you, and you read it.
- **The intent.** You know what an aimed turn is for. He means harm.
- **The wanting.** He wants you to recognize the intent. If he didn't, this would be a leak, not a message.
- **The injury.** Your recognizing it is already the harm; no separate deed has to follow. But the recognition is also meant to produce a reaction, and the reaction is going to become his proof.
- **The disavowal.** He will deny the intention if you name it, and he'll believe the denial.
- **The proof.** So your protest becomes evidence of the verdict that provoked it, and you know that in advance.

That's the meaningful content, and it's why the sentence with no base case isn't empty. Its base is the shared knowledge of what hiding-while-revealing means. Call the whole payload **H**: being made to know, without recourse, that he has turned away from you and intends this very knowledge to hurt you. He inflicts H by making you recognize that he intends to inflict H. The act's purpose is its own reception. It has real content at every ingredient, and nothing outside itself to point to, and that lack is what "without recourse" means: there's nothing auditable to produce against him, so anything you produce is your reaction, and your reaction is his evidence. The disavowal and the proof aren't added on. They follow from H having nothing outside itself, and you know that in advance, so they're part of what it delivers. That's where it closes.

Three other things sit around H, and they're easy to mistake for it. The **reflection**: you recognize that he meant you to recognize H, he knows you recognize it, you recognize that he knows. Those levels add nothing to H; each reflects the same thing, which is why the mirrors feel bottomless and why the bottom is never new. The **loop in time**: if you protest, he takes the protest as evidence of the verdict that provoked it, the renewed verdict sends H again, and the hall of mirrors goes on for years. And the **hiding that is the showing**, which is what makes all of it possible without any auditable act: his intention is unowned by him, and the non-ownership is part of what you're meant to see. He doesn't hide the intention and then let it slip. What he reveals is that he's hiding it.

Why contempt and not malice in general? All malice hides one thing from the one doing it: the sameness of the target, held down even in the sadist. But malice as such needs neither of two things contempt needs. It need not be received; the target can be asleep while the deed is done. And where it does demand reception, a threat, a sadist's display, it needn't disavow itself as righteous; the honest villain says *yes, I mean you harm, and no, you're not my kind.* Contempt needs both. The target must receive the harmful intention, which makes the recognition the payload, and the sender must sincerely deny that it's harmful, which puts the intent in limbo and turns every protest into proof. The trap is generated by that conjunction and by nothing less. It's why malice is frightening and contempt is maddening.

**Now look at what you were depending on.** For the look to deliver all of that, several things had to be true of the person in front of you, and you counted on every one:

- That they're the same kind of thing you are, since they read the move the way you'd have read it.
- That there's a standard the two of you share, since what they felt was hypocrisy, and hypocrisy needs a shared standard and a departure from it.
- That the standard is care, and that turning away is the departure from it: what you're like when nothing is in the way, and what happens when something is.
- That the care in them is still there, obscured and not gone, since a verdict can only sting someone who can still feel what it denies.
- That they can still turn back, since the whole point of the message was to push them further away, and a rock doesn't need provoking.

Each of those is the opposite of what the verdict says. The verdict says *this is all you are*: no sameness, no shared standard, nothing left to reach, nothing to turn back. And the act can't work unless all of them are false. Contempt can tell someone *this is all you are* only by addressing the capacity in them that the verdict denies exists. Its explicit message denies equality. Its successful operation presupposes it.

And look at what those five are, stated without the labels. That the mind's nature is care. That the confusion covering it is not the same thing as it, and doesn't touch it. That the covering is a turning away, not a substance. That recognition is available, and the way back. Anyone who practices has heard all four before, as the view. Here they arrive as the conditions your own contempt was counting on, in the one act that denies them loudest. You didn't have to believe them. You had to have been using them, and you were.

**The fixed point, for those who want it written down.** Let G(P) mean *make the target know, without recourse, that you intend P.* Then H = G(H), a fixed point in the ordinary sense, and no iteration from a seed is needed to define it. What's unusual is its content: an ordinary fixed point of a message-operation would have a threat or a claim inside the wrapping, and H has nothing inside it but the ground it stands on, the sameness, held out of view, and the fact of holding it. You can write the reflection down too, as M for the turn and F(P) for *hide P from yourself while showing it to me*, and the tower M, F(M), F(F(M)). Keep one thing straight if you do. The tower doesn't collapse as propositions; "I'm hiding that I'm hiding it" is a different sentence from "I'm hiding it." What collapses is the acting. There is one act in the encounter, turning away from the sameness, and every level of the tower is that act under a further description. No depth calls for a new act, which is why the sender never has to climb it and why it fits in one look. Limbo is that act from inside; F is the act as a witness describes it; H is the act named by what it does. And the tower doesn't start with the witness. Limbo contains itself in one person, before anyone is watching. The witness adds no depth. He's the one person standing where the act can be seen, because he's turned away from nothing.

Now look at what's in the room. Other things are present, but every capacity doing anything here belongs to the one faculty, the recognition of a perceiver of the same kind, present in two bodies and turned against itself in one. The darkness contributes no equipment of its own. Everything needed to send the message, conceal it, receive it, and answer it is borrowed from the faculty whose sameness the message denies.

H has no external payload beyond its own delivery. If it had one, a slur, a shove, a lie you could catch, you'd have something to point to. Names for it exist and each catches a piece: hostile metacommunication, a double bind, deniable provocation, gaslighting. What doesn't exist is a name that survives being said to his face, because every name is auditable and the act isn't. Say *you're being contemptuous* and you've made a claim about tone, which he can deny, and now you're the one making things unpleasant.

## 2. The argument, laid out

*Section 1 in the order a checker wants it: what you observed, what's derived, and where each seam is.*

**The claim.**

Moral contempt says *this is all you are*. It can only say that by addressing the capacity in them that the verdict denies exists. Its explicit message denies equality. Its successful operation presupposes it. (Moral, to set it apart from disdain for someone's competence or taste, which carries no verdict on what they are. Everywhere in this book, contempt means the moral kind.)

**Checked, in your own case (Tier I).**
- O1. Intending harm to someone requires holding them out of view.
- O2. Holding someone out of view takes work, and the work rises under exposure.
- O3. Contempt lands as a knowingly disavowed double standard, and the sender counts on its being read that way.

- O4. Contact carries care: seeing another person carries the wanting that they be all right, before any thought.
- O5. Turning away obscures the care; it doesn't remove it. It's still there when you look back.

**What each does.** O1 to O3 are the mechanism of one encounter. O4 and O5 say what's being turned from, and that it survives the turning. Nothing is assumed. What the act needs in order to work, its coherence conditions, is derived from these five; and since the act worked, the conditions held.

**What the act presupposes.** That the other person's faculty is the same kind as yours, since they read the move. That there's a standard shared by both of you which turning away departs from, since what they read was hypocrisy; and by O4, that the standard is the care. That the care in them is present, obscured, and movable. Each of those is the opposite of the verdict.

**What follows.**

Turning away from them is turning away from your own caring, one act. Limbo is that same turning away seen from inside: the knowledge is right there and not let in, because the care it would show doesn't go anywhere. One act, not two conditions. The state is hidden from the one in it, because the faculty that would show it to him is the one he's turned, and visible to everyone else, because theirs isn't. Contempt is that state made legible to its target on purpose, with the verdict attached. And the target can be moved, which is what the sender is counting on.

**Conclusion.**

The act presupposes what it denies, as a condition of its possibility. Contempt works only on someone who can harm only by turning away and who can turn, and on nobody else. And you already knew this, because every act of it that worked was relying on it (in the sense section 4 makes exact).

**The seams.**

That you relied on the person's recognition and responsiveness is Tier I. That what they recognized it with is care, present and obscured, is O4 and O5. That care is their nature in the working sense, the baseline that turning away departs from, is what hypocrisy presupposes. That it's the nature of awareness as such is a further reading, marked at the end.

**The punchline, marked as belief.**

If the care is what awareness is, then evil is awareness turned away from its own nature, and you can only do it to another by first forgetting it in yourself. And the knowledge is common because nothing tells the two awarenesses apart: to know it in yourself is to know it in him. Contempt is coherent only if, somewhere, you already know that.

### What was checked, and what follows

**Five things you checked.** O1: you tried to give a stranger the gaze and couldn't; you could see them, and the intention lost its footing. To keep it, you'd have had to stop seeing them first. The converse holds too, with a purpose attached.[^converse] O2: the stranger tracked your every exit, and pointed at, the behavior intensified in the direction needed to stay hidden; and your own flash, in When You Send It, had to be aimed, land, and go unrecorded, none of it calculated. O3: when it landed on you, what you felt wasn't threat or disapproval but hypocrisy: he was doing what he condemned you for, meant you to feel it, and would deny it. And he was appealing to your capacity to recognize the violation while treating you as the kind of thing that has no such capacity. From the sending end, you counted on exactly that. O4: hold a stranger's eyes and the wanting that they be all right arrives with the looking, before any thought; O1 is the same fact from the other side, since if seeing were neutral, harm wouldn't need to break contact first. O5: in the limbo recall, the knowledge that it was wrong wasn't new information; it had been there the whole time, and when the second stranger made it safe to look, the care was there to be found. Each of those is a claim about experience, open to a reader who looked and found otherwise.

**What follows, in five steps.** Turning away from them is turning away from your own caring, since by O4 the care is in the seeing; one act, and it's why a dehumanizing gaze reads as someone absent from themselves. Limbo is that act seen from inside: by O5 what's turned from stays, unlooked at, which is Chapter 1's definition of limbo word for word; so limbo isn't a second condition harm requires, and it's hidden from the one in it because the faculty that would show it to him is the one he's turned, and visible to you because yours isn't. The state shows, since you can tell whether somebody is seeing you only because you're seeing them. Contempt is that state made legible to its target on purpose, with the verdict attached and under a righteous disavowal; the disavowal is forced, since he can't own it. And the target can be moved, since by O5 the faculty is present and turnable; that's what the sender is counting on, and the faculty that can be pushed further is the faculty that could have turned back. Call a person **fundamentally innocent** if they can harm only by turning away, and can turn. Not harmless; everyone the book calls redeemable is this, and only this.

**What the act presupposes.** By O3, for contempt to land the target has to recognize the same turning away in the sender that the sender condemns in them, and feel it as hypocrisy. That requires that they can still tell a turned-away face from a looking one, and that the standard being violated is one they hold. So the act presupposes that his faculty is the same kind as yours, and that the target is not exhausted by the verdict, since its effect depends on a capacity the verdict says they don't have. Hypocrisy alone doesn't settle whether a harmful disposition might also be intrinsic; it settles that both people recognize a shared standard and the sender's departure from it. O4 says what the standard is, the care, and O5 that it survives the departure. So the message *this is all you are* can function only by addressing the part of them that knows it isn't. Contempt works only on the fundamentally innocent, and on nobody else. Its explicit message denies equality. Its successful operation presupposes it. That isn't a by-product; it's a condition of the act's possibility. Contempt can portray darkness as someone's nature only by exploiting the shared knowledge that it isn't.

**The title.** By the first step, evil at the level of the person names the turning: your own caring held out of view so that theirs can be. Each premise supplies a word of that: O1 the *is*, since the harm and the turning are one event; O4 the *from what*; O5 *no substance*, since what turns away obscures and adds nothing. Contempt is that turning made legible with the verdict attached, so it isn't a response to evil or a symptom of it. It's the thing itself, made into a message, and that isn't a judgment of anyone. And O3 says you already knew it. The cover, *mine is the good kind*, is refuted not by the fact that it stung, since a clean confrontation stings too, but by what they recognized in the sting: the turning away, the intention that it land, and the disavowal. If it had been the good kind, there'd have been nothing of that shape to recognize, and it would have landed as the second stranger's did. The title isn't a claim the book asks you to accept. It's a claim your own contempt has been counting on.

Two things the word usually carries, and both are wrong, and you already know they're wrong. The first is size. We reserve "evil" for the extreme, but watch what cutting in line does to you. The cost is thirty seconds. The heat is out of all proportion to thirty seconds, because what you're reacting to isn't the cost. It's the exception: somebody put you out of view to get ahead, and let you see it. Chapter 1 found the same thing from inside, in the flash that was shameful to admit out of proportion to its size; the smallest morsel carries the whole. The second is outcomes. We think "bad" is about damage done, but the friend who forgot your birthday did more damage by any accounting than the friend who made one small cutting remark, and the remark is the one you're still thinking about a week later. The corpse case has no damage at all and everyone knows it's wrong. Two acts identical from outside can have opposite effects. What you're tracking, in every one of those, is the turning, and you were tracking it before this book named it.

## 3. A prediction

If contempt were an increasingly accurate perception of badness, then the clearer the observer, the more of it they'd hold toward worse people, or at least the more they'd converge on it. If the verdict is the sender's own turning away showing itself, clarity should do the opposite: preserve or sharpen the perception of the harm while dissolving the verdict. The wise person sees the wrongdoing at least as clearly as you do and sees less essence, not less harm.

The second is what we see. Two strangers looked at one act, and one delivered the verdict and one didn't; the act didn't decide it. The people we call evil hold the most contempt, not the least; contempt for their victims is how the harm was done. And the people we'd call wise are the ones who can look at the worst person, seeing everything you see, without the verdict forming. Not because they're soft, and not because they're suppressing it. They haven't lost contact with the thing in them that recognizes the person, so there's nothing for a verdict to be made of, and the harm is as visible to them as it is to you. Which says what *now I finally see what they really are* is. The darkness you see is real, and that's why the sentence has force. The *really are* isn't a perception of them. It's your own turning away, reading itself as insight, and it's available to you only because you've lost contact with the thing that would have recognized them. That last one is intuitive rather than measured, and I'm not going to dress it up. But it's a differential prediction, and the perception account gets it backwards.

The same gradient is the fear account in Chapter 16 from the outside. The more a person has turned away, the more feared essence there is to put somewhere, and the more faces it needs.

## 4. The finale, with its seams

Every time your contempt landed, you were relying on the person in front of you not being exhausted by their darkness: on their recognition, and their responsiveness, still being there to reach. O4 and O5 say what remained is care, present and obscured. The act itself needs the care to be what they are when nothing is in the way. The further reading says it's what awareness is, in anyone. If that reading is right, then the book's central premise, that nothing has to be built and the only work is turning back, isn't a hope laid over the evidence. It's the deepest account of what your own contempt was already counting on. Every act of it that worked was coherent only because you already knew this, and kept it where you couldn't own it.

The seams, one per sentence. That you relied on their recognition and responsiveness is checked: if the flash landed, they read the hypocrisy, and you counted on their reading it. That what they recognized it with is care is O4 and O5, checked in your own case. That the care is the baseline turning away departs from is what hypocrisy plus O4 gives, so it's derived. That the care is the nature of awareness as such, rather than of human beings, is belief, in section 5, with its evidence in Appendix 3. Why that reading isn't arbitrary: it makes a prediction the other reading can't, that contempt tracks the sender; it matches the shape four traditions reached by other roads; and it survives the hardest case so far. And two things the sentence doesn't say. It doesn't say the turning is easy, or that not-seeing can be reversed by deciding to see; what's trained is how fast you catch it. And it doesn't say the turning is enough. Being free of self-deception makes you honest, not wise.

And what is not claimed.

Not that turning away is sufficient for harm. It also produces numbness, avoidance, and paralysis, with nobody harmed. The direction that holds without qualification is that harm or use requires it. The converse holds with the purpose clause attached, which is how O1 states it.

Not the cold form. The argument is about contempt, the aimed form. The general rule for harm and use rests on O1, checked in both directions in The How, not on this page.

Not universality, from this page. O1 to O3 are claims about experience, stated as holding for everyone and open to anyone who looked and found otherwise; the interlude says what that finding would count for. The deduction itself needs one working instance, and your own, from When You Send It, is enough for it: if the flash landed, the structure held in that room, for both of you. That it holds in every room is what the rest of the book argues, case by case, and this page doesn't.

Not that every target can be reached in practice. In principle, (f) entails it: the faculty is there to be turned. What the page says nothing about is how much has to be true around a person before turning back is survivable, and Chapter 12 holds that for some it may not happen in a lifetime. Movable in principle is not the same as near.

## 5. What I believe, marked

*From here I'm saying what I believe about what the care is. The deduction reaches this door and stops. Nothing practical depends on going through it.*

The deduction gives *present, obscured, movable*, and that care is the baseline turning away departs from. It does not by itself give that care is intrinsic to awareness, as against a baseline human beings evolved into, which could have been otherwise and could be missing in some. A naturalist can accept every step above under the second reading, since the argument needs only the capacity present and readable. I offer the first reading anyway, lightly. If the care is what awareness is, then evil is awareness turned away from its own nature, and you can only do it to another by first forgetting it in yourself; limbo, turning away, and the parasite are one thing named from three sides; and the knowledge is common because nothing tells the two awarenesses apart, so to know it in yourself is already to know it in him. Not that the awarenesses are one; only that nothing distinguishes them in nature, which is all the mutual recognition needs. On that reading, awakening is the implicit becoming explicit: what was already counted on between two people becomes sayable by the person, and contempt is the act that keeps it from being said while both go on knowing it. Appendix 3 states the correspondences, the evidence for this reading, and the test that would count against it. What decides it, in the end, isn't the argument. It's what you find under your own hiding when the hiding stops, and the book can only take you to where you can look.

**A note on Gödel.** The comparison will occur to some readers. It's an analogy, not an argument, and the analogy is to the second theorem. A formal system strong enough to matter can't certify its own consistency using only its own resources; a stronger system can assess it from outside, and then the question moves to the stronger one. The lesson contempt teaches, demote the part that knows and be only the part that proves, asks the reasoning part to vouch for itself in the same way, and the reasoning that justifies an act can't show, by producing more justification, that it hasn't been recruited. That's Chapter 6's claim in Gödel's shape. What the theorem doesn't supply is that the outside faculty is sound: it gives truth conditional on consistency, established from outside, and the analogue, that the part that knows is right if it isn't itself turned away, is a problem the book carries rather than a result it has.

## Where else to look

**On common knowledge.** Lewis, _Convention_ (1969); Aumann (1976), for the definition. Chwe, _Rational Ritual_ (2001), for the public event that usually produces it. The modal-logic literature on unawareness, for knowledge present in a state without being available for use. Section 1 is the contrast; section 5 develops the analogue under its own premises.

**On what it is.** Bateson et al. (1956) on the double bind: a demand, a contradicting demand at another level, and no way to comment on the contradiction. Pinker, Nowak & Lee (2008), "The logic of indirect speech," on content conveyed _so as not to_ become common knowledge; the trap is an innuendo whose subject is itself. Goffman on face-work, for the auditable surface the act hides behind.

**On limbo.** Fingarette, _Self-Deception_ (1969): self-deception as declining to _spell out_ what you're doing. Sartre's bad faith; Mele as the deflationary rival. Kunda (1990) on motivated reasoning: the search stops at the wanted conclusion.

**On the leak being functional.** Trivers; von Hippel & Trivers (2011); Kurzban (2010). Adjacent, with one inversion: here the leak is the payload.

**On the two strangers, measured.** Tangney & Dearing, _Shame and Guilt_ (2002): "I did a bad thing" repairs, "I am bad" hides and attacks. Braithwaite, _Crime, Shame and Reintegration_ (1989): stigmatizing versus reintegrative shaming, with outcome data. Gilligan, _Violence_ (1996): shame as the universal precursor to violence.

**On turning away as a perceptual event.** Harris & Fiske (2006): social-perception regions go quiet for dehumanized groups. Gray & Wegner on moral typecasting: seen as agent, unseen as sufferer. Bandura on moral disengagement, which catalogs limbo's toolkit.

**On the arrangement.** Girard on the scapegoat. Baumeister, _Evil_ (1997), for the exterior view of the license, the terminus, and the myth of pure evil. Browning, _Ordinary Men_ (1992).

**On the shadow.** Jung.

**On privation.** Augustine, _Enchiridion_.

[^converse]: The other direction says that when you hold a person out of view, it's in order to do something their being in view would stop, so the holding-out and the permission are one event seen from two sides. That needs one distinction. Walking through an airport, you don't take in the hundred people around you as people. They're in the background, and that isn't turning away: if one of them dropped a bag and started crying, they'd come forward into view at once, and you'd feel the pull. Turning away is keeping someone out of view when they'd otherwise come forward, because their reality would place a claim on you. People also turn from each other to escape grief, or fear, or a claim they can't meet, and that isn't this page's subject.

[^ck]: Game theorists call something common knowledge when everyone knows it, everyone knows that everyone knows it, and so on without end. It normally takes a public event to build: a trusted party says the thing aloud where everyone can see everyone hear it. The child in *The Emperor's New Clothes* adds no information; everyone already knew. He makes it common knowledge. The term has two uses on this page. The rule, that holding someone out of view is for harm, is common knowledge in Lewis's sense: a convention nobody was told, grounded in everyone having been on both ends. The encounter itself is not common knowledge in the game theorist's sense. Nobody says anything, nobody is trusted, and nothing becomes established; for him to know that you know, he'd have to know your faculty was working on this the way his was, and neither of you can verify that. What's there instead is shared experience, and shared experience is enough.
