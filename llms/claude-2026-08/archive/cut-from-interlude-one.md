## Notes for the integration

**On claim 4's rivals.** The two-line dismissal ("bias does none of those things") should not survive anywhere. The replacement: name motivated reasoning, status defense, shame avoidance, and dominance-reward as *what the agent is built from*, and state the claim that makes this more than relabeling — they share one blind spot, and it fails the same way for all of them when the thing looked away from is put back in view. For motivated reasoning that's Kunda's finding: people stop searching when they reach the conclusion they wanted. That's a prediction, and it's the difference between a unification and a tautology.

**On claim 9.** Recast throughout as *recursive mutual recognition under deniability*. That's what the stranger showed. "Common knowledge" and "everyone, accurately" are the appendix's overclaim leaking into the body.

**On the hot/cold vocabulary.** If motivated reasoning, negligence, and bureaucracy are all "turning away," keep "contempt" for the hot interpersonal form only. Otherwise the title word stops doing work.

**On sadism.** Not in the cut material, but it's the case that tests the whole thing and belongs in Part C §10. Tier II answer: the sadist sees the victim as a *subject* (the pleasure requires it) but not as the *same kind of thing as himself* (the permission requires it). Tier III answer: the deeper thing he isn't seeing is that they are him. State the first as a hypothesis with the test attached: find someone who passes the same-kind check and harms anyway.

## The original text, unchanged

## What this adds up to

What follows are the claims of this book. I'm setting them out here so you'll recognize them when they come back, not because I've earned them yet; most get a chapter of their own. Beside each one I've put the ordinary explanation, the version where none of this is anything special, so you can weigh the fit yourself.

**1. Conscience isn't a rulebook, and it isn't a harm detector.**

It detects one thing: the claim that some of us count for more than others.

And notice how it detects it. Not by weighing the claim and finding it false. It registers that somebody had to go out of focus before you could make it.

*The ordinary explanation:* a fast learned rule about harm, firing quickly.

*The trouble:* your conscience went off when nobody was being harmed and nothing had been worked out. A detector tuned for harm shouldn't fire when there's none. This one does, every time.

**And this is why nothing in this book is a rule, and nothing in it will be.** I'm not going to tell you that self-interest is wrong, or anger, or punishment, or making an exception of yourself. Each of those is sometimes exactly right. The moment I hand you a rule, two things happen, and you've watched both already. Your conscience gets a substitute to hide behind. And the part of you that argues gets something whose letter it can satisfy while betraying its spirit. It is extraordinarily good at that, and that is most of how the harm in this book gets done. What I can give you are clues. A clue you can be wrong about. A rule you can obey.

**2. What decides whether an act turns somebody toward what they know or away from it isn't anything you could write down.**

Two strangers, nearly the same words, opposite results. And the second could have been harsher and still worked.

*The ordinary explanation:* one of them had a better manner.

*The trouble:* manner was only the channel. What came through it was a verdict about what you are, and no transcript would have caught it.

I'm avoiding the words *good* and *harmful* here on purpose. Whether an act helps is a further question, and often a hard one; there are people for whom the most helpful thing is prison. What Chapter 1 showed you is narrower and more certain than that. One of those men made it easier for you to turn away from what you knew. The other made it harder. That difference is real, you felt it, and nothing in any record of the encounter would show it.

**3. There is only one wrong move, and everything we call wrongdoing is a version of it.**

That's the real claim, and cheating and contempt are the first two cases of it. *This doesn't apply to me* and *you're not really one of us* are the same assertion, pointed in opposite directions, and one faculty catches both.

*The ordinary explanation:* two different social instincts that happen to overlap.

*The trouble:* both arrive instantly, without words, without argument, and both are accurate. That's one instrument, not two. Whether it holds for every wrong is what the rest of the book has to show.

And there's one more turn here, which took me a long time to see.

Contempt isn't just a second example alongside cheating. It's a self-exception in its own right. Nobody accepts being treated with contempt. Nobody has ever thought the rule stopped applying the moment it was pointed at them. So when you aim it at somebody, you're claiming the thing holds for everyone except you, right now, in this particular case, for reasons.

Which is the cheating move exactly.

Now put the two halves together. To cheat, you needed to believe you'd get away with it. Contempt is the version where you always do. There's no transcript, no record, nothing anyone can hold up afterward. So it's the one form of this you can perform in front of witnesses, at full strength, and be admired for.

And there's a third thing, which you can check right now.

Bring up a scene of somebody confronting something evil, fiercely, and completely cleanly. No contempt in it, just refusal. It feels good to watch. Something in you stands up.

Now bring up a scene of somebody doing it with contempt. A takedown. A destruction. Somebody getting exactly what they had coming, delivered with relish. That feels good too.

But go back and compare the two feelings, because they are not the same feeling. The second one has a charge in it. There's a *fuck yes* in there, and it's hot, and if you're honest it's a little violent, and it wants more. It isn't the feeling of watching something be protected. It's the feeling of watching something be crushed, and being allowed to enjoy it.

Now notice you've had that feeling thousands of times without once thinking of yourself as a person who enjoys watching people get crushed. That's the same limbo, in the audience seat.

So here's why this particular wrong is the one that got out of hand, and it isn't a coincidence. Take any other kind of wrongdoing. Theft leaves evidence. Violence is visible. Lying can be checked against the truth. Every one of them can, in principle, be caught and named. Contempt has none of that. It leaves no record, so it can't be proven. It arrives dressed as duty, so it needn't be defended. It's performed in public and rewarded there. And the watching is *pleasurable*, which means it spreads through people who never did anything at all.

There is no other wrong with that set of properties. That's why it deserves a book rather than a paragraph.

(This book was going to be called *Contempt Is Evil*. I dropped it, because a title like that reads as a judgment being passed, and this isn't a judgment. It's meant the way you'd say a shape is a triangle. But I'd like to keep the sentence, and I'd like it to mean the flat thing.)

**4. Something was operating in him that could not have survived his looking straight at it.**

He half-knew, the way you half-knew: that's limbo, and you've already found it in yourself. But notice this operation needs the not-looking in a way your cheating didn't. If he had let himself see that he was provoking you in order to use your reaction as proof, the proof would have been worthless to him. Nobody convicts on evidence he knows he planted. So the operation is only available from behind the half-turned head. Its hiding isn't a side effect of it. It's a condition of it.

Then look at what still got done from back there. It aimed. It tracked what you did and adjusted. It closed off every road you might have taken. When you noticed it, it got stronger rather than embarrassed. And it benefited from being mistaken for the man carrying it.

*The ordinary explanation:* bias.

*The trouble:* bias does none of those things. Whatever has that profile is behaving like something with interests of its own. I'll be calling it an agent, and later something less flattering.

**5. Whatever it is, it needs to be mistaken for him. By you, and by him.**

Look at what your contempt for him would have accomplished. You'd have gone after the man, because the man is what you could see. Which presses down the one thing in him that could have ended this. Your accuracy about the thing, aimed at the person carrying it, is what buys it another day.

And the same requirement runs on his side, which is harder to look at. He isn't protecting a parasite. He experiences it as his own convictions, his own judgment, his own moral seriousness. Ask him to give it up and you're asking him to give up himself.

Now put those together with something you can check directly.

From the outside, the thing looks monstrous. You saw it in him in two seconds and it was ugly.

From the inside, it feels *righteous*. Not tolerable. Not a lapse you're excusing. Good. There's a heat in it, and a rightness, and you've felt it, and it did not feel like a monster. It felt like caring more than other people do.

And notice: nobody ever gets both views of the same instance. You get the inside of yours and the outside of everyone else's. So does everyone. Which means we're all accurate about each other and blind about ourselves, permanently, as a matter of arrangement rather than character.

That's why we can't simply tell each other. Hand somebody the outside view of themselves and it arrives on the inside, where it lands as an attack on the best thing in them. Which is precisely what happened to you.

**6. It doesn't only change what he does. It changes what he sees.**

The person you once despised looked rotten to you. Not *seemed*. Looked.

*The ordinary explanation:* you were interpreting them uncharitably, the way anyone does when they're angry.

*The trouble:* an interpretation is something you can catch yourself making. This never presents itself as one. It arrives already finished, as the plain state of the world. Which is why nobody has ever talked themselves out of it, and why we'll have to find another way.

**7. The caring is already there, and it's already enough to stop you.**

That's why not even the smallest unkindness would go through with the person fully in view, and why you needed limbo before you could do the thing you did.

*The ordinary explanation:* social inhibition. You'd have felt watched.

*The trouble:* it works the same with nobody around. And nobody builds machinery that elaborate to get around a part of himself that doesn't care.

**8. He didn't merely prefer that you not improve. He needed it.**

Your getting better was the worst available outcome. It would have put a person where he required a different kind of creature, and taken away the only thing that made his own behavior alright.

*The ordinary explanation:* frustration.

*The trouble:* frustration wants the problem solved. This wanted the problem kept.

**9. Everyone has this, and everyone reads it in everyone.**

Instantly, untrained, across languages, and accurately.

*The ordinary explanation:* shared culture; you'd both picked up the same cues.

*The trouble:* nobody taught you, and you weren't wrong.

**10. Contempt for him would feed the thing in him. And it needs you to know that.**

Including, and especially, when you have every reason for it.

Here's the part that took me longest. When you aim contempt at somebody, they can read your reasoning. Not your words: the fact that you're running on reasons at all. And running on reasons is what a person does instead of listening to the thing that just knows. So what lands on them isn't only the insult. It's a demonstration, by someone who believes himself good, that the inner knowing can be overridden when you have a good enough excuse.

That's the lesson. That's what makes them worse.

And here's the strange part. Your contempt doesn't merely happen to be legible to them. It has to be. Concealing it perfectly would defeat the entire purpose, because their reading it is the mechanism. The thing in you needs the thing in them to see it.

This is the one I can't argue you into. I can only hand you places to check it. Look at what your contempt has actually done to the people you've aimed it at, across years. Ask whether any of them got better.

Now the thing all of that is circling, which I should say straight out.

Those ten claims aren't ten separate ideas. They're one idea from different sides, and I'd rather you knew that than assembled it yourself three chapters late. Here it is with the sides named.

**There is one move.** Turning away from what you know, and from the person in front of you. It's a single act, not two: you can't do the second without the first.

**Everything we call wrongdoing is made of it.** Not accompanied by it. Made of it. Take the turning away out and there's nothing left over that was the wrong part.

**Contempt is that move in its purest form.** Which is why this book is named for it.

**So the only thing that helps anybody is turning toward.** Yours, first. Nothing done from turned-away can turn anyone else toward, however it looks from outside and however sure you are while doing it.

The first and third of those, you've largely seen. The second and fourth I've just asserted, and the fourth is the one this whole book exists to earn.

But hold onto what it does to the vocabulary. If that's right, then turning toward and turning away aren't just *related* to right and wrong, help and harm, good and evil. They're what those words have been pointing at the whole time.

Test it on the hardest one. When we call something evil, we don't mean very bad. We mean the thing we'd most want gone from the world, whatever it cost to remove it. So bring up the worst thing you know of; something done to people who couldn't stop it. Now try to build it in your head without the part where whoever did it had stopped counting their victims as the same kind of creature as himself. You can't. Take that away and there is no version of the thing left standing. Every atrocity anyone has ever committed runs on that one move, and it cannot run on anything else.

So contempt isn't a bad way of responding to evil. It's the thing itself, in the dose we've all agreed not to notice.

I'm stating that rather than arguing it, because it needs the whole book behind it. But carry it while you read. A great deal depends on it, including whether "sometimes you have to be a little evil to fight evil" is a hard truth about the world or a description of feeding the thing.
