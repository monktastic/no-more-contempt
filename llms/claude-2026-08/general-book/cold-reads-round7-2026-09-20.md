# General book, Chapter 1: cold reads, round 7 (September 20, 2026)

*Two new readers. Stage 1: the general book's Chapter 1 as rebuilt on September 20 (planted evidence, limbo, two channels, verdict against aim, the covering), plus the opening paragraphs of Chapter 2 (the agent). Stage 2: the short book's Chapter 1, described only as a different opening for a different audience. Letters verbatim.*

## Marcus: 53, high-school principal, not a Buddhist; AME church, centering prayer, Thurman and Baldwin; seven years in a men's group

### Stage 1

Dear Renee,

I read your friend's chapter Saturday morning after prayer, with coffee, a pencil, and nobody in the house. I did what it asked, with real people. I'll tell you who as I go, because otherwise this is just a book report. Pass along whatever of this you think he can use. I'm not going to be gentle, but I'm not going to be unfair either; he didn't hand me something cheap.

**The first page.** It put my guard up, and not because of the claim. It was the pitch. "Something highly counterintuitive," "implications far beyond the political sphere, touching nearly everything we do." That's the voice of every consultant the district has ever sent me. And then the very next thing: "Immediately you sense something hypocritical about my behavior, right?" No. When a man mocks me, what I sense first is heat, and insult, and my grandmother's voice telling me not to give him the satisfaction. "Hypocritical" was his word, handed to me with a "right?" on the end so I'd take it. Then "Can you feel that?" twice in three paragraphs. I felt managed before I'd felt anything else. And "othering" is a word I have, Lord knows, from a hundred professional development slides, which is exactly why it costs him something with me. It's a workshop word. It's the one place in the chapter where he sounds like the district office.

Also: he opens on politics and never goes back there. My enemies aren't political. They're an assistant superintendent and a handful of parents. I had to translate, and the first paragraph made me think this was going to be a book about Twitter.

The paragraph that starts "Here's what I'll claim" I read twice and half got. "You don't have to grant any of this yet" was the first sentence that made me lean in, because it was the first one that left me alone.

**Where I was with him.** The narration. All of it. I put a father in that chair, a man who cursed out a first-year teacher in my front office until she cried, and I had him in my office afterward with the door closed. "He's **bad**, dammit." "I can't be nice to evil. That's how it wins." I have said that second one nearly word for word, to my wife, about that man. And then the eyes: "Don't pretend you're doing something noble here. We both know what you're actually doing." Renee, that man gave me that look. So has more than one sixteen-year-old. And the "*Shit.*" that follows is exactly right; it's the truest word in the chapter. This section reads like a man telling on himself, and I'll follow a man who does that almost anywhere. "Ha! Classic." made me laugh out loud at myself.

The contrast section worked too. My old department chair, Mrs. Whitfield, once told me in front of God and everybody that I'd graded a set of papers like I was angry at the children for being fifteen. She was not gentle. I did not dig in. "What makes us dig in isn't the harshness. It's the verdict." I'm keeping that; it's going on an index card for my assistant principals. One complaint: he asks "What was different about it?" and answers it himself two lines later. I had my answer ready. Let me have it for a beat.

**Limbo** is the best-made part. When my marriage was coming apart I was punishing Denise with silence for the better part of a year, and it took the men's group two more years to get me to say that I knew I was doing it while I did it. "Did it feel like new information? Or like something you had always known, but somehow hadn't *let* yourself know?" That's the question. That's the one the group asked me in worse words. And "limbo" was fine for a church kid; I know it as a place, and it works as a place.

**Where I started skimming.** I'll name it: the section that begins "Now it's possible to say what's happening," and specifically from "The revealing must look intentional" through the long italic message. "He sees that I intend it, and I see that he sees," and then a footnote about game theorists. "Two channels of communication... Both must appear intentional, and remain isolated from each other." This is where cleverness outran experience for me. He'd been telling me what happened in him, and suddenly he was drawing me a wiring diagram and using "must." Why must it? I don't know that it must. Then "*That's* the hypocrisy you sensed at the start of this chapter." It is not. What I sensed at the start was much simpler than that, and I didn't call it hypocrisy; he did. "What I'm showing him *is* that I'm hiding it" I read three times. I think it may be true. I can't tell whether I understood it or admired it.

I'll rescue one sentence from that stretch, because it's one of the best in the chapter: "he knows he can't appeal to my better nature, because I think this *is* my better nature." I've been on the receiving end of that from downtown and I've never had words for why it's maddening rather than merely frightening. Now I do.

The earlier slip, smaller: "Deep down, I know there's darkness in me... So I tell myself I'll do the next best thing: attack it in others." Nobody tells himself that. That's the author's theory wearing the narrator's clothes. And "This makes *fighting evil* the ideal way for my darkness to get into my behavior" is where the darkness first starts wanting things, which I noticed and flagged. (He flags it himself in Chapter 2, to his credit.)

**The guess about him.** I appreciated that he called it a guess. And the line in the box, "why the fuck shouldn't I? I've earned the right," I know. I knew it as a young Black teacher under a certain kind of administrator, and I have watched it arrive on children's faces for twenty-eight years. "In that world, looking out for yourself isn't a lapse. It's realism." That is close to Baldwin and he earned it. But the paragraph after it says "Now recall how I said that the hidden purpose of my contempt is to provoke more darkness in my enemies." I went back. He didn't say that, not in those words; he said something about an ideal way in. It's a reference to a sentence that isn't quite there. And "My behavior already assumes that his wrongdoing is sustained by something like my own limbo. Otherwise there would be nothing for my contempt to deepen" lost me outright.

**"My verdict says you're irredeemable. My aim depends on your not being so."** Half true of me, half clever. Here's the true half. When I gave that father the look, I was aiming it at the part of him that could be ashamed. You can't shame a stone. So yes: the look is addressed to the very conscience it says isn't there. I grant that fully, and it stung. The other half, that my *aim* is to make him more like my verdict, I couldn't verify from the paragraph, which leans on "for my behavior to be coherent, I must know." My contempt isn't coherent; I thought that was his point. What did verify it was something he didn't ask me: that father came to the spring concert, shook the teacher's hand, and apologized, and I was *disappointed*. I felt robbed. If he'd asked "Have you ever been let down when someone you'd written off turned out decent?" he'd have had me cold, and he could drop half the argument. The two closing sentences are better than the paragraph that carries them there.

**"The claim people object to first: surely there's a difference in kind between a look and real evil."** What claim? He hadn't made it yet. That sentence answers a conversation I wasn't in. It's the clearest seam in the chapter.

But the three checks that follow are where the chapter got me. I tried to picture humiliating a teacher in a staff meeting with Mrs. Whitfield's eyes. Can't be done. Something has to go out first. (The footnote, though, handled me: "If the picture held for you, it may be because the harm you pictured was small." Every door I might leave by has a man standing in it. The hood and the number are real; the first sentence of the footnote is the problem.) The second check is a leading question, "Doesn't the person... feel somehow absent." The third is the one. "Did you have to keep not caring: the case kept open, the reasons rehearsed, his face kept out of view?" My father. Thirty years of upkeep. "If the caring had gone, there'd have been nothing to maintain." Seven years in that group and nobody said it that cleanly. That's what I'm carrying out of the room, that and the verdict line.

**Covering, not what he is, and still responsible.** I granted it, but you should tell him I'm an easy mark; Thurman had me there decades ago. Where it's earned: the upkeep check, and "If harming were as natural to me as helping, I could do it with my eyes open. I can't." Where it's asserted: the step from "it's covered" to "what I turned away *from* has the better claim to being what I am." Why the better claim? Because it was there first? Because it outlasts? A harder man than me says: both are you, friend. "Better claim" is modest, and I respect the modesty, but it's a step on air. The responsibility half is fully earned. "Those are two different questions. Am I responsible for what happens while I'm turned away? Yes. Does the turning away show what I most deeply am? No." That's the clearest paragraph in the chapter, and the reason he gives for using the word evil, "so that I never again get to believe that my mistake is a different kind of thing from what my enemies do," is a man binding himself, not me. I trust that.

One seam there, though. He settles "is it me" at the end of the three checks, and then the next section opens "So is the darkness me, or isn't it?" as if he hadn't just said. He even writes "it's the one I just gave." It reads like two drafts of the same answer laid end to end. Same with "for my behavior to be coherent," which comes round twice, and "off the hook," twice, and "subtle but primal," twice.

**One voice or stitched?** Two voices. One is the man in the room, profane, funny, telling on himself. The other is an explainer: "Now it's possible to say what's happening," "two channels," "It is the inherently paradoxical nature of the state that enables us to do this." That last one I would circle in red on a junior's essay. The first voice I'd follow. The second one I put up with. "Recall that," "Now recall how I said" is teacher talk, and I know teacher talk.

**Told rather than asked:** "right?" on the first page; "It's the same thing you felt above"; "*That's* the hypocrisy you sensed"; "they are reminding you of something: that *you're better than this*" before I'd answered; the footnote; and the turn from "I" to "we" in the loved-ones paragraph: "we make sure they see it." It happens to be true of me. The sigh. "The look on their face that tells us it landed, which we'll pretend not to have seen." I did that to Denise for years and that sentence went in like a splinter. But it would have gone in deeper as "I."

**The late sections.** The "one more exit" paragraph is strong: "It's searching for somewhere to keep the verdict." The cold form is *my* form, the principal's politeness with nothing behind it, "a small satisfaction at being done with them," and I wished it had come sooner, because the hot scene I recognized from memory and the cold one I recognized from Thursday. "*I* could never do that... a dozen times before lunch": yes. Guilty. "It's **all a single move**" and "benefit ourselves at the world's expense" are big claims riding on small ones; I let them pass rather than granted them. And "as long as I haven't healed it in myself": healed how? First I've heard the word. I took your note that the way through comes later. He did not leave me somewhere dark, for what it's worth. "What I need in order to look is what he needed from me: to be seen as more than what I did." That's the gospel of the thing, in a plain coat.

The ending question is good. For me it isn't a mystery, I'd say "that of God in him" and be done, but I want to hear what he says.

**Chapter 2.** I'd read on, with one eyebrow up. I liked that he caught himself talking about his darkness as a thing that does things; I'd caught him too. "A reflex doesn't improvise" is right. I tried his little test with a call from my brother I haven't returned, and sure enough: "it's a school night." A reason arrived on its own. But "'I' literally cannot be the one showing you something that is hidden from me" is a "literally cannot" I don't grant yet. And "something else takes control": I grew up hearing "the devil made me do it" used as an alibi by grown men. He says he isn't absolving himself, and I believe him, but he is walking on Romans 7, "it is no more I that do it, but sin that dwelleth in me," and either doesn't know or isn't saying. He should know what that ground has been used for.

**Words I didn't have or didn't believe:** "common knowledge" as a term of art, "channels," "othering" (had it, don't want it). Nothing about darkness or light cost me a thing. What I don't believe, yet, is that my contempt has a *purpose*. That it has an effect, and that some part of me likes the effect, yes.

What's working: the man telling on himself, Limbo, the three checks, the distinction between nice and kind, the cold form, the sigh. What isn't: the first page, the wiring diagram in the middle, and the habit of telling me what I just felt.

Tell him thank you. I called my brother.

### Stage 2

Dear Renee,

I read the second one Sunday. Short version: the second has the better front door and the better manners. The first is the better chapter, and it's the one that did something to me. If he could put the first one's body behind the second one's first page, he'd have it.

**The opening.** No contest. "Imagine looking into a stranger's eyes long enough that their face stops being something you're looking at and becomes a place somebody is looking out of." That's a sentence Thurman could have written, and it asks me to do something instead of selling me something. No "highly counterintuitive," no "touching nearly everything we do." And it does a job the first opening never does: it gives me the thing *before* it's covered. In the first chapter, when he gets to "What I turned away *from* has the better claim to being what I am," I had to supply the "what" out of my own prayer life. Here it's on the table in four lines: "This one is like me. I want them to be all right." So when the turning away comes, I know what's being turned from, because I was just standing there.

Then "they've just done something atrocious." I didn't use the father from my office this time. I used the young man who shot a former student of mine in 2019. I looked, and what I found was what he says he finds: "now there is only something rotten." And then: "Is there something in you that wants this verdict to *land*; to register deep inside? There is in me." That's how to do it. He asks, he stops, and he says the finding is his. Compare "Immediately you sense something hypocritical about my behavior, right?" in the other one. One is a man beside me; the other has a hand on my back. Even "othering" goes down easier here, because he names it after I've felt it rather than before. (It is not "the everyday word," though. Not on my block.)

**What the second does better, besides that:**

It leaves me a door. "If not, I have no argument to offer here. Read on, and see whether anything else fits." The first chapter has nothing like that, and has a footnote that does the opposite.

This, which I'd call the most important thing the first one lacks: "This part of me—it needs him to be bad. I don't mean that as a standing fact about me, or about you. It's true while I'm in it, and close to invisible when I'm not, which is why looking back rarely finds it. If you can find even one memory where it's plainly true, that's the one to look at." In the first chapter I balked at the idea that my contempt has a purpose. This paragraph is what would have let me grant it: not always, not as my character, just find one. I found one, the spring concert.

It grants me my accuracy. "That's genuine darkness, and I really did assess it accurately. And that comes from a good place... But the need to identify it with him? That doesn't come from a good place." That father really was wrong. A reader like me needs to hear that the seeing was sound before he'll look at what he did with it. The first chapter says "Fighting evil is actually a good thing," which is thinner.

The conscience passage after Limbo: "Why did one side have to argue, and the other not? And which side did you listen to—the part that 'just knew' or the part that could prove?" I stopped on that for a while. The part of me that knew I was freezing Denise out never made a single argument. The other part was a trial lawyer. The first chapter should have this.

The self-contempt passage comes at the right moment, right after I've dug up the memory, when I actually need it, and it has two sentences the first one dropped: "It feels like accountability. It isn't." The first year of my men's group was grown men flogging themselves and calling it ownership. In the first chapter this material shows up near the end, after the damage.

And for a man in my line of work, this: "My behavior might make him think twice about doing it again—at least, if there's any chance of him getting caught. But does he feel remorseful? Is his private behavior likely to get better?" That is the whole argument about school discipline in three sentences. With it, "everything I can accomplish *with* the darkness can be accomplished better without it," said as his own finding. I want that in whichever version lives.

Small one: the threat gets an extra clause here, "What I do to you no longer qualifies as harm." Colder and better.

**What the first does better:** nearly everything that went into me. The second has no Mrs. Whitfield. There's no corrected-with-dignity section, no "What makes us dig in isn't the harshness. It's the verdict," and that's the line my people need, because every disciplinarian I know believes the only alternative to contempt is going soft. It has no upkeep check: "the case kept open, the reasons rehearsed, his face kept out of view." No sigh, no "which we'll pretend not to have seen." No "why the fuck shouldn't I? I've earned the right" and no "It's realism." No "one more exit." No "a dozen times before lunch." No "My verdict says you're irredeemable. My aim depends on your not being so." And nothing on responsibility: the "two different questions" paragraph, the clearest thing he's written, isn't there. The second chapter tells me there's darkness in me and stops short of telling me what I am under it, so it left me lower than the first did, for all its better manners.

In the scene itself, the first is sharper in the small places. "*Shit.*" beats "Truth be told." "Just to prove he's beyond help" beats "just to prove I'm right about them." And the first marks "Now a guess about him" as a guess, where the second simply announces "it's the same trick he used."

The first also ends better. "So what was it that both of us already knew?" pulls. "I might even have an inkling of what that way is" is coy, and I don't like being teased by a man who's just asked me to look at the worst of myself.

**Where they share a fault.** Both have the wiring diagram. In the second it's "*we know they know we know*," "limbo contains knowledge of itself, in limbo," "what I was hiding from myself, underneath everything else, was nothing but *the fact that I was hiding*." I skimmed all three, same as I skimmed the game theory footnote. But the second at least opens a window: "You don't have to trace the logic. You might have felt it before, as a kind of hall of mirrors." I had. That sentence would have kept me in the first chapter's middle. The second also runs hot in its last pages: "I want to be crystal clear here," "in my absolutely deepest moments of introspection," **devastating** in bold, "the darkness leaks out of my skull." When a man raises his voice I start wondering what he's unsure of. And "All harm begins by turning away" is enormous, though he does say "I can't prove this to you," which I respect.

One structural thing: the second spends the improvising, "I call it an invitation. He sees through that, so I call it being fierce. He still won't let it go, so I call it projection," inside Chapter 1, where I've just watched it happen. The first saves it for Chapter 2. It belongs where the second has it.

**If I were handing one to a colleague tomorrow:** the first. I'm thinking of my assistant principal, who is good and fierce and has a filing cabinet in her head for certain parents. She needs the Whitfield section, the upkeep check, and the cold form. But I'd be nervous about the first page, because she'd smell a consultant and be gone by "othering."

So what I'd change first: take out everything in the first chapter before "I'll narrate what happens in my mind," and put in the second's opening, from the stranger's eyes through "Usually the latter." Second: cut the stretch from "The revealing must look intentional" through the italic message down to a third, and give it the hall-of-mirrors sentence as a release. Third: bring over "I don't mean that as a standing fact about me, or about you," and "Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" Then leave it alone. The man telling on himself doesn't need help.

Marcus


## Dana: 41, public defender, no spiritual leanings; Sapolsky, Kahneman, Stevenson; four years of therapy

### Stage 1

Sam,

I read your friend's chapter on Sunday with a pen, did what it asked, and I'm writing before I open the second file, as instructed. You said he wants what actually happened, so here it is, in order, and then the questions.

**The faces I used.** He says put a real face on the enemy. I used three. A prosecutor I'll call K., who smiles at the floor while I argue mitigation. A colleague down the hall who pleads out everything and whom I have been courteous to for six years. And my partner, because by the end the chapter sent me there whether I wanted to go or not.

**The first page.** The opening paragraph put my guard up. "Highly counterintuitive," "implications far beyond the political sphere, touching nearly everything we do." That's a book-jacket voice, and it promises size before it has shown me anything. Then: "Immediately you sense something hypocritical about my behavior, right?" No. When K. gives me that look, what I sense is insult and a low-grade threat. "Hypocritical" is his word, and he hands it to me with a "right?" on the end. He then asks "Can you feel that?" three times in six lines. By the third I felt like a witness being led.

The second claim, "Here's what I'll claim, and this chapter is my case," did the opposite. I'm a lawyer. I like an opening statement, and "You don't have to grant any of this yet" is the sentence that let me keep reading. The first half of that claim I got at once: to deny my humanity in a way I can feel, you have to be counting on it. The second half I had to read twice. So: the first paragraph raised my guard, the thesis paragraph lowered it. If he cut the first paragraph and started at "Say you and I are political enemies," I'd lose nothing. Also, the politics never comes back. The chapter opens on political fights and is then entirely about a man who did something cruel. I noticed the bait had been switched.

**The narration.** This is the best writing in the chapter and I was with him nearly the whole way. It sounds like a person. The line that stopped me was "he can't prove I'm doing this, can he? No. And crucially, neither can I." I work from transcripts. Tone isn't in the record. I have known for fifteen years that a judge can bury my client with a face and the appellate court will never see it, and I have used the same deniability myself on cross. "Neither can I" is the part I hadn't said to myself. I'll carry that one out.

One problem in here for someone like me. "I can't be nice to evil. That's how it wins" is put in the mouth of the man deceiving himself. I hold a version of that sentence sincerely, about fighting hard. For about twenty lines I thought he was building toward telling me my fierceness was the disease. That's the thing that makes me close a book.

**The contrast section** is where he said what I needed: "Not *gently*. Maybe even fiercely," and then "Fighting evil is actually a good thing." My case was a senior attorney who took me apart in a stairwell after I humiliated a rookie cop on cross and watched the jury turn on me. She was furious and she never once made me smaller than the mistake. So yes, it's there, and it's about a quarter of the way in, which is just early enough. I'd want one clause of it before the narration starts, so I'm not braced through the best part. One complaint: he asks "What was different about it?" and answers it himself in the next line. He gave me no room to find it. His answer happened to match mine. That isn't the point.

Does it ever sound like it's asking me to stop fighting? No, not once, and I was listening for it. What it does say is sharper and less comfortable: "I'm only holding you to account" is listed as the cover story, the clean channel. That's K.'s line about my clients and my line about cops. He isn't saying accountability is the problem. He's saying it's the alibi. I can take that. What I still need, and it may be coming, is what fierce-without-the-verdict looks like when your role is adversarial and the person across from you is lying under oath. "Stops the harm without cutting off their way back" arrives very late, and in my world "the way back" is not a metaphor. It's parole eligibility.

**Where I stopped following.** Two places.

First, the paragraph beginning "Deep down, I know there's darkness in me—the same thing I see in my enemies." Up to there "dark" was an adjective for something underhanded, and I was fine with it. In that sentence it becomes a noun, a substance that "leaks out," and I never agreed to it. From there on I was translating. My translation was "my willingness to write a person off," and with that most paragraphs survive. So it's mostly the word, not the claim. Where translation fails is the purpose language: "the hidden purpose of my contempt is to provoke more darkness in my enemies." He says "That's not a coincidence," and I agree, but a loop can be non-accidental without anyone aiming it. Confirmation bias plus reactance gets you the same loop. I went and checked whether I aim it. Here's what I found, and he can have it: when K. makes a decent offer, I feel a flicker of disappointment. I wanted her to be what I'd decided she was. That's his claim, and it's true of me, and that example would have persuaded me faster than the word "purpose" did.

Second, I skimmed from "The revealing must look intentional" through the block quote. "He sees that I intend it, and I see that he sees" and the game theory footnote read like a different, more pleased-with-itself writer. "Must" is doing work there that nothing earned. "What I'm showing him *is* that I'm hiding it" is a good sentence stranded in a bad stretch.

**The two messages, the verdict and the aim.** Split decision. "My verdict says you're irredeemable" is flatly true of me and K. The first-page version of the point landed as true: nobody sneers at weather. You sneer at someone who can be hurt by it. But the long paragraph before the punchline slides from "you can feel this" to "goodness exists in you." Those aren't the same thing. People with very little goodness on display can be needled. So the first half landed as true, the closing pair of sentences as well made, and the step between them as clever. The disappointment I just described is what would close the gap for me.

**Darkness, evil, the covering, responsibility.** Less trouble than I expected, and in a different place than you'd guess.

"Evil": he earned it, with one sentence. "I use the word so that I never again get to believe that my mistake is a different kind of thing from what my enemies do." In my courtrooms "evil" is the word that marks a difference in kind, right before the ask for the maximum. He uses it to erase the difference in kind. That's the opposite use and I'll allow it. The footnote, "the hood, the number instead of the name, the word for vermin," did more for the same-road claim than the argument did.

The kindness test: I ran it and it held for me. I noted that he said "For me it comes apart every time" and "In me it's always the second." Those are his findings, labeled as his. That is the right way to talk to me and he should do it everywhere.

Where I stopped: "What I turned away *from* has the better claim to being what I am." Why better? Being there first is a claim about order, not identity. The part of me that writes off my colleague is also me; Sapolsky would say so and so would my therapist. The version I accept is weaker: the contempt isn't all of me, and it only runs with something switched off. And then I noticed, with some discomfort, that I say the strong version at every sentencing, about every client, and mean it. I just don't extend it to K. or to myself. So the claim isn't the problem. He's saying Bryan Stevenson's sentence from the inside and then asking me to apply it to the prosecutor. That's fair. "So it wasn't reduced. It was covered" goes by too fast, though, and the third check assumes I cared about the man to begin with. What I maintain might be my self-image, not buried caring.

"Fully responsible": you'd expect me to worry he's letting himself off. My worry runs the other way. My whole job is that "fully" is rarely true of anyone. He answers "Am I responsible? Yes" in one word. The paragraph that did land is "we never get both views of one instance": mine is always the kind I couldn't see, my enemy's always the kind he knowingly chose. That is every charging decision I've ever read, and every thought I've had about the people who write them.

**What landed hardest,** more than any of the above: the receiving-end paragraph. "If this is the kind of world I live in... why the fuck shouldn't I?" and then "where even the people who call themselves good will drop you the moment they can call it justified. In that world, looking out for yourself isn't a lapse. It's realism." I have heard that, in those words, from nineteen-year-olds who've been getting the look since they were nine. It's also what I felt the year I burned out. And then the sigh at home, "dressed up to make us seem like the reasonable ones," and "the look on their face that tells us it landed, which we'll pretend not to have seen." I do the reasonable voice. I've seen it land. I put the pen down there for a while. And "well, *I* could never do that... a dozen times before lunch." I think that about prosecutors before I've had coffee. The cold version, "a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them," is my colleague down the hall, exactly.

**One voice or stitched?** Stitched, in places I can name.
- "That brings me to the claim people object to first: surely there's a difference in kind between a look and real evil." He hasn't made that claim yet. He makes it at the end of that section. The objection arrives before the thing objected to.
- "Now recall how I said that the hidden purpose of my contempt is to provoke more darkness." He didn't say that, not in those words. I went back and looked.
- "Okay, back to the story." There's no more story. It's analysis from there on.
- "Limbo" is the only headed section, and the voice changes to "We like to call such knowledge..." It reads as brought in from elsewhere. Then "That's how I did it" sits alone between two rules like a patch.
- "So is the darkness me, or isn't it?" re-asks what the previous section just answered, and says so: "it's the one I just gave."
- The last long section keeps raising the stakes: "all a single move" in bold, "benefit ourselves at the world's expense," "darken the world a little for my own benefit." And "healed" shows up from nowhere. The loved-ones paragraph inside it is the best thing in the back half. The rest of that section I'd call a second chapter's claims passing through.
- Small: "the way the person who corrected you looked at you... his anger." Mine was a woman.
- The bold and italics. In the narration they're his character. Outside it they read as shouting.

**Where I was told rather than asked.** "You sense something hypocritical, right?" "It's the same thing you felt above." "*That's* the hypocrisy you sensed at the start of this chapter." (I didn't.) In Limbo: "you found a way to deflect. Did that happen passively?" and the italic "can you see how some part of you knew exactly what you were doing." As it happens I did deflect. My case was a plea I let a client take without having visited him, and I snapped at my supervisor when she asked. But he stated it, then asked a question with the answer built in. "They feel it just the same." "We both feel it." "Doesn't the person looking at you feel somehow absent?" And in Chapter 2: "You have your own version, from the Limbo memory." He's telling me what my memory contains.

**Chapter 2.** The exercise worked: "Try it now with something small you'd rather not look at, and watch a reason arrive by itself." I picked a call I owe my mother, and a reason showed up on schedule. Good. But "A reflex doesn't improvise" is a false choice. Between reflex and agent sits all of motivated reasoning. I'd reach for Kahneman's System 1, for the split-brain patients whose left hemisphere invents fluent reasons for acts it never chose, for Trivers's idea that we deceive ourselves to deceive others better. None of that needs a someone. Where he lost me is "it pursued a goal I would never choose with my eyes open, a darker world." The goal I can observe is protecting my picture of myself. The darker world is the exhaust, not the destination. And "as though *something else* takes control" is, I'm sorry, what my clients say. "That wasn't me." I've heard it from people it was true of and people it wasn't. "'I' literally cannot be the one" is an argument from a definition. Will I read on? Yes, to see whether "behaves like an agent" stays a way of speaking. If the next page gives it a name and a will, I'm out.

**So: what's working and what isn't.** Working: the narration, the receiving-end paragraph, the loved ones, the cold form, "neither can I," every place he says "in me" and stops. Not working: the first paragraph, the leading questions, the noun "darkness" arriving without an introduction, the intentionality stretch, the ending that keeps enlarging. It's a true thing, told by someone who has plainly done it himself, which is why I kept going. He doesn't need to push. When he pushes, I leave. When he tells on himself, I tell on myself.

Dana


### Stage 2

Sam,

Second letter. I've now read the other opening, the one he wrote for the meditation people. Short answer: the first one ("The Look") works better on me, and the second one ("Turning Away") treats me better. I'd hand a colleague the first, after taking about six things from the second.

**What happened with the second one.** It opens by asking me to look into a stranger's eyes until the face "becomes a place somebody is looking out of." I expected to bounce off that, and I half did. "With it comes a sense of their innocence" is a hard word to hand a defense lawyer; innocence is a term of art where I work and I almost never get to use it. But I did the exercise, because I do a version of it for a living. I sit across glass from men who have done atrocious, deliberate things to someone innocent, and finding the person looking out is the job. So when he ran it again with the atrocity and said "It's harder, isn't it?", my true answer was: not for that man. I've trained for that man. It's harder for K. It's harder for the cop who lied about the consent search. That was a real finding and the first opening didn't give it to me: my turning-toward is issued by role. I can do it for the defendant and I refuse it to the prosecutor. He did leave me the door: "If it isn't, make the case real: someone from your own life." That's the difference between the two chapters in one line. The first says "right?" The second says "if it isn't."

**What the second does better.**

It defines its words before using them. "The need to make the verdict land is _contempt_: turning-away made into a message, with the sense that sending it is righteous." That's the best single definition in either file, and the first opening never defines contempt at all. It also tells me what "darkness" means before it leans on it: he "had to 'turn away' from... the people he was harming. That's genuine darkness," and later, "a person turned away from their own better nature. It's what we mean by darkness." My main complaint in the first letter was that the noun showed up unintroduced. Here it's introduced. I still don't love the word, but I know what I'm translating.

It lets me leave. After the hot and cold forms: "If not, I have no argument to offer here. Read on, and see whether anything else fits." The first opening has the same hot/cold paragraph, word for word, without that sentence. It should have it.

It answers the objection I raised about "purpose." I said a loop doesn't need an aim, and that I only believed him once I caught my own disappointment when K. behaves decently. The second opening says: "This part of me—it needs him to be bad. I don't mean that as a standing fact about me, or about you. It's true while I'm in it, and close to invisible when I'm not, which is why looking back rarely finds it. If you can find even one memory where it's plainly true, that's the one to look at." That is what I needed to be told, and how. One memory, not a theory of me.

It gives me the split that matters most to someone in my job: "That's genuine darkness, and I really did assess it accurately. And that comes from a good place: my desire to see the world free of it. But the need to identify it with him? That doesn't come from a good place, even though it says it does." The first opening says "Fighting evil is actually a good thing," which is reassurance. This is a distinction. My read on the lying cop can be accurate, and the verdict on him is a separate act. I can work with that on a Monday.

It concedes that contempt works. "Not that it can't stop people. My behavior might make him think twice about doing it again—at least, if there's any chance of him getting caught. But does he feel remorseful? Is his private behavior likely to get better?" That's deterrence against rehabilitation, which is my whole field, stated fairly. And then the sentence a fighter needs: "I can be fierce and uncompromising with my enemies *without* provoking that 'digging in' response." The first opening never says it that flatly. But in the second it comes five paragraphs from the end. Too late for my people.

On self-contempt it has a line the first lacks: "It feels like accountability. It isn't." And it puts that passage right after Limbo, before the hard material, as something to hold onto. In the first it's near the end, after I'd already spent ten pages being hard on myself.

And the conscience passage: "Why did one side have to argue, and the other not? And which side did you listen to—the part that 'just knew' or the part that could prove?" I argue for a living, so that stung in a useful way. I also pushed back: jurors "just know" my client did it. The part that doesn't argue is not always the part that's right. He should expect that from anyone who's read Kahneman.

Also true, from fifteen years of client interviews: "Even the worst person experiences theirs as *right*." Nearly every man I've represented had a version in which it was owed to him.

**What the first does better.** Almost everything that actually got under my skin is only in the first.

- The correction-with-dignity section. The second has no counterpart. It asserts at the end that fierceness without contempt is possible; the first makes me find my own stairwell. Assertion versus my own evidence. No contest.
- "He can't prove I'm doing this, can he? No. And crucially, neither can I." The second has "nothing that anyone could ever hold against me, nor that I could hold against myself," which is the same idea in a suit.
- The receiving end. "If this is the kind of world I live in... why the fuck shouldn't I?" and "In that world, looking out for yourself isn't a lapse. It's realism." The second says only that it "makes him feel more justified refusing that knowledge in himself." One of those is my clients talking. The other is a summary of them.
- The people we love, the sigh, "which we'll pretend not to have seen," and "*I* could never do that... a dozen times before lunch." Not in the second at all. Those are what I carried out of the room.
- "My verdict says you're irredeemable. My aim depends on your not being so," and the first-page statement of it. The second circles this and never says it.
- The sentence that earned him the word evil ("so that I never again get to believe that my mistake is a different kind of thing"), the hood-and-number footnote, "we never get both views of one instance," and the bad-essence-one-layer-down paragraph. All missing from the second. In their place the second has "turning away isn't one form of darkness among many. It is the very *root*" and "All harm begins by *turning away*," followed by "I can't prove this to you." I respect the admission. But the first opening does better than admit it. It gives me three checks and the footnote.
- The first starts with me as the target of the look. I didn't need to gaze at anyone. For a reader with no practice, that's the easier door. And "So what was it that both of us already knew?" is a better last line than "I might even have an inkling of what that way is," which is coy.

**Where the second is worse for me.** The darkness is a creature from the first analytic paragraph: "it lives in me, too. It is revealing itself to him, knowing how he'll receive it." The first opening at least waits for Chapter 2 and says "behaves like." Then "The darkness leaks out of my skull, empowering his, because its meaning is universal." I don't know what that sentence claims. "In my absolutely deepest moments of introspection" asks me to defer to the depth of his introspection. "I want to be crystal clear here." "I don't think anybody, underneath, believes their contempt is righteous" tells me what's in everybody. And "limbo contains knowledge of itself, in limbo" lost me completely. The hall of mirrors is in both, but the second at least says "You don't have to trace the logic," which the first should borrow.

**Voice.** The second reads as one continuous thought, a man working it out in order, windy in the back half. The first reads as cut and rearranged, with better material added. I won't guess which came first. But two of the seams I flagged are explained by the second file. "Okay, back to the story" makes sense in the second, where Limbo really is a detour from a scenario he returns to; in the first there's nothing to go back to. And "recall how I said that the hidden purpose of my contempt..." points at sentences that exist in the second ("I am *relying* on his reaction, because it is what exonerates me in my own mind") and not in the first. The second has its own loose thread: "We'll see later where it actually comes from."

**If I were handing one to a colleague tomorrow:** the first. Public defenders will forgive being led before they'll forgive eye-gazing and "innocence" on page one. What I'd change first, before anything about style: get the second opening's split, "I really did assess it accurately... But the need to identify it with him?", and its flat sentence about being "fierce and uncompromising" without the digging-in, into the first opening ahead of the narration. My colleagues will quit at "I can't be nice to evil. That's how it wins" if they think he's mocking the fight. Once they know he isn't, they'll read the narration as a confession instead of bracing against it as an accusation.

After that, in order: cut the first paragraph and the "right?"; add the contempt definition; add "I have no argument to offer here"; add "one memory where it's plainly true"; move the self-contempt passage up and give it "It feels like accountability. It isn't."; and keep "darkness" as something I do, the turning away, for as long as he can stand to, before it becomes something that does things.

Tell him the material that moved me is all his own confessing. Neither chapter needed to push, and the second mostly knows that.

Dana
