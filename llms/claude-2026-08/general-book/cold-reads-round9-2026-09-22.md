# Cold reads, round 9 (September 22)

*Two new readers. Stage 1: the general book's Chapter 1 after the round-8 fixes and the rewritten "what is darkness" section (stranger's face, remorse check, no such thing as evil, the agent profile). Jonah's stage 2 is the short book's Chapter 1; Priya's is the round-8 version. Letters verbatim.*

## Jonah (58, Jungian therapist, twenty years of Zen, teaches the shadow; the reader who already gets it)

### Stage 1

Aditya,

You asked for brutal, so I'll skip the part where I tell you it's good. Some of it is. Here's what happened, in order, and I did the exercises with the real cases, which cost me an evening I'd rather have spent otherwise.

**Where the boredom started.** Second paragraph. "Have you ever done something you knew was wrong at the time, but couldn't fully admit it to yourself until later?" I have asked a version of that question of roughly three thousand people, and I sighed. The one I brought was the patient I kept two years past the end, because the income was steady and the sessions were easy, and I told myself she was consolidating. When you asked "did it feel like new information?", no, obviously not. I had noticed the sessions had become maintenance in the first six months. I had a sentence ready for the noticing. That's what the noticing was for.

Then this: "We like to call such knowledge 'unconscious'—as if we had no way of knowing." Here you lost me for a page, and not because it was familiar but because it's a straw man. Nobody in my trade since about 1925 has meant by "unconscious" that we had no way of knowing. Freud's disavowal, Sartre's bad faith, Fingarette's whole book on self-deception: all of them are about knowledge that is kept at arm's length by the same person who holds it. Sartre's point was precisely that you have to know the truth in order to hide it from yourself. So "limbo" is a new coat on an old dog, and any trained reader will see the seams. I'll grant that a lay reader needs a word and "disavowal" is dead on the page. But don't build the word on a caricature of the alternative. Say: the trade has a word for this, it's ugly, here's mine.

What kept me from putting it down in that stretch was one question, and I want you to know which one: "Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" I stopped there. I have sat across from the arguing side for twenty-six years and I knew it was verbose, I had never put it as an asymmetry. The rationalizing part produces reasons in volume; the knowing part produces none and doesn't need to. That is a diagnostic I can use on Monday, and it's the first thing in the chapter I didn't have. Weil is somewhere behind it, but she never said it that plainly.

"Shaming yourself for any of this can feel like accountability. It isn't. It just leads to more hiding." I teach that in the first hour of the seminar. Cut or keep, it's not for me, but a general reader needs it and it's short. It's also the first place you slip into workshop voice, and you'll slip into it again.

**The two strangers.** The first stranger is Marcus Dahl, and you know that story. The complaint was dismissed, and what I felt at the time was your sentence exactly: "who the hell is he to judge?" What I'll add, since you asked for honesty, is something the chapter made me see that I hadn't lined up before. The board letter arrived in the same year I was keeping that patient too long. The complaint was about someone else entirely and it was groundless. But the effect of Marcus's contempt was not confined to the thing he accused me of. It made me dig in on everything. Every place in me where I was in limbo got harder to reach, because looking at any of it felt like conceding to him. So your line "that makes it harder to confront what I've done, not easier" is true and I have a fresh instance, and I don't thank you for it.

The second stranger is Ruth, and this is where I'll push back. "Would you have dug in? Vowed revenge?" The honest answer is that I dig in against Ruth too, and she is not delivering a verdict. She is fierce about what I do, "you go clinical on me," and I go more clinical in response. So the thought experiment as you've written it stipulates its result. You describe a stranger who corrects without verdict and then tell me what I'd feel. What I'd say from the ISTDP side is that you've described pressure with the alliance, Davanloo's whole method: you attack the defense, never the person, and the person's healthy part tends to come toward you. That's real and it's teachable. But "tends to" is the honest verb. Some of us dig in anyway, because the cold form has its own reasons. Your sentence "what makes us dig in isn't the harshness. It's the verdict" is true of the hot cases and you should say so rather than make it a law.

**Where the boredom stopped.** The narration. Specifically: "On the other hand, he can't prove I'm doing this, can he? No. And crucially, neither can I." From that sentence to the end of the narration I was reading as a reader and not as a reviewer, and I'll tell you why. I know projective identification. I could write the textbook paragraph in my sleep: I induce in the other the state I've disowned, then treat it as evidence of who he is. You wrote it better than the textbook and without the term: "I send the verdict. The verdict provokes his defiance. Then I treat his defiance as if I'd discovered it." "As if I'd discovered it" is the sharp bit. And "the stories kept changing; the conclusion never did." I am going to use that in the room, verbatim, with a patient who has three different reasons for cutting off her brother every month and one verdict that never moves. What's the verdict protecting? That's a question I can now ask, and I couldn't quite before.

So: was the writing enough where the content was familiar? In the narration, yes, because the self-observation was finer-grained than the theory and I trust that. Elsewhere, no. A stranger would have put it down at "We like to call such knowledge 'unconscious'" if trained, and at "the only way to truly end evil is to end the turning away" if not. I'll come back to that sentence.

**Your four moves.**

(a) Limbo rather than unconscious. Had it, as I said: Sartre, Fingarette, disavowal. Your version adds nothing to the concept. What it adds is the question form, "did that happen passively?", which gets the reader to the active character of the not-knowing faster than any account I've read. Keep the questions, drop the swipe at "unconscious."

(b) That the contempt is intentional and that its looking intentional is part of how it works. This is the one that's new to me, and I want to be exact about what I had and what I have now. I had: contempt is projection of disowned material. I had: the double bind, from Bateson, as something the receiver suffers. I did not have this: "It requires being present enough to know what I'm doing and to keep it on track, yet absent enough to genuinely believe that I'm not." And then: "Limbo is the only way to do this. It isn't just what lets me do it; it's also what shows him that I am doing it." That is a claim I have not seen anywhere. The disavowal is not merely the enabling condition of the act. It is the content the other person receives. What maddens him is not the malice, it's the malice plus the innocence, delivered as one signal. I tested this against the shower. In the shower I rehearse the case against Marcus, and what I want is not that he suffer. It's that he suffer while knowing that I'm entitled to be glad, and that he can't prove I'm glad. That's the signal, and you've named it. Now: does the argument hold? No, not as argument. "If I didn't, then how could I deliver it so effectively?" is an inference from efficacy to knowledge, and Trivers spent a career on the opposite: self-deception is effective because the deceiver doesn't know, which is why it evolved. Skilled behavior doesn't require awareness. So a Trivers reader has a ready answer to that sentence. What holds is not the inference but the report, which I could verify in myself. You should say it's a report and stop pretending it's a proof.

(c) Darkness is nothing but the turning away; evil is an absence. Augustine, privatio boni, and I have to tell you that Jung spent a good part of Aion and most of Answer to Job calling that doctrine a swindle. He thought evil is real, a substance in the psyche, and that the shadow's whole dignity depends on that. I've held a version of Jung's view for thirty years and I'm not going to drop it on the strength of "consider the possibility." So you've walked into an old fight without marking that it's a fight, and a Jungian reader will feel patronized. To your credit, you do mark it as optional: "if you're not ready to go that far, again just notice what happens when you assume a bad essence." That's the right move and it's the only reason I kept reading. What's new in your version isn't the thesis, it's the route: you get to absence not from theology but from what contempt has to presuppose in its target. Which brings me to the one that matters.

(d) The verdict is addressed to the part it denies. "My contempt is squarely addressed to—and received by—the very part of him I'm claiming doesn't exist. If darkness were truly all he was, there would be nothing left in him for my contempt to corrupt. My verdict says he's irredeemable. My aim depends on his not being so." I have taught for two decades that the person who did wrong must be seen as more than the deed. I held that as a stance, a therapeutic and moral premise, defended on outcomes. I did not have it as a contradiction inside the act of contempt itself. What you've shown, and I think shown rather than asserted, is that contempt is self-refuting in the doing: its efficacy presupposes exactly what its verdict denies. That's not a stance. That's a fact about the mechanism, and it makes the stance consequential in a way it wasn't on Tuesday. This is the sentence I'd tell a colleague to read the chapter for. And since you asked what it changes: my pleasure in Marcus's setbacks is addressed to a Marcus who can feel that he's been seen as nothing. I'm counting on his conscience to deliver the blow. I'd written him off as a man without one. The chapter caught me in the contradiction, and I can't unsee it, and I don't yet know what to do with it, which is presumably what Chapter 2 is for.

**The two exercises.** The stranger's face: I've done this on retreat more times than I can count, it's a standard practice in two traditions I know, and it worked as it always works, until I put Marcus's face there. Then it didn't hold. Not for a second. The story arrived before the face did, "he did it to get ahead," and I watched it arrive. So the exercise did what you wanted, but not by producing the turned-toward state. It produced the turning-away, live, observable. You should know that's what it does for someone with a real enemy, and that it's more useful than the version that works.

"Remorse over having done right." This stopped me, and it's small enough that you might not know what you have. I tried it on Marcus; the picture wouldn't hold. I tried it on Eichmann, whom I've read Arendt on more than once; wouldn't hold. It's a one-sentence demonstration that the good and the harm are not symmetrical in us, without arguing for it, and I've never seen it. One hole: for the true psychopath the picture won't hold because remorse won't hold at all, in any direction, so the test doesn't discriminate at the hard end. Say that or a clever reader will say it for you. For everyone who can feel remorse, it only runs one way, and that's enough.

**Where I was handled.** Count the word "check." I did: eight, plus "see if," plus "notice." By the fifth I was being marched. "Check if you've felt that before, from the receiving end." I'll check what I like. The worst is not any single one, it's the rhythm, which turns a book into a worksheet. For a general reader, some of this stays; they need to be told this is a practice and not a lecture. For me, halve it. Two more sentences: "None of this lets anyone off the hook. We are completely responsible for the harm we cause while in that state." That's answering an objection I hadn't raised, in bold, and the bold is insistence. And "This is why, as best I can tell, the only way to truly end evil is to end the turning away." Aditya. End evil. That's where a stranger puts the book down, and it's where I nearly did, because a sentence that size after a chapter that careful reads as the author losing his nerve and reaching for the big drum. Cut "end evil" and the sentence gets truer.

One more, on the footnote. "Contempt, with the volume turned up" for atrocity is too quick, and it wastes a point you need. Arendt's whole finding about Eichmann was that it wasn't hot at all. It was absence, thoughtlessness, the cold form, the man who couldn't think from the other's position. That's your thesis, evil as the missing thing, sitting in the most famous case there is, and you've filed it as loud contempt.

And this, which is not a tic but a gap. You ask me for my wrongdoing and give me none of yours. "Mine was." That's the whole confession. The narration is fine-grained about what happens in your mind, and I believed it, but the memory the chapter is built on stays unnamed on your side while I'm asked to name mine. A reader like me notices the asymmetry and trusts you a little less for it.

**The paragraph that cost me.** "The sigh that lets them know what we really think of them in that moment, dressed up to make us seem like the reasonable ones." That's "going clinical." That's what Ruth means, and I'd have said she meant I get distant. She means the sigh in a lab coat. The verdict delivered as a diagnosis. And your line about the cold form, "a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them," is my form, and I've never seen it described as contempt rather than as detachment. Detachment is what I called it. You've taken the name away.

Would I read Chapter 2? Yes, on the strength of (b) and (d), and I'd read it with the clock running. It has to do three things. Say what the second stranger does with his face and his voice, in the body, not the principle, because I know the principle. Take the cold form as seriously as the hot one, since the hot one is a chapter and the cold one is a paragraph and the cold one is the one most of your readers over fifty actually run. And not end evil. If Chapter 2 is about ending evil, I'll give it to Ruth and she can tell me what she thinks.

One line I liked and think is slightly false, so you know I was reading: "Limbo aimed at a particular person is malice. Limbo that believes it's righteous is contempt." Malice believes it's righteous half the time too. It's a good sentence. Check whether it's a true one.

Jonah


### Stage 2 (against the short book's Chapter 1)

Aditya,

Second one read. You didn't say who it's for, and I don't need telling. It opens on an eye-gazing practice, it says "a sense of their innocence," it talks about darkness leaking from one skull into another because "its meaning is universal." This is written for people who already sit. I sit. It still bored me more in the first page than the first version did in its whole first half, and I want to say why, because it's not the reason you'd guess.

The face exercise is our standard practice. I've led it. Putting it first means the reader you're writing for meets the chapter on ground they've walked a hundred times, and the phrase "with it comes a sense of their innocence" is the sort of thing that made me leave two sanghas. It's the conclusion smuggled into the instruction. The first version asks me to look and then says "don't try to add it on if not." The second adds it on for me. Between the two, the first trusts me and the second doesn't, and that's the whole difference in the opening.

But the variant is better than anything in the first. "Run it again—but this time, they've just done something atrocious... Keep looking into the same eyes. Can you still find that same recognition?" That's the exercise the first version needed and didn't have. I told you what happened when I put Marcus's face in the first version: the story arrived before the face did. Here you've built that in on purpose, and then you watch what the reader shows the face: "Try and feel what you're showing them through your gaze." That's a better route to "this is all you are" than a hypothetical stranger catching you, because I'm generating the look myself and can feel it in my own eyes. Take it. Put it where the first version does the face, or right after.

The order otherwise is wrong for a stranger and right for me, which is a problem, since you're not writing for me. The second version runs the confrontation narration before the limbo memory. So the reader meets the mechanism before they've found the state in which they can see it. Any clinician will tell you that you start with the patient's own material, not the theory, or the theory bounces. The first version's order is the clinical order: your own case, then the strangers, then me narrating. Keep it.

Now the part that's better argued here than there, and it's the part I told you was new. First version: "It requires being present enough to know what I'm doing... yet absent enough to genuinely believe that I'm not." True, and I could verify it. Second version goes further and gets it sharper: "this is the (possibly unique) case where revealing my self-deception actually benefits me." That's the sentence. It's the thing I hadn't seen anywhere, stated as the anomaly it is. In every other case self-deception has to stay hidden to work, which is Trivers's whole point. Here the leak is the payload. Put that sentence in the first version, near "neither can I," and my objection from Trivers mostly dissolves, because you're no longer inferring knowledge from efficacy; you're pointing out that the efficacy is the knowledge, transmitted. And then this, which I'll quote because you should not lose it: "the more he can see that I'm lying to myself, the more maddening it becomes for him, which makes him more defiant, which I can increasingly use as evidence against him." That's the loop with the gear teeth showing.

Also take: "It actively evades detection. That's not what we usually mean by 'unconscious,' is it?" This does the work the first version's straw man was trying to do, without the straw. You show the defense shifting three times and then ask whether that's passive. I'd still say that repression is active in Freud too, but as a question it leaves me room to say so, and the first version's "as if we had no way of knowing" did not. Swap them.

And take this exercise, which the first version lacks: "Can you harm or use someone while staring your conscience straight in the face, with no justification at hand? Try it in imagination, with something small." I did it with the patient. Could I have kept her in treatment while looking straight at her, with no "she's consolidating"? No. It won't go. The justification isn't decoration on the act; the act needs it to happen at all. That's a small demonstration of something I've taught abstractly, and it puts the reader's hand on the thing.

And "limbo contains knowledge of itself, in limbo." That's the recursion, said in seven words, and it's true of the shower.

The self-contempt section. "Nobody ever looked honestly at something in themselves while being told they were worthless for having it." That's my seminar in a sentence and it's the best one-sentence statement of the shame-hiding link I've read, and it's not new to me and it's needed. What is new to me in that section is the pivot: "What you need in order to look is what your enemy needed from you: to be seen as more than what you did." That joins the inward verdict to the outward one as one act, which the first version's throwaway "shaming yourself... just leads to more hiding" doesn't do. Take the pivot. You can lose most of the rest of that paragraph around it.

Now what the second version is right to have left out, and what it's wrong to have lost.

Right to leave out: the metaphysics. There's no "consider the possibility that there is no such thing" as evil here, no absence thesis, and a Jungian reader gets no fight picked with him. The second version stays at "turning away is the root" and "I can't prove this to you, but I invite you to check it," which is honest and I accepted it on those terms. If you want the Buddhist reader, they already have the privatio boni in their own vocabulary and don't need it from you. If you want me, I've told you where I stand. Either way the first version's "no such thing" costs more than it buys and the second shows you can do without it.

Wrong to have lost: the sentence the whole thing turns on. The second version never says that the contempt is addressed to the part it denies. It gets close, "the part of my mind that needs him to dig in must reveal a combination of darkness and self-righteousness to him," but the performative contradiction, my aim depends on his not being irredeemable, is not in here. That was the one line in the first version that changed something I'll do. Without it the second version is a finer description of a mechanism and a worse argument, because the reason contempt is self-refuting is never given. Also lost: the remorse check, which I told you is small and sharp and I've not seen elsewhere, and the paragraph about the people we love, which was the one that cost me. The second version has nothing about the sigh. For a reader who sits, that's the paragraph they need most, since the meditator's favorite contempt is the cold one at home while being serene at the center.

Where the second bores me more, and it does, overall: the ending. From "This is why I say there is no 'good kind' of darkness" to the last line, you're preaching to a room that's already nodding. "I want to be crystal clear here." "Would be **devastating**." "It's **all a single move**." "Not that anyone could 'deserve' to have their mind darkened." "I might even have an inkling of what that way is." That last one is coy, and coy is worse than workshop voice. The first version's ending, "Where else might it be, in an ordinary day?", is a question I'm still carrying. The second version's is a promise you're withholding. And "We'll see later where it actually comes from" is a second IOU on the same page. I count debts, and you're running two.

"We know they know we know." I liked it and I'd cut it, or footnote it. It's Schelling's common knowledge applied to a look, and it's correct, and it turns the reader into a logician for one sentence at the moment you want them feeling the hall of mirrors, which you then say they might have felt, which is the sentence to keep.

One more thing the second version does better and the first should steal without the bolding. "That means it must produce no external evidence of what it's doing—nothing that anyone could ever hold against me, nor that I could hold against myself." That's the design constraint on the whole apparatus, and it explains why it's a look and not a sentence. The first version has "he can't prove I'm doing this... neither can I," which is the same fact from inside. Put the constraint next to it.

So: the first, with these transplants. The atrocious-stranger variant of the face. The "possibly unique case" sentence and the gear-teeth loop. The "actively evades detection" question in place of the straw man. The conscience-straight-in-the-face exercise. The self-contempt pivot, shortened. The "no external evidence" constraint. And keep, from the first, what the second forgot: the aim that depends on the denied part, the remorse check, and the sigh at the dinner table. Drop "end evil" from the first and the sermon from the second and you'll have a chapter I'd hand to a trainee without wincing, which, from me, is the review.

Jonah

**Intentional**

When Devesh embarrasses himself at dinner I feel something I'd have called satisfaction and it's actually closer to relief, because for a minute I don't have to wonder whether I'm being unfair to him. I hadn't seen that the enjoyment was doing a job.

From the receiving end, "A slip I can shrug off, or even forgive. What I can't shrug off is the sense that he means it, and wants me to know that he does, and to top it all off, wants to believe he's the good guy for it." Yes. That's Carla in the meeting exactly: the killing wasn't the injury, "de-risking" was, because it made her the responsible one.

So did I grant "intentional"? About you, yes, you're describing something and you seem to know it. About me: I grant it the way the failed essay was intentional. I didn't decide to punish him. I also didn't not decide. "It requires being present enough to know what I'm doing and to keep it on track, yet absent enough to genuinely believe that I'm not." That's the right description of the grading and I'm not going to argue with it.

Where it lost me: "And it only works when it looks intentional—and it worked on him." I read that three times. I think you mean the receiver only digs in if he reads intent, but the sentence is doing two things at once and the em-dash isn't helping. And "Limbo is the only way to do this" is the first "only" I didn't believe, followed by "This is the hardest part to admit," which is you telling me what a paragraph will do before it does it.
