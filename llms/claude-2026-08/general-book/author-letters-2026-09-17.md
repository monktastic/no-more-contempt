Dear Aditya,

I read it twice. The first time was over two evenings with the two people you asked me to bring. One is the public man I think is doing real damage. The other is the woman who plagiarized my friend and was never made to answer for it. The second reading was with a pencil.

**What happened as I read**

The preface and introduction nearly lost me, even though you had warned me who they were for. The introduction opens with "The mind is intrinsically compassionate. Evil is nothing but a mind turned away from itself." That is two claims I don't grant, stated in the indicative before you have shown me anything. By the end of the introduction I had been promised the Devil and "hidden conspiracies," followed by "You don't need to believe that to read on." In a bookstore that is where I put it back. You had told me what I would find before I had looked.

Chapter 1 begins as a different book. "Imagine looking into a stranger's eyes long enough that their face stops being something you're looking at and becomes a place somebody is looking out of." I did it, and it worked.

The hot version didn't fit me. "*Ha!*" is not a sound I make. I was getting ready to feel excused when you wrote "a quiet filing-away, a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them." That is what I have done to her on two panels and at one memorial. "Small satisfaction" is accurate. I would not have used the word contempt for it before reading this, and I would now.

Limbo is the best section in the manuscript. "Did it feel like new information? Or like something you had always known, but somehow hadn't *let* yourself know?" My memory was a review I wrote at thirty-four of a rival's book. Every sentence in it was accurate, the whole was unfair, and I knew that while I was typing. Then you ask "Why did one side have to argue, and the other not?" I wrote that on an index card. You asked the question and stopped, and I did the rest myself. When this manuscript is good, that is its method.

The back half of Chapter 1 is where I put it down for the night. Within a few pages my sigh at a colleague becomes "the condition we all recognize as **evil**," in bold. It is then "the source of all hatred," and then "That's how the thing behind the atrocities is fed by a sigh." That last sentence is well made, but I don't believe it yet, and at that speed it reads as a sermon. The sentences about me then turn into verdicts:

- "So you *know* they're redeemable, and you're blocking their way back."
- "Some part of you knew that too."
- "This was all there to see, if you had looked."

This is the first stranger's move, and it is being made on me. Your theory predicts my resistance, because resistance is what limbo would produce, so I cannot say no in any way the book couldn't absorb. You seem to know this, because you keep opening doors. One is "Check whether any of that is true in the case you picked." Another is "If you find none of this, good; I have no way to argue with that and won't try." Those doors kept me reading. In that stretch, though, there are far more verdicts than doors.

The face-to-face model also doesn't fit my public enemy. He has never heard of me, and I am not "keeping him trapped in the darkness." My contempt lands on me and on the people at my table. You do know this. The executive paragraph covers it, and so does "each one negligible, and together they set how dark everyone assumes the world is." I believe that more modest claim, and it is the stronger one. Most of your readers' enemies are on a screen.

The Interlude got me. "In me it's a hot little surge, something like *fuck yeah.* A fist closing. I want to watch it again, and send it to someone. Notice that nothing got better." I had sent that kind of clip the week before.

The pill, in the daily form it takes in Chapter 4, also got me: "would you feel relief, or robbed?" I asked it about him. Suppose he simply faded, with no fall and no footage. My answer was robbed, and it arrived before I could dress it up. I asked it about her and the answer was robbed again. Part of that is legitimate, because my friend is owed an acknowledgment, and you allow for that when you write "costs them what they owe." There was also something left over that wasn't about my friend. I found it, and I don't like it. It is what I couldn't shake.

The two catchers landed at once, because I have had both. The list of "extras" is the best writing in the manuscript: "*You lied to me.* With the extra: *Of course you lied to me.*" The difference is tiny and concrete, and I can check it against my own mouth.

In Chapter 2 I leaned in hard at "Contempt and love are addressed to the same place. One says *I know you're in there.* The other says *there's nobody in there*, and they both say it to the somebody who is." That passage is your central paradox in a form I can carry with me. The hall of mirrors and "common knowledge in the game-theoretic sense" are not that form. I can verify two levels of we-both-know in myself. I cannot verify "etc."

You say the full book earns that from the reader's own memory. Then you already know the route, because it is what you do in Limbo and the Interlude. Wherever you do it, I am reached. Everything practical survives a naturalist reading. That paragraph in the appendix is more persuasive than the chapter it footnotes.

**Short routes**

The first page should be the two people who caught you. Every reader has had both. It needs no enemy and no metaphysics. It flatters nobody. It also plants the distinction between the act and the verdict, which everything else hangs on.

After that I would order it this way:

1. The clip.
2. Relief or robbed.
3. Limbo.
4. The stranger's eyes and the mechanism.
5. The paradox, in its "same place" form.
6. The Chapter 4 checks and the extras.

Then I would stop the book there.

I would move several things out of Appendix 1 and into the body:

- The cutting-in-line passage: "The cost is thirty seconds. The heat is out of all proportion to thirty seconds."
- The forgotten birthday set against the cutting remark.
- The six-step list, which is clearer than the hall of mirrors.

I would bring Braithwaite forward too, because the data will make a skeptic relax.

I would save these for a later book:

- Awareness as care.

Wherever the evidence is your own, change "you know" to "I found." State the contract with the naturalist reader once, early in the book.

**The one idea**

In my own words: the thing in me that wants my enemy to know he is garbage is not my conscience, although it uses my conscience's voice. I can tell the two apart by asking whether I would be relieved or robbed if the harm simply stopped. I will still be carrying that a week from now, because it is five words long and it worked on me.

The idea that would make the book matter is different. It is that contempt is addressed to the very person it declares absent. That idea isn't planted well enough yet. The passages that plant it are "the same place" and "we only treat people as irredeemable because part of us knows they're not." The mirrors bury it.

**What I would tell a friend**

Here are the two sentences. "It's about the difference between wanting harm to stop and wanting the person who did it to know what he is, and it argues that the second, which feels like conscience, is the thing it claims to fight, passed hand to hand. The author is a Buddhist practitioner who thinks you already know this and gives you ways to check."

Would I recommend it in its current form? To my friend who sits, yes. For a burned-out organizer I know, I would photocopy the Interlude and the Chapter 4 checks. I would not yet give it to my general-reader friends. I would not yet give it to the friend who was plagiarized.

The other is Appendix 2, which is the best prose in the manuscript: "If those are the conclusions, I haven't discovered anything." "So that claim is false, and I withdraw it." I would follow that voice anywhere. The revealer's voice in the introduction is one I would not follow. Use the Appendix 2 voice for your opening pitch: what is new here is smaller than it looks, and it lives underneath the old conclusions.

There are no people in this book. You have hypothetical executives and a locker room, and that is all. Your origin story is a subordinate clause in an appendix: "it was done to me and I had, for once, no way of not noticing." Tell that story. Tell me who the two catchers were. A general reader is carried by a person, and at the moment you aren't on the page.

Smaller things:

I wanted this to be good, and a third of it already is.

Then Chapter 1 began and I paid attention. The stranger's eyes worked modestly on me. I did get to "a place somebody is looking out of," which is a good phrase. "The takedown you reread after posting it" describes me, and it happened this month. The audience is the transmission route for someone like me, and you leave it to one sentence that defers to Chapter 3.

The Interlude is the best thing in the manuscript, and the quality changes sharply there. The pill worked on me. With the public figure I would take the pill, with a small pang. **How close**

The book has two layers. One is a phenomenology of contempt with tests a reader can run on himself. That layer is close to ready for a reader like me. It needs scenes and reordering, and its core needs no rewriting.

The other layer is a metaphysics of awareness with a cosmology attached: the first turning, parasites resonating, the Devil as emergent coordination. That layer is a long way from my kind of reader, and another draft won't close the gap. The gap is between what you can show and what you believe.

The short version puts the second layer in front of the first, which makes sense for your sangha. For the book you want, keep about 40 percent and reverse the order.

**Where it's hardest**

*Words.* "Evil," "the darkness," "the light," "true nature," "awakening," "the Devil." You do define "evil" carefully: "By *evil* I don't mean harm... I mean the not-seeing that licenses it." The definition comes after the word has already cost you. "The light," with or without the capital, is a word that lets a conclusion in through the vocabulary. I can see you know this, because you keep adding hedges around it.

The words I would keep: "contempt," "the verdict," "turned toward / turned away," "limbo," "the extra," "the part that knows / the part that proves," "fierce." These are plain, they belong to you, and they hold up.

I was surprised to find I would keep "parasite," as a biologist, because you state its drawbacks: "a groove in attention so old that its shape looks like intent." That is well put. But a parasite has interests of its own and a groove does not. Once yours begin to "want" things and "collaborate," you have the creature you promised not to bring in.

*The unfalsifiable "part of you knows."* This will bother a skeptical reader throughout. Your opt-outs are real, and I noticed each one. "If you find none of this, good; I have no way to argue with that and won't try" is a generous sentence. They sit alongside "We all know what it is, while pretending not to," "some part of you knew that too," and "and part of us knows it, and we need it to be that way." If I look and don't find it, the theory says it is hidden.

That is the form of the worst habit in psychoanalysis, and a reader like me recognizes it within a page. You can't remove it entirely, because limbo is your subject. You can state the problem in your own voice early on, and then limit "you know" claims to places where the reader has just found the thing himself. At present you use it where he has found it and where he hasn't.

*Empirical claims.* These didn't matter to me in the Interlude or in Limbo. My own memory was the evidence there, and no citation would have added anything.

They started to matter with "Usually the latter," on whether contempt produces defiance. That is the hinge of the whole book. It is probably true, and you have Braithwaite and Tangney in an appendix. Put them in the body, in two paragraphs, early. For my kind of reader, that is what turns the book from a sermon into a finding.

They mattered most in four places.

- "The murderer, the abuser, the man in the news: each is this one step, taken thousands of times." This is a developmental theory of violence stated as observation. Gilligan supports part of it. It also leaves out temperament, head injury, and everything else I would reach for.
- "even the psychopath appears turned away... nobody has reported the case," in Appendix 1. The sadism and instrumental-aggression literature does not read that way. Your own Appendix 3 treats the same evidence more honestly: "it can't prove the care is there." The two appendices contradict each other, and the weaker claim is the correct one.

- "Stockholm syndrome." It is a contested construct, and you use it as though it were settled.

The deepest issue is this one: "If awareness were neutral between care and harm, why would harming someone require turning away from it?... The simplest explanation is that care belongs to awareness itself." For a biologist that is not the simplest explanation. The simplest one is an evolved empathic brake on aggression that has to be downregulated. You also never engage the evolutionary account of punitive sentiment. The urge to punish looks like deterrence machinery, and it is rewarding in its own right.

Your account of why we feel contempt, a buried suspicion that we ourselves aren't good, may be true of you. It wasn't what I found in myself. I found pleasure, status and the warmth of being on a side. Your core does not need the fear story. "The draw is separable from the stopping, and the draw makes things worse" holds on either account.

You seem to know this too. The best paragraph for my kind of reader is near the end of Appendix 1: "What's left to a naturalist is a universal human faculty that can't be outweighed and happens to have an evolutionary history. That differs from my reading only about origin." Give me that concession on page 10 and I will follow you much further.

*Overreach I would cut or keep for another book:*
