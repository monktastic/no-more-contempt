# Chapter 1: The Look

You've seen it before: political fights that rapidly degrade into each side becoming more entrenched; more certain that the other side is evil. I want to show you that what's happening beneath this common interaction is actually something highly counterintuitive. If I'm right, it has implications far beyond the political sphere, touching most of what we do.

---

Say you and I are political enemies. Put a real face on me, if you have one. I mock, shame, or contemptuously judge you. Immediately you sense something hypocritical about my behavior, right? But what, exactly?

I'm making a claim without using words: you're *less* than me. I might not even be thinking it explicitly, but there's a feeling: I'm a better kind of thing than you. *I* could never be like *that* (whatever you are).

Can you feel that? What's hypocritical about it?

I'm *othering* you, and with it comes a subtle (but perceptible) sense that your wellbeing matters less than mine. It's not that I wish you harm, per se, but if you *were* harmed, it wouldn't really affect me much—because you're a different kind of thing—and I want you to know that. Can you feel that?

Here is what I'll claim, and this chapter is my case. To deliver that feeling, I have to address it to the part of you I'm claiming doesn't exist: the part that's exactly like me, and can read what I'm doing. I have to know I'm doing it, and know that you can *see* I'm doing it, all while claiming to be doing no such thing. That's the hypocrisy you're sensing. But the really strange part is this: I'm not just claiming not to know—part of me really doesn't.

You don't have to grant any of this yet. I'll walk us through it.

---

I'll narrate what happens in my mind in a real-life encounter. I'll do it to a target who's not you, so that you can inhabit the co-pilot seat alongside me. To make it sharper, say this is someone who's done something bad. Maybe he's just been cruel to an innocent person. The threat is over, and now I'm confronting him.

If it sounds like I'm contradicting myself in places, it's because two parts of my mind are in tension. See if you recognize any of it in yourself.

Okay, so: I've decided that he's a *bad person*—not just holds bad ideas or does bad things, but *is* bad. And I want him to feel the full weight of it, almost as a kind of punishment. I tell myself that maybe this will get through to him. That if he finally sees how bad he is, maybe he'll change. The message is something like: *this is all you are*.

Part of me senses something *dark* about this, but I can't quite place it. If I were on the receiving end, I would recognize it instantly. The message *this is all you are* carries a subtle but primal threat: *... and therefore your wellbeing has ceased to matter.* It's the same thing you felt above.

Who *wouldn't* violently defy a message like that? I certainly would.

But if I knew this is what I was conveying, it would undermine the verdict I feel strangely attached to: he's **bad**, dammit. Irredeemable. So I push that knowledge out of mind. My message is *righteous*, and if he reacts poorly, it only proves my point.

And it's not like I'm *actually* threatening him. I'm just hinting at what will happen if he doesn't stop. When you look at it that way, it's really more like an *invitation*—to give up his darkness and return to the realm of those who matter.

But the truth is, there's **nothing** inviting about my tone or gaze. Nothing that signals he'd be welcome back. How could such a signal coexist with my message that *this is all he is*? It couldn't.

On the other hand, he can't prove it, can he? And neither can I.

Here's the story I'm telling myself: sure, I may be flashing him something dark, but it's the *good* kind of darkness—the kind meant to fight *real* darkness. If he can't see that, it's on him. What else would you expect from a person like this?

Part of me senses it's a bit underhanded, but I quickly push that out of my mind. Because he's *actually bad*. I can see it in him. Who cares if I have to skirt some rules to expose it? I can't just be nice to evil. That's how it wins.

And anyway, it's not like he knows I'm being disingenuous. I barely recognize it myself, so how could he? He's not a mind reader—so if he accuses me of it, it can only mean one thing: he's projecting.

And wouldn't you know it: there it is. He's accusing me of exactly that. Not with his words, mind you, but I can see it in his eyes:

> *Don't pretend you're doing something noble here. We both know what you're actually doing.*

*Shit.* Something inside knows he's right. But I absolutely cannot afford to admit it, least of all now. I don't even want to believe I have that kind of darkness in me—the kind that would knowingly forsake a fellow being just to prove he's beyond help.

The good news is, maybe I *don't* have it in me, because here's the thing: fighting evil genuinely requires being fierce. Isn't that all I'm doing? Surely that's all I'm doing.

Look, I know I'm not a saint, but being a little sharp with a *bad person* is a pretty silly thing to quibble about.

And yet he just won't let it go. He thinks *I'm* the bad guy. *Ha!* Classic.

Any doubt I had is now erased. *This really **is** what you are—and I'm going to make sure everyone sees it.*

Yet for some reason, this only makes him even *more* defiant...

---

To provide contrast, think of a time when someone corrected you without the slightest bit of contempt, but with dignity instead. Not *gently*. Just that his anger was directed at what you *did*, and not what you *are*.

Did it make you dig in? Vow revenge? Probably not. 

What was different about it?

It's like he was reminding you of something: that *you're better than this*. So what makes you dig in isn't the harshness. It's this.

Okay, back to the story with my target.

---

I'll just go ahead and name what's happening. I needed him to be bad, and his digging in proved to me that he *is* bad. And as you will have guessed, that's not a coincidence.

Deep down, I know there's darkness still left in me—the same thing I see in my enemies—but I'm not ready to confront it. So I tell myself I'll do the next best thing: defeat it in others.

The problem is, try as I might, my own can't help but leak out. I'll anthropomorphize my own darkness, for clarity's sake. I'm not claiming it's a real thing.

It knows that the easiest way to keep itself hidden from me is to attack the same thing in others. Because fighting evil is actually a good thing, and it can be hard to tell apart healthy fierceness (like the above) from the dark kind. The only difference is that the dark kind delivers a verdict: *this is all you are.* It's not something I have to say out loud. It can be communicated entirely via tone and gaze—things that can't easily be audited, either by others or, crucially, *by myself.*

If I knew I were planting the evidence, I couldn't turn around and sincerely hold it against them. So I must genuinely not know I'm doing this, at least in any way I can prove.

And notice what else this accomplishes. If I were only communicating darkness, what they receive would feel like malice. That would be frightening, but probably not maddening. What makes it maddening (causing them to dig in) is that we both know it's dark, and at the same time I appear to genuinely believe it's good. It means they have no recourse, and I'll have nothing to feel guilty about.

Now: how can we "both know it's dark" while I also *genuinely* believe it's good? Notice that both halves must come through for it to be maximally triggering.

Let's take the first half. I must *know* it's dark, they must *see* that I know this, they must see that I know *they* see it (so that it appears intentional, not accidental), and so on. You don't have to trace the logic. Just notice that eerie sense of *we both know what's really happening here.* It's because part of me *does* know it, and needs them to know that.

And the second half is that I genuinely *don't* know it, and it helps if I know they can see that, too. That way, they know they can't appeal to my better nature, because I'm convinced this *is* my better nature. They are trapped.

---
----








Think of a time from your real life when someone treated you with contempt. Something in their face or tone said you weren't worth the trouble of seeing.

How long did it take you to know what it was? My guess is very quickly, and that you knew before you could have said what you'd seen. Stay with what it was like. Where did it reach? Not your pride. Pride shrugs off an insult and comes back with one. This went somewhere further in. Remember where, because we'll need it later.

Now a second memory, for contrast. Someone caught you at something, and was harsh about it, and you walked away more yourself than before. If you've had one of those, put it next to the first. The second person may have said more, and said it in front of more people. What was different? Not the harshness. Not whether they were right; both may have been right. Something else.

Here's what I think it was, and you can check it against the two memories. The first person had stopped seeing you. Whatever was looking out of your eyes had been ruled out of the picture, and the verdict was *this is all you are.* The second one never stopped. You could feel yourself still in view the whole time he was taking you apart. Call the first *turned away* and the second *turned toward.* The words are only labels for something you could tell apart before I named it.

One more thing about the first person. He wasn't just turned away—he was calling it right. He'd have told you he was pointing out what you were, for your own good or for everyone's. That's the hypocrisy from the opening, and notice the sting doesn't come from the words; the second person's words may have been worse. It comes from the turning away, worn as righteousness.

<!-- beat 3: where harm comes from -->

Try something. Picture yourself harming someone, on purpose, while looking them in the eyes with what you'd recognize as real kindness. Not tough love; the real thing, the way the second person looked at you. Does the picture hold? For me it comes apart every time. I can picture the harm, and I can picture the kindness, and I can't get them to happen at once. To do the harm, something has to go out of the eyes first.[^far]

If that's what you find too, then you know something about the first person's look. It wasn't an unpleasant manner. It was the thing that has to happen before harm can. Whatever he did or didn't do next, he'd shown you the place harm comes from, in himself. And you read it. That's what you knew in under a second.

[^far]: If the picture held for you, because the harm you pictured was small, run it out. Every account we have of atrocity has the same step in it before the harm: the hood, the number instead of the name, the word for vermin. Nobody skips it. The look you got was that step, at a size where nothing followed.

<!-- beat 4: now you; limbo -->

Now the harder memory. A time you did it. Keep it small, because the small ones are the honest ones: the sigh when someone you love got it wrong again. The *of course you did.* The look across the table that you'd deny if asked. Something from this week, if you can find one.

A few questions about it. Did you deliver it where it would land? Not to the wall, not to the mirror; to them, at the moment they'd catch it. Was it in a form you could deny? The sigh that was "just tired," the look you "didn't give." And did you know, on the way out, what they'd feel?

I'll tell you how it goes in me. I know what they'll feel; that's what the sigh is for. I keep it small enough to deny. And if you asked me in the moment, I'd say I was tired, and I'd half believe it. Knowing, and not letting myself know, at once. See whether you can find that in your own case. If you can't, the rest of this chapter is about me.

One more question. If it had come at you instead, the same sigh from them: would you have reflected on what you'd got wrong? Or gone quiet, or hit back?

<!-- beat 5: the message assembled; the out-loud test -->

So put it together, from the sending end this time. You're turned away from them. You know what that is, because you've been on the other end and knew it instantly there. You're showing it to them: aimed, deniable, timed to land. And you're calling it fair; they had it coming, you were only reacting. They can see all of that, and you know they can, because you were counting on it. Otherwise, why aim?

If you'd said it in words, it would have gone something like this:

> I know what you are, because I'm it too. I'm doing it to you right now, and I'm calling mine fair. You can see that. Try proving it.

Ask what saying it aloud would have added. Would they have learned anything? Would you have learned anything about what they knew? I don't think so. Everything in that sentence had already passed between you. The only thing the words would change is that now they could quote you.

That's the difference between contempt and plain malice. Malice wants to hurt and doesn't care whether you understand. This needs you to understand, and needs to be deniable, both at once. It's a taunt. And a taunt only works on someone who gets it.

<!-- beat 6: what it does; relieved or robbed -->

What does it do to them? You know from the other end. They dig in. Then you use the digging in as proof: see, he can't be reasoned with. Which is strange, since you'd have done the same.

Here's a check I use on myself when I catch this. Say the thing I'm objecting to stopped tomorrow, and the person simply saw it: no punishment, nobody made to feel anything. Relieved, or robbed? When there's a flicker of robbed, something in me wanted more than the correction. It wanted them to feel it. And a person who's been made to feel it doesn't reflect. So whatever I was telling myself, the sigh wasn't for their improvement.

<!-- beat 7: the harm that needs all this -->

Go back to the first memory, the one that reached further than pride. What it did to you wasn't only to put you on the defensive. A threat does that, and you recover from a threat. I think this reached where it did because it taught you something. You knew what he was doing. You could not have proved it to anyone. And the lesson that lands in that gap is: the part of you that knew, and couldn't prove it, isn't worth listening to.

<!-- beat 8: the address; the punchline -->

Now the claim I opened with. What, in you, was he aiming at? He was calling you a person with nothing in you worth addressing. But a taunt lands only on someone who still cares what you are; someone who, right then, still had him in view and wanted better from him. Pride can't be taunted, only insulted. What can be taunted is care. He was aiming at the one part of you that could hear him, and calling it absent.

And he found it. He didn't miss. To aim at that part of you, he had to know where it was, and the only thing that can find that in another person is the same thing in yourself. So the message denies, in both of you, the very thing it needed in both of you to be delivered at all.

That's why it feels like hypocrisy before you can say why. Contempt is a message from care to care, denying both.

<!-- close: the other claims, now that the reader has the material -->

So here is what I've claimed, and you can hold each against what you found. Something in me wants you to feel it. Part of me works to make sure you do, while making sure I don't know I'm doing it. You can read both halves. What you're reading is a very mild version of the thing we all recognize as evil. And to deliver it at all, I have to address it to the part of you I claim isn't there.

<!-- beat 9: covered, not gone; hand-off -->

One last check, and the next chapter leans on it. After the first memory, did you stop caring about him? Or did you have to keep not caring: the case kept open, the reasons rehearsed, his face kept out of view? In me it's always the second. It takes upkeep. If the caring had gone, there'd have been nothing to maintain.

So it wasn't reduced. It was covered. And that leaves a question about what was under the cover the whole time, in him and in you. You couldn't have sent it if you'd seen them clearly. Neither could he. What is it that had to be covered first?
