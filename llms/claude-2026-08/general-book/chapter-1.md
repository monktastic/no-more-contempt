# Chapter 1: The Look

You've seen it before: political fights that rapidly degrade into each side becoming more entrenched; more certain that the other side is evil. I want to show you that what's happening beneath this common interaction is actually something highly counterintuitive. If I'm right, it has implications far beyond the political sphere, touching nearly everything we do.

---

Say you and I are political enemies. Put a real face on me, if you have one. I mock, shame, or contemptuously judge you. Immediately you sense something hypocritical about my behavior, right? But what, exactly?

I'm making a claim without using words: you're *less* than me. I might not even be thinking it explicitly, but there's a feeling: I'm a better kind of thing than you. *I* could never be like *that* (whatever you are).

Can you feel that? What's hypocritical about it?

I'm *othering* you, and with it comes a subtle (but perceptible) sense that your wellbeing matters less than mine. It's not that I wish you harm, per se, but if you *were* harmed, it wouldn't really affect me much—because you're a different kind of thing—and I want you to know that. Can you feel that?

Here's what I'll claim, and this chapter is my case. To deliver that feeling, I have to address it to the part of you I'm claiming doesn't exist: the part that's exactly like me; that can read what I'm doing. I have to know I'm doing it, and know that you can *see* I'm doing it, while claiming to be doing no such thing. That's the hypocrisy you're sensing. But the really strange part is this: I'm not just *claiming* not to know—part of me really doesn't.

You don't have to grant any of this yet. I'll walk us through it.

---

I'll narrate what happens in my mind in a real-life encounter. I'll do it to a target who's not you, so that you can inhabit the co-pilot seat alongside me. To make it sharper, say this is someone who's done something bad. Maybe he's just been cruel to an innocent person. The threat is over, and now I'm confronting him. If it sounds like I'm contradicting myself in places, it's because two parts of my mind are in tension. See if you recognize any of it in yourself.

Okay, so: I've decided that he's a *bad person*—not just holds bad ideas or does bad things, but *is* bad. And I want him to feel the full weight of it, almost as a kind of punishment. I tell myself that maybe this will get through to him. That if he finally sees how bad he is, maybe he'll change. The message is something like: *this is all you are*.

Part of me senses something dark about this, but I can't quite place it. If I were on the receiving end, I would recognize it instantly. The message *this is all you are* carries a subtle but primal threat: *... and therefore your wellbeing has ceased to matter.* It's the same thing you felt above.

Who *wouldn't* violently defy a message like that? I certainly would.

But if I knew this is what I was conveying, it would undermine the verdict I feel strangely attached to: he's **bad**, dammit. Irredeemable. So I push that knowledge out of mind. My message is *righteous*, and if he reacts poorly, well, it only proves my point.

And it's not like I'm actually threatening him. I'm just hinting at what will happen if he doesn't stop. When you look at it that way, it's really more like an *invitation*—to give up his darkness and return to the realm of those who matter.

But the truth is, there's **nothing** inviting about my tone or gaze. Nothing that signals he'd be welcome back. How could there be, when I'm trying to convince him that *this is all he is*?

On the other hand, he can't prove I'm doing this, can he? No. And crucially, neither can I.

Here's the story I'm telling myself: sure, it may be "dark" in some abstract sense, but it's the *good* kind of darkness—the kind meant to fight *real* darkness. If he can't see that, it's on him. What else would you expect from a person like this?

Part of me senses it's a bit underhanded, but I bury that too. Because he's *actually bad*. I can **see** it in him. Who cares if I have to skirt some rules to expose it? I can't be nice to evil. That's how it wins.

And anyway, it's not like he knows I'm being disingenuous. I barely recognize it myself, so how could he? He's not a mind reader—so if he accuses me of it, it can only mean one thing: he's projecting.

And wouldn't you know it: there it is. He's accusing me of exactly that. Not with his words, mind you, but with his eyes:

> *Don't pretend you're doing something noble here. We both know what you're actually doing.*

*Shit.* Something inside knows he's right. But I absolutely cannot afford to admit it, least of all now. I don't even want to believe I have that kind of darkness in me—the kind that would knowingly forsake a fellow being just to prove he's beyond help.

The good news is, maybe I *don't* have it in me, because here's the thing: fighting evil genuinely requires being fierce. Isn't that all I'm doing? Surely that's all I'm doing. Look, I know I'm not a saint, but being a little extra sharp with a *bad person* is a pretty silly thing to quibble about.

And yet he just won't let it go. He thinks *I'm* the bad guy. *Ha!* Classic.

Any doubt I had is progressively erased. *This really **is** what you are—and I'm going to make sure everyone sees it.*

Yet for some reason, this only makes him even *more* defiant...

---

To provide contrast, think of a time when someone corrected you without the slightest bit of contempt, but with dignity instead. Not *gently*. Maybe even fiercely. But his anger was directed at what you *did*, and not what you *are*.

Did it make you dig in? Vow revenge?

What was different about it?

When people do this well, they are reminding you of something: that *you're better than this*. What makes us dig in isn't the harshness. It's the verdict.

---

Okay, back to the story. I need to know my enemy is bad, and his digging in proves to me that he *is* bad. As you may have guessed, that's not a coincidence. I'll just go ahead and name what's happening. 

Deep down, I know there's darkness in me—the same thing I see in my enemies—but I'm not ready to confront it in myself. So I tell myself I'll do the next best thing: attack it in others. That way, I'm still making the world a better place. The problem is, whenever I do this, my own leaks out. 

To make this easier to follow, I'll anthropomorphize my own darkness. I'm not claiming it's a real, separate thing.

My darkness knows that the easiest way to keep itself hidden from me is to attack it in others. That's because fighting evil is actually a good thing, and it can be hard to tell apart the healthy kind (which the person above did, and which works) from the dark kind (which creates more darkness). The only difference is that the dark kind delivers a verdict: *this is all you are.* And it's not even something I have to say out loud. It can be communicated entirely via tone and gaze—things that can't easily be audited, either by others or *by myself.*

This makes *fighting evil* the ideal avenue for darkness to inject itself into my behavior: it causes me to reinforce it in others, which helps me continue blaming them instead of looking inside.

If I knew I were planting the evidence, I couldn't then turn around and sincerely hold it against him. So I must genuinely not know I'm doing it, at least in any way I can prove. At the same time, I *must* know it well enough to actually do it.

This means my darkness has two jobs: to reveal itself to my target, and to hide itself from me.

The revealing must look intentional. He has to know it's no accident: that I know what I'm doing, and that I can see it landing. A slip he could shrug off, or even forgive. A message he can't. So he has to know what I'm flashing him, and know that I know, and know that I know he knows, etc.[^ck]. Tone and gaze can carry all of it, while leaving nothing either of us could quote.

[^ck]: Game theorists call this *common knowledge*.

And hiding itself from me accomplishes two things. As we already saw, it allows me to hold his reaction against him. But it's also crucial in *provoking* that reaction: if I were displaying open malice, it might be frightening, but probably not maddening—and it needs to be maddening for him to dig in. It's that unique combination of intentional darkness and feigned innocence that makes it work. That way, he knows he can't appeal to my better nature, because I think this *is* my better nature. My look tells him that he has no recourse, and I'll have nothing to feel guilty about.

But "feigned" isn't quite the right word, because again, part of me genuinely doesn't know.

This means there are two channels of communication operating: one saying *I know exactly what I'm doing* and another saying *I'm only holding you to account.* Both must appear intentional, and remain isolated from each other. Together they produce a message that's something like this:

> *I am revealing to you the very darkness I'm accusing you of, because I know it will provoke you, proving that you are what I say you are. But I won't know I'm doing this, and you have no way of proving that I am—which is exactly what makes it provocative.* 

*That's* the hypocrisy you sensed at the start of this chapter.

Notice that the second channel takes no extra work, and nobody has to plan it. My darkness isn't operating my innocent part. It only has to stay hidden from me. Once it has, I *am* sincere, and I say so myself, in the ordinary way: it's my stated position, and I'd repeat it to anyone.

---

This is why calling my darkness an "it" makes sense. "I" (in the usual sense) literally cannot be the one showing you something that is hidden from me.

The above demonstrated the "hot" form of contempt. It can also run cold. I just write the person off: a quiet filing-away, a politeness with nothing behind it, and a small satisfaction at being done with them. It's the same verdict, *this is all you are*, delivered by withdrawal, and they feel it just the same. If the hot version didn't sound like you, check this one. And if neither does—maybe because your enemies are rarely in the room with you—check if the people who *are* in the room might sense something "dark" about your behavior.

Now: for the darkness to be in the open between us, I have to know I'm sending it. For me to hold his reaction against him, I have to not know. Both have to be true of me at once. How?

---

## Limbo

Let's switch gears for a moment.

Have you ever done something you knew was wrong at the time, but couldn't fully admit it to yourself until later? Maybe you took credit for someone else's work, said something cruel to someone who'd done nothing to you, or cheated on a test (or even a partner).

Recall what it was like to finally own up to it. The knowledge that it was wrong: did it feel like new information? Or like something you had always known, but somehow hadn't *let* yourself know?

We like to call such knowledge "unconscious"—as if we had no way of knowing—but that's not entirely accurate. It was *right there* to see if you had looked; fully available to consciousness. You were just *turned away* from it. The knowledge existed in a strange kind of **limbo**, in which you simultaneously knew and yet somehow didn't.

And it wasn't just sitting there idle, either: it affected your behavior. Any time someone came close to exposing it, or you started getting close to it yourself, you found a way to deflect. Did that happen passively?

Looking back, can you see how *some part of you knew exactly what you were doing, even as you did it?* The work of hiding it took place in limbo, too.

---

That's how I did it. I was in limbo: present enough to plant the evidence, yet absent enough not to recognize that I did. That's why the defense "he's not a mind reader" was never going to work: part of me was busy telegraphing the very thing I insisted was hidden from him.

And before you file this under things we do to enemies: we also send it to people we love. The *what's wrong with you?* that needs them to know they're broken. The sigh that lets them know what we really think of them in that moment, dressed up to make us seem like the reasonable ones. The look on their face that tells us it landed, which we'll pretend not to have seen. For one second the same "something" flashes through our eyes, and we make sure they see it, and then we go back to loving them as if nothing had happened.

We don't do this because we're "bad people." Seeing this about ourselves can make us want to hide—but that's the same verdict, *this is all you are*, pointed inward, and it does what the verdict always does: it makes the part of us that did wrong dig in and hide. Nobody ever looked honestly at something in themselves while being told they were worthless for having it. That is what I was doing to my enemy. What I need in order to look is what he needed from me: to be seen as more than what I did.

---

So is the darkness me, or isn't it? 

If I say it is me, then it's hard to avoid feeling shame—especially when I see the full extent of the harm I've caused while in that state. But if I say it's not me, then I'm letting myself off the hook. 

I'm fully responsible for the harm I cause while under its sway.

Of course it's me in [any sense that ???]. But there's also a genuine sense in which it's not me— and I'm not just saying that to shirk responsibility. We'll get to that in a later section.

The same problem occurs when we ask if it's conscious or unconscious. If it's purely conscious, then I must be some kind of evil mastermind. Just reflect on how devious the plan and execution are. But I call it unconscious, then I'm not really owning the full weight of it. Sure, maybe I'll fix it when I have time, but I'm not owning to the fact that it was right there, fully accessible to my conscious mind. I absolutely knew, even if I also didn't.

And now turn it on our enemies. If it's purely conscious, we can't help but feel contempt—and we saw where that leads. If it's purely unconscious, then we'll be *nice* to them. What we should be is *kind*—fiercely kind, in a way that stops the harm without cutting off their way back.

Notice, too, that we never get both views of one instance. My own turning away is always the kind I didn't (and couldn't) see; my enemy's is always the kind he knowingly chose. Same act, opposite interpretations. It is the inherently paradoxical nature of the state that enables us to do this. Both views are incomplete, and our inability to hold the paradox is what lets it continue.

Now think how it feels on the receiving end. For me, it's something like this:

> *If this is the kind of world I live in—one where people can get away with lying to themselves—then why the fuck shouldn't I? I've earned the right.*

See if you've ever felt something like this before.

Now recall how I said that the hidden purpose of my contempt is to provoke more darkness in my enemies, to better hide mine from me. This is what I meant. At some level, I know that their wrongdoing is enabled by essentially the same mechanism as mine: limbo. So what it *means* to provoke their darkness is to push them deeper into limbo; to make that state feel more natural.

Sending people that subtly (but primally) threatening signal—*your wellbeing no longer matters*—is precisely how we do that. If you think back to the example of your own wrongdoing, was it because some part of you felt you *deserved* something? Or perhaps that everyone does it, and only a fool plays it straight? What this message does is show you that you live in a world where that's simply true: where even the people who call themselves good will drop you the moment they can call it justified. In that world, looking out for yourself isn't a lapse. It's realism.

When I treat you with moralizing contempt, I'm effectively claiming that no goodness exists in you. But for my behavior to be coherent, I must know that it does. It's the thing I'm driving you away from. My contempt is squarely directed at—and received by—the very part of you I'm claiming doesn't exist. *That's* why I know it will damage you. And you can see that I know this, and I *need* you to see this, and you can see that, too. That's precisely what makes it "dark."

---

Turning away from him, from myself, from my conscience, from how much the two of us have in common: it's **all a single move**. And it includes turning away from what my behavior sets loose in the world. It's what allows us to harm one another without compunction, and to benefit ourselves at the world's expense.

Because the harm doesn't end with him, and at some level I know that. He *will* pass it along, likely to people who don't deserve it (not that anyone could "deserve" to have their mind darkened). That knowledge sits deep in limbo. Part of me is willing to darken the world a little for my own benefit. And as long as I'm unwilling to face that in myself, I'll keep behaving in ways that reproduce the problem out there, so that I don't have to look.

---

One claim is left, and it's the one people object to first: surely there's a difference in kind between a look and real evil.

Try something. Picture yourself harming someone, on purpose, while looking them in the eyes with what you'd recognize as real kindness. Not tough love; the real thing, the way the second person looked at you. Does the picture hold? For me it comes apart every time. I can picture the harm, and I can picture the kindness, and I can't get them to happen at once. To do the harm, something has to go out of the eyes first.[^far]

[^far]: If the picture held for you, because the harm you pictured was small, run it out. Every account we have of atrocity has the same step in it before the harm: the hood, the number instead of the name, the word for vermin. Nobody skips it. The look you got was that step, at a size where nothing followed.

So I'll call what goes through my eyes a very mild version of the thing we all recognize as evil. Not like it; the same thing, much further back along the same road. I use the word so that I never again get to believe that my mistake is a different kind of thing from what my enemies do.

One last check, and the next chapter leans on it. Think of someone whose contempt got under your skin. Afterward, did you stop caring about him? Or did you have to keep not caring: the case kept open, the reasons rehearsed, his face kept out of view? In me it's always the second. It takes upkeep. If the caring had gone, there'd have been nothing to maintain.

So it wasn't reduced. It was covered. And that leaves a question about what was under the cover the whole time, in him and in you. You couldn't have sent it if you'd seen them clearly. Neither could he. What is it that had to be covered first?
